Start with the problem in your records

Follow the steps for your decision. The examples below use fictional USD amounts; replace them with your own records.

A retainer keeps the team busy but loses contribution

  1. Reconcile the client-month

    Match the fee with work delivered. Show direct contribution separately from any allocated shared overhead.

  2. Cost the added scope

    Record extra hours and incremental expenses without rewriting the original scope. Distinguish a request from an estimate miss.

  3. Check monthly team capacity

    Include committed work and use a stated available-hours denominator. Planned billable hours are not guaranteed sold hours.

The client target or hiring plan does not fit the cash calendar

  1. Calculate whole-client targets

    Use a consistent client-month, contribution, monthly commitments and total delivery hours.

  2. Compare staffing on the same workload

    Include employer burden, minimum contractor terms and onboarding. Do not count committed payroll again as a variable client cost.

  3. Forecast actual receipt dates

    Place invoices, payroll and supplier payments into the thirteen-week schedule and test a delayed receipt.

Gather these records before you calculate

  • Earned service revenue, agreed deliverables and changes; keep reimbursed media spend separate.
  • Delivery, meetings, revisions and account-management hours, including owner delivery time.
  • Loaded labour basis, client-specific contractors and tools, payment fees and committed payroll.
  • Available productive hours, client concentration, invoice clearance dates and payroll due dates.

Keep customer identities and private financial details out of shared copies. If a figure is missing, mark the result provisional rather than treating the missing cost as zero.

Worked check: the required client count exceeds capacity

Fictional USD example; the stated cost boundary applies
Input or resultAmount
Fee / direct labour / other delivery cost$2,000 / $700 / $100
Payment fees / client contribution$60 / $1,140
Overhead + owner pay$2,000 + $3,000 = $5,000
Required whole clients5
Productive hours / hours per client-month104 / 24
Whole-client capacity / contribution at capacity4 / $4,560

Five clients are required because $5,000 ÷ $1,140 is about 4.39. One worker at 30 weekly hours and 80% utilisation provides 104 productive monthly hours using 52 ÷ 12 weeks. At 24 hours per client-month, only four complete clients fit.

At that capacity, contribution is $440 below the monthly target. Test scope, price, costs or productive capacity before promising a fifth retainer. These are fictional averages, not evidence of actual demand or a recommendation to hire.

Connect client scope to productive team hours

A retainer client, a project and a billed hour are different units. Define the promised work and include meetings, revisions, coordination and delivery before estimating contribution. Unpaid extra work still consumes capacity.

Record committed payroll separately from genuinely variable contractor costs, and count owner pay once. Utilization is productive delivery time relative to the stated available hours; it is not a guarantee that those hours will sell.

Revenue concentration, contribution concentration and unpaid invoices describe different risks. The examples are hypothetical planning assumptions, not market prices, staffing advice or predictions of client loss.
