Photography guides for pricing, assignments and capacity

Run the whole-business plan

Calculate photography break-even →Required sessions, revenue, owner pay and productive capacity from your own inputs.

An image price or shooting fee is only part of the assignment. Include preparation, travel, editing, revisions and delivery consistently. The monthly plan also needs business commitments and the work you can deliver.

Start with the problem in your records

Follow the steps for your decision. The examples below use fictional USD amounts; replace them with your own records.

A good session fee leaves little after editing

  1. Track the full assignment

    Record preparation through final delivery. Shooting time alone understates the hours consumed.

  2. Compare complete packages

    Keep image count, revisions, assistants and products aligned with each offered price.

  3. Check the monthly booking target

    Use total worker-hours per assignment and monthly commitments. Spare shooting dates cannot repair an editing bottleneck.

A mini-session day or add-on needs its own cost boundary

  1. Separate event costs from each booking

    Assign shared setup and venue once, then account for per-client editing and delivery.

  2. Cost the extra photographer

    Include travel, promised hours and lead-photographer coordination. A second shooter does not necessarily increase editing capacity.

  3. Review product fulfilment

    Include lab cost, design/revisions, shipping and fees before combining print contribution with session income.

Gather these records before you calculate

  • Agreed deliverables, image count, revisions and delivery dates for one assignment.
  • Enquiry/admin, preparation, travel, shooting, selection, editing and delivery hours.
  • Assistants, rentals, lab products, shipping, gallery charges and actual payment fees.
  • A consistent owner-labour allowance, monthly commitments and shoot/editing capacity.

Keep customer identities and private financial details out of shared copies. If a figure is missing, mark the result provisional rather than treating the missing cost as zero.

Worked check: two extra editing hours change the package result

Fictional USD example; the stated cost boundary applies
Input or resultAmount
Selling fee / non-labour direct costs$650 / $100
Owner delivery allowance / payment fee$30 per hour / 3%
Original full assignment hours8
Original contribution after labour allowance$290.50
Revised hours / revised contribution10 / $230.50
Contribution change / revised contribution per hour−$60 / $23.05

This separate fictional assignment costs 8 × $30 + $100 + $19.50 = $359.50, leaving $290.50. Two additional editing hours add $60 under the same allowance, leaving $230.50. Divide by all ten hours, not the shooting time alone.

The $30 is a stated delivery-cost allowance, not a market rate. Do not count that same owner delivery time again as another job cost. Monthly overhead and any compensation outside the allowance remain separate. Clarify scope before promising a new package.

Count the full assignment, not only camera time

A session, an image and a client are different units. Specify which one your price and cost describe. Include preparation, paid travel, editing, review and delivery before comparing contribution per working hour.

Separate variable delivery labor from committed payroll, and count owner pay once. A second photographer changes both cost and worker-hours; the assignment can still be limited by editing capacity. Print orders need their own lab, shipping, design and transaction costs.

The worked figures are hypothetical planning examples, not local market rates or predicted results. Usage scope is a pricing input here; the guides do not determine legal rights or contract terms for you.
