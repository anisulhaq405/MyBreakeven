How to write your landscaping business plan

The same plan must explain who buys, what one visit includes, how the crew delivers it and how the quiet months are funded. Start with evidence from customers and records, then write the executive summary after the operating and cash schedules agree.

  1. Business and ownership

    Name the owner, service area, business structure and responsibilities. Record local registration, insurance and service-specific requirements to verify.

  2. Customers and demand

    Define the customer, property scope and evidence from interviews, paid trials and comparable local quotes. Separate signed work from targets.

  3. Services and pricing

    Define a complete visit, exclusions, revisit policy and seasonal extras. Record time and materials before setting the price.

  4. Acquisition and retention

    List channels, spend, qualified enquiries, conversion evidence and renewal dates. Do not count each repeat visit as a new customer.

  5. Operations and staffing

    Record travel, setup, worker-hours, equipment availability and weather cancellations. Assign responsibility for route and quality checks.

  6. Seasonal financial plan

    List active months, costs that continue off-season, owner compensation and cash collection dates. Link figures to the workbook.

  7. Funding and cash

    List opening cash, equipment purchases, deposits, debt payments and a minimum reserve. Explain each source of funding.

  8. Risks and response

    Choose specific triggers and responses for weather, route delays, repairs, late invoices and insufficient capacity.

  9. Milestones and evidence

    Assign an owner, date, evidence and pass condition to each launch step. Review actual visits, hours, costs and collections monthly.

Add an executive summary with the customer, offer, funding need and next milestone. If a bank or investor asks for a particular format, supporting statements or a longer projection, adapt the plan to that request. This download does not imply funding approval.

Completed example: Cedar Loop Lawn Care

This is a fictional planning case for residential lawn maintenance within a compact service area. Figures are assumptions, not industry averages, actual customer results or a forecast of demand.

Business and ownership

Cedar Loop is a fictional owner-operated lawn-maintenance business serving one compact residential area. The owner handles quotes, scheduling and field delivery. Local requirements and insurance quotes remain launch conditions.

Customers and demand

The intended customer wants a dependable maintenance visit with a clear scope. The launch test uses homeowner interviews and paid trial visits. No signed contracts or established demand are assumed.

Services and pricing

The example models one comparable maintenance visit at $80, with $30 direct cost including job labor, fuel and consumables. Tree work and installations are excluded and require separate quotes.

Acquisition and retention

The owner will test local referrals and a service-area website. Repeat visits will be scheduled by customer and address. The 100-visit monthly target is a hypothesis, not 100 distinct customers.

Operations and staffing

One worker has 160 monthly hours, with 80% available for modeled delivery. Each visit requires 1.5 worker-hours including allocated travel. Capacity is only 85 whole visits, so the original sales target fails.

Seasonal financial plan

March through October carry 100 planned visits per month; the other four months have no modeled sales. Fixed commitments are $3,000 every month. Owner pay, tax, debt and equipment purchases are excluded until entered.

Funding and cash

The example starts with $10,000 cash. It collects 75% of sales in the service month and 25% next month. Early bills reduce cash before March receipts; no loan or funding approval is assumed.

Risks and response

Before accepting 100 visits, the owner must add feasible paid capacity or lower bookings. Any added worker changes direct cost and cash. Weather cancellations require both volume and hours to be reforecast.

Milestones and evidence

Days 1–30: validate demand and quote equipment and insurance. Days 31–60: log paid pilot visits and route time. Days 61–90: commit only the visit volume supported by hours, demand and the cash reserve.

A positive annual result does not make the delivery plan feasible. The workbook deliberately exposes the 15-visit capacity shortfall. Changing active-month visits to 85 reduces annual contribution to $34,000 and the result to a $2,000 loss before excluded items. Adding staff instead needs a new cost and hours assumption.

The result is sales less the specified direct costs, fixed commitments and entered additional owner pay. It excludes unentered tax, interest, depreciation and other costs. Include compensation for owner delivery work once in the chosen labor boundary. A separate owner draw can require cash without being an operating expense; the workbook's additional owner-pay row is an explicit funding allowance.

Connect the plan to customer receipts and cash payments

January and February each pay $3,000 commitments with no sales receipts, reducing opening cash from $10,000 to $4,000. March receipts are $6,000, direct payments $3,000 and fixed payments $3,000, leaving cash unchanged at $4,000. March ends with $2,000 uncollected sales. The workbook rolls that receipt into April. Monthly cash totals do not identify a shortfall inside a month.

The workbook rolls closing cash and uncollected sales into the next month. Opening cash and receivables apply only in January. The uncollected share is assumed received in the next month; there is no automatic bad-debt or longer-delay calculation. Enter equipment, principal, tax and other cash payments in their separate monthly rows. These cash amounts do not automatically become operating expenses.

Use the dated 13-week cash forecast for payroll dates, supplier deposits, staged funding and within-month cash gaps. A positive month-end balance cannot prove every payment date is covered.

Replace assumptions with evidence before committing money

  • Completed visit and route-time logs
  • Customer confirmations and renewal dates
  • Equipment, insurance and supplier quotes
  • Bank receipts and unpaid invoices

Cost and timing mistakes to avoid

  • Treat eight active months and twelve months of bills separately.
  • Keep crew elapsed time distinct from total worker-hours.
  • Include travel once in the productivity and visit-time boundary.
  • Price installation projects separately when their scope differs from routine maintenance.

A practical 90-day review

In days 1–30, validate the customer problem and gather dated quotes. In days 31–60, run a small paid test and record delivery time, costs and collections. In days 61–90, compare actual results with the same plan and approve expansion only when the demand, hours and cash assumptions are supported. Give each milestone an owner, date and measurable pass condition in your plan.

Frequently asked questions

Are these templates free and editable?

Yes. Download the Word narrative and Excel financial workbook directly without signing up. Replace the fictional example with your records.

Is a business plan the same as a break-even calculation?

No. The plan also describes the customer, offer, sales approach, operations, risks and milestones. Break-even is one check on its financial assumptions.

Can I use the completed example unchanged?

Use it to understand the structure and arithmetic. Its prices, demand, staffing and collection timing are fictional. Replace them before making a business commitment.

Does the workbook show net profit or guarantee enough cash?

No. The result covers only entered costs, and the cash schedule uses monthly timing assumptions. Add omitted costs and use dated cash planning for actual payment decisions.
