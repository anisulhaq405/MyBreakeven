How I used MyBreakeven to bring clarity to Daily Outfitter

Connecting manual costing with a monthly order target for a clothing business.

By Anis Ul Haq · Published October 16, 2026

Founder relationship: I founded MyBreakeven and also run Daily Outfitter, selling ladies’ and gents’ clothing in Pakistan. This is my own product-use account.

I had costs in Excel but needed a clearer target

I was already tracking delivery, packing, material cost, labor and operating overhead manually in Excel. What I could not clearly see was how many orders I needed to cover those costs and reach my desired monthly profit. That uncertainty made it difficult to decide how to pursue my target and where to save or spend.

Between October 1 and 10, 2026, I used MyBreakeven Free to work through my calculations, examine product pricing and understand the order volume needed for my goal. The main benefit I experienced was clarity: a more explicit target and a clearer direction for my team’s sales efforts.

“Before using the calculator, I was confused about how to achieve my target and where I needed to save or spend. It helped me gain clarity in my business.”

My founder interview, translated from Roman Urdu and edited for clarity.

Orders arrive through our website and WhatsApp. Most contain one suit, but some contain several. That means a single-suit cost scenario and an entire store’s order count have different boundaries.

What I did with that clarity

  1. Directed more team effort toward orders. I encouraged the team to increase its sales activity around the target.
  2. Contacted previous customers again. The comparison below does not separate orders generated by this outreach.
  3. Put more focus on Meta. Advertising spend was higher in the October comparison window.

I reported no change in selling prices, discounts or delivery offers between the two comparison periods. Although I examined pricing, I am not claiming a price increase or a measured cost reduction during this period.

Three costing lessons a store owner can reuse

  1. Keep the selling unit consistent. Price, product cost, packaging, acquisition and delivery must describe the same order. Recalculate multi-suit orders and courier weight bands rather than multiplying every cost by the suit count.
  2. Separate a planning allocation from a variable cost. Ribbon packing was included with the suit; shipment packing was in the recalled monthly overhead. If shipment packing changes with order volume, model that behavior explicitly and remove any duplicate allocation. Review full shipping cost per order.
  3. Label targets and allowances clearly. PKR 400 was an acquisition allowance in the recalled scenario. PKR 200,000 was a desired monthly profit beyond owner pay, not achieved profit. Compare plans with actual paid orders, expenses and returns. Inspect return costs separately.

My main takeaway is that cost information became more useful when I connected it with a monthly goal. Clearer planning supported my next actions; it did not guarantee sales.
