How to write your ecommerce business plan

Revenue, inventory and bank receipts follow different schedules. The plan keeps product cost used in shipped orders separate from the cash spent on replacement stock. Start with evidence from customers and records, then write the executive summary after the operating and cash schedules agree.

  1. Business and ownership

    Describe the store, ownership, product category, sales territories and operating responsibilities. Verify location-specific obligations.

  2. Customers and demand

    Define buyer needs, alternatives, demand evidence and a paid validation test. Separate sessions, checkouts and shipped orders.

  3. Product and pricing

    Define the order basket, price, discounts, shipping collected, product cost and returns policy. Match payment fees to the charged amount.

  4. Acquisition and retention

    Set channels, test spend, attributable orders and repeat purchase definitions. Avoid counting platform-attributed sales twice.

  5. Supply and fulfillment

    Record units, purchase quantities, receipt dates, lead times, storage and dispatch capacity. Define how returned stock is handled.

  6. Order contribution and costs

    Show product cost, fulfillment, fees, acquisition and returns once. Keep fixed costs and owner compensation explicit.

  7. Inventory cash and funding

    Schedule stock purchases and settlement dates. Record opening cash and unsettled receipts. Do not deduct purchases again from operating contribution.

  8. Risks and response

    Set triggers for stockouts, supplier delays, ad cost, refund rates and settlement holds. Describe a funded response.

  9. Milestones and evidence

    Assign dates to supplier validation, product tests, launch and reorder decisions. Record actual orders, returns and bank receipts.

Add an executive summary with the customer, offer, funding need and next milestone. If a bank or investor asks for a particular format, supporting statements or a longer projection, adapt the plan to that request. This download does not imply funding approval.

Completed example: Juniper Desk Supply

This is a fictional planning case for online shoppers buying a single comparable desk-accessory product. Figures are assumptions, not industry averages, actual customer results or a forecast of demand.

Business and ownership

Juniper Desk is a fictional direct-to-consumer desk-accessory store. The owner manages suppliers, fulfillment and customer service. Tax, product compliance and local registration requirements must be verified for the actual goods and location.

Customers and demand

The proposed customer wants one comparable desk-accessory item. Interviews, sample feedback and a limited paid launch will test demand. The model assumes 500 shipped orders per month; it does not predict traffic or conversion.

Product and pricing

One shipped order contains one modeled unit and charges $50 before tax, with no separate shipping receipt. Unit product cost is $20. The fee is 3.5% of the full $50 checkout amount.

Acquisition and retention

The sample allocates $8 acquisition cost to each shipped order. Paid and repeat orders are not modeled as different cohorts. A real store should replace this blended amount with a documented channel mix.

Supply and fulfillment

Opening stock is 1,000 units. January receives and pays for 600 units; later months receive 500. Each order uses one unit. Fulfillment and shipping cost $6 per order. Returned units are assumed non-resalable in this example.

Order contribution and costs

Costs per shipped order are $20 product, $6 fulfillment, $1.75 fees, $8 acquisition and $2 returns allowance. Contribution is $12.25; 500 orders leave $6,125 before $5,000 fixed costs.

Inventory cash and funding

The example begins with $20,000 cash and no unsettled sales. January stock payments are $12,000, while shipped-unit product cost is $10,000. Settlement is 90% this month and 10% next month.

Risks and response

The store will review stock coverage and actual refunds weekly. A negative closing inventory balance means the shipment plan is unsupported. Higher returns or acquisition costs require a revised contribution and cash forecast.

Milestones and evidence

Days 1–30: validate samples, buyers and supplier terms. Days 31–60: run a limited paid launch and track order costs. Days 61–90: reorder only after checking sell-through, cash reserve and delivery reliability.

The $2 allowance covers modeled refunds and return handling after the full checkout amount has been recorded. It is not an additional deduction from already refund-net revenue. The cash schedule assumes the same allowance is paid in that month. Change that assumption when refunds arrive later; do not treat an accounting allowance as an observed bank payment.

The result is sales less the specified direct costs, fixed commitments and entered additional owner pay. It excludes unentered tax, interest, depreciation and other costs. Include compensation for owner delivery work once in the chosen labor boundary. A separate owner draw can require cash without being an operating expense; the workbook's additional owner-pay row is an explicit funding allowance.

Connect the plan to customer receipts and cash payments

January settles $22,500 and leaves $2,500 unsettled. Cash payments are $12,000 stock, $3,000 fulfillment, $875 fees, $4,000 acquisition, $1,000 refunds and $5,000 fixed costs: $25,875 total. Cash falls $3,375 to $16,625. Compared with $1,125 operating result, the $4,500 difference is $2,000 added inventory cost plus $2,500 unsettled receipts.

The workbook rolls closing cash and uncollected sales into the next month. Opening cash and receivables apply only in January. The uncollected share is assumed received in the next month; there is no automatic bad-debt or longer-delay calculation. Enter equipment, principal, tax and other cash payments in their separate monthly rows. These cash amounts do not automatically become operating expenses.

Use the dated 13-week cash forecast for payroll dates, supplier deposits, staged funding and within-month cash gaps. A positive month-end balance cannot prove every payment date is covered.

Replace assumptions with evidence before committing money

  • Supplier quotes, purchase orders and receipt dates
  • Shipped-unit and stock records
  • Checkout, refund and processor settlement reports
  • Fulfillment invoices and acquisition-cost records

Cost and timing mistakes to avoid

  • Define shipped orders and the number of units per order consistently.
  • Do not deduct the same return through both net revenue and a reserve.
  • Separate supplier cash payments from product cost consumed.
  • Account for settlement holds and resalable returns when those apply.

A practical 90-day review

In days 1–30, validate the customer problem and gather dated quotes. In days 31–60, run a small paid test and record delivery time, costs and collections. In days 61–90, compare actual results with the same plan and approve expansion only when the demand, hours and cash assumptions are supported. Give each milestone an owner, date and measurable pass condition in your plan.

Frequently asked questions

Are these templates free and editable?

Yes. Download the Word narrative and Excel financial workbook directly without signing up. Replace the fictional example with your records.

Is a business plan the same as a break-even calculation?

No. The plan also describes the customer, offer, sales approach, operations, risks and milestones. Break-even is one check on its financial assumptions.

Can I use the completed example unchanged?

Use it to understand the structure and arithmetic. Its prices, demand, staffing and collection timing are fictional. Replace them before making a business commitment.

Does the workbook show net profit or guarantee enough cash?

No. The result covers only entered costs, and the cash schedule uses monthly timing assumptions. Add omitted costs and use dated cash planning for actual payment decisions.
