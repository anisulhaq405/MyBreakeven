Ecommerce Break-Even: Net Orders, Costs and Capacity

Published September 10, 2026Updated October 7, 2026

Published by MyBreakeven. Editorial standards and corrections. Report a calculation or content issue to support@mybreakeven.com.

Calculate ecommerce break-even orders after product, shipping, returns and ad costs. Compare discounts, acquisition, owner pay and packing capacity.

EcommerceCOGSCustomer AcquisitionFulfillment

Ecommerce break-even starts with what one completed order leaves after product cost, fulfillment, shipping, acquisition, returns-related costs and fees. Divide the monthly funding requirement by that contribution and round up to whole orders. In this fictional store, a $60 order contributes $18.20; covering $1,000 overhead, $2,000 owner pay and $500 extra profit needs 193 orders. The next check is whether fulfillment hours and customer demand support them. All figures are USD assumptions, not ecommerce benchmarks or forecasts.

Quick answer

Use one consistent order unit and a net selling price after discounts. Subtract product and order-related costs, including acquisition and payment fees, to find contribution. Divide overhead plus owner pay plus target profit by that amount, rounding up. Then check fulfillment workload, traffic assumptions and inventory cash separately from the operating result.

Cost the same order on both sides

Assume one order sells for $60 before any pass-through tax. The product costs $22 including the inventory cost allocated to goods sold. Incremental paid fulfillment labor costs $3. Packaging and shipping paid by the store cost $7, unrecovered return-handling costs average an assumed $2, and acquisition costs $6 per completed order.

Non-percentage variable cost is $22 + $3 + $7 + $2 + $6 = $40. Payment processing is modeled at 3% of the $60 service revenue, or $1.80. Contribution is $60 − $40 − $1.80 = $18.20 per order, a 30.33% contribution ratio.

The $2 return line in this example covers separate unrecovered handling expense. The model assumes the $60 net selling value and the $22 product cost are already reconciled consistently to the order basis. It does not deduct the same refunded revenue or recoverable stock twice. If your order history includes refunds, build a reconciled average first rather than adding a refund percentage to an unreconciled gross ticket.

Monthly overhead is $1,000, owner pay is $2,000 and the additional profit goal is $500. Total contribution required is $3,500. At $18.20 per order, 192 orders contribute $3,494.40 and miss the goal by $5.60. One hundred ninety-three orders contribute $3,512.60.

Sales at that volume are 193 × $60 = $11,580. After $1,000 overhead and $2,000 owner pay, the result is $512.60 before taxes and unmodeled expenses. Its owner-paid planning margin is $512.60 ÷ $11,580 = 4.43%, much lower than the order contribution ratio of 30.33%.

If you only want to cover operating overhead, the model needs 55 orders because 55 × $18.20 = $1,001. Including owner pay needs 165 orders, and adding the $500 target raises it to 193. Name the threshold you mean by break-even rather than switching between those three questions.

Keep product, order and customer units separate

This example is one average completed order, not one product sold or one new customer. A basket can contain multiple products, and a customer can place several orders. Put revenue and product cost on the same order basis before dividing the monthly requirement.

An acquisition figure per new customer needs a stated conversion into the chosen order period. Do not spread it over an unlimited assumed customer lifetime. If you use $6 per completed order, explain which orders and marketing spend produced that figure. Keep a committed monthly marketing budget outside the same acquisition line when it is already included in overhead.

Shipping charged to the customer should be handled consistently. Include the shipping revenue you retain and the actual shipping expense, or use a reconciled net shipping subsidy. Do not use both treatments on the same order. The shipping-cost guide explores that boundary.

The return cost-per-order guide helps separate refunded revenue, recovered stock and unrecovered handling. Those records are needed before an average order can support a meaningful monthly estimate.

Four variables that move the order requirement

Net basket value. Discounts change what the customer actually pays. Keep product cost unchanged unless the basket itself changes. A percentage payment fee should be recalculated on the actual fee base rather than left at the original ticket amount.

Acquisition. An order that costs more to obtain leaves less for the month. Track the chosen order and customer boundary. A return customer may have different acquisition economics, but those savings should be measured rather than assumed for every order.

Fulfillment and shipping. Packing time, carrier expense and parcel size can vary with mix. An apparently better basket may require a more expensive parcel or additional labor. Count that change before treating higher sales as higher contribution.

Monthly commitments and capacity. Rent, storage, software and committed payroll change the required contribution. A new fulfillment hire or larger storage contract can add a step in fixed cost. Test the new requirement, not only the previous per-order margin.

Keep owner labor consistent. This example uses paid fulfillment labor distinct from the $2,000 owner income goal. If the owner performs that same packing work, choose a single compensation treatment and retain all packing hours in capacity.

Three worked store scenarios

A $6 discount on the same basket

Price falls from $60 to $54 while the $40 non-percentage cost stays unchanged. The fee is $1.62. Contribution becomes $54 − $40 − $1.62 = $12.38.

Funding $3,500 needs 283 orders, because 282 contribute $3,491.16 and 283 contribute $3,503.54. Required order volume rises by 90 compared with 193 at the original price. Whether the discount can create those extra orders needs demand evidence; the arithmetic alone cannot establish that outcome.

Acquisition increases from $6 to $10

Non-percentage cost rises to $44. At the original $60 price, contribution becomes $60 × 0.97 − $44 = $14.20. Funding $3,500 requires 247 orders; 246 contribute $3,493.20 and 247 contribute $3,507.40.

The extra $4 acquisition cost raises the order target by 54. The break-even ROAS guide isolates advertising economics for a defined basket. Use the complete store model as well so a campaign threshold is not mistaken for owner-paid monthly profit.

Packing time rises from 0.25 to 0.4 hours

Suppose paid packing labor is $12 per worker-hour. The baseline 0.25 hours cost $3. At 0.4 hours, paid labor costs $4.80, increasing non-percentage cost to $41.80. Contribution becomes $60 × 0.97 − $41.80 = $16.40.

The funding target needs 214 orders, since 213 contribute $3,493.20 and 214 contribute $3,509.60. Capacity also changes. One fulfillment worker with 20 weekly hours at 75% utilization has 65 usable monthly worker-hours. At 0.25 hours, that supports 260 whole orders; at 0.4 hours, it supports only 162.

Thus the 214-order financial requirement cannot fit the revised 162-order workload ceiling. At 162 orders, contribution is $2,656.80 and the result after $3,000 overhead and owner pay is a $343.20 shortfall. Faster work, additional hours, outsourcing or a different basket changes the model and its costs.

Translate orders into demand without calling it a forecast

If the original 193-order target is spread across 26 fulfillment days, it averages 7.42 completed orders per day. That average does not describe peak days or dispatch cutoffs. Review actual daily volume before relying on it as a staffing plan.

With a hypothetical 2% purchase conversion from qualified site visits, 193 orders require 9,650 visits. This is a scenario using a chosen conversion assumption, not a benchmark or a prediction of traffic. The store still needs evidence for traffic source, conversion and repeated orders.

Inventory cash is another separate check. The $22 product cost represents goods sold. Buying 300 units at $22 uses $6,600 cash before those units necessarily sell. Do not deduct the purchase again from the contribution of those same goods. Keep supplier payment dates, stock recovery and reorder timing in a cash schedule.

How to run your own numbers

Use the ecommerce break-even calculator with $60 price, $22 product cost, $3 labor, $9 other variable costs and $6 acquisition, plus a 3% fee. Enter $1,000 overhead, $2,000 owner pay and $500 target profit. For capacity use one worker, 20 weekly hours, 75% utilization and 0.25 worker-hours per order. It requires 193 orders and permits 260. Other currencies are supported. Use your own conversion assumption; the model does not forecast traffic, inventory balances or parcel-level queues.

Common mistakes

  • Using a product price with costs from a multi-product order.
  • Calling contribution margin final profit before overhead and owner pay.
  • Deducting refunded sales twice through revenue and a returns allowance.
  • Spreading acquisition across an unsupported lifetime order count.
  • Treating inventory purchases and the same goods sold as two operating costs.

FAQs

Should I use gross sales or net sales?

Use a consistently reconciled selling value after customer discounts and refunded sales. Keep pass-through tax outside revenue available to fund the business. Match the product and return-cost treatment to the same basis.

Does break-even include owner pay?

Only if you include it in the requirement. This example shows overhead-only, owner-paid and additional-profit thresholds separately. State which target the order count supports.

Can I use average order value?

Yes, when the matching average costs describe the same order mix and period. Recalculate when baskets, channels or returns change materially. An average can hide a negative-contribution product or channel.

What if contribution is zero or negative?

Additional orders cannot fund positive monthly commitments under that unchanged model. Revise price, product cost, shipping, acquisition or scope before increasing volume. Check the resulting demand and workload again.

Is fulfillment capacity a sales forecast?

No, it is a ceiling under the entered hours and time assumptions. Demand, stock availability and peak dispatch timing need separate evidence. A monthly ceiling can still hide busy-day bottlenecks.

Can a profitable store run short of cash?

Yes, inventory purchases and other payments can precede customer receipts. Contribution does not show those dates. Keep a cash schedule alongside the order model.

Takeaways

  • Match net revenue and costs to one consistent order unit.
  • Label overhead, owner pay and additional-profit thresholds.
  • Recalculate fees, contribution and hours when the basket changes.
  • Keep demand evidence and inventory cash beside the operating model.
