Cost-Benefit Analysis: Incremental Cash, Discounting and Decision Evidence

Cost-benefit analysis compares the additional consequences of a decision with a defined alternative. Identify who bears each cost, when each benefit occurs and what cannot credibly be monetized before interpreting a total or ratio.

All amounts and operating results below are original fictional teaching examples in USD. They are not customer results, industry averages or forecasts. Use your own dated records and applicable accounting policies.

Define the decision and the alternative first

Write the question in operational terms: should a workshop replace its manual scheduling process with a specified system for the next three years? Compare that option with continuing the present process, including the costs and performance reasonably expected without the change. A baseline is not automatically zero cost or zero improvement. Include a lower-cost repair or smaller pilot if it could meet the same objective.

This guide uses an original fictional USD business case, not a vendor quote, observed customer result or investment recommendation. Its perspective is the workshop’s incremental operating cash before financing and income tax. A social benefit-cost analysis can include effects on other parties and therefore have a different boundary. OMB Circular A-94 is a federal-program methodological reference; it does not set this private business’s hurdle rate or approval policy.

Build a cost-and-benefit register without double counting

Include future changes caused by choosing the option: setup, integration, training, recurring fees, transition disruption, decommissioning and any supported residual proceeds. Identify a resource’s next-best use when an opportunity cost matters. A nonrefundable study fee already paid before the decision is normally outside the future incremental comparison; an unpaid cancellation obligation or avoidable next stage can still be relevant. Record the reason for each inclusion or exclusion.

Do not value the same released labor twice as both avoided payroll and additional delivery contribution. If payroll continues, freed hours are capacity, not automatically cash savings. Include incremental sales only through their additional cash contribution after the associated costs, rather than adding gross revenue and ignoring fulfillment. Use a separate nonfinancial record for effects that lack a defensible monetary estimate. A precise spreadsheet cannot turn an unsupported claim into evidence.

Reconcile a dated three-year teaching case

Assume $18,000 implementation plus $2,000 transition cash at time zero. Each year-end brings $12,000 of avoided external-service payments relative to the baseline and $4,000 incremental operating payments. Assume identical nominal dollar amounts each year, a three-year horizon, no taxes, financing, terminal proceeds or working-capital change. These exclusions keep the arithmetic inspectable; a real proposal must assess them rather than presume they are immaterial.

That sum treats a dollar received in year three like a dollar spent today, so it is a screening total rather than the discounted decision result. Preserve the gross benefit and cost columns even when a net cash column is convenient.

Discount benefits and costs on the same basis

For a stated rate r and end-of-year amount in year t, present value equals amount / (1 + r)t. Use the same timing convention, currency and price basis across the comparison. Nominal cash amounts require a compatible nominal rate; constant-price amounts require a compatible real rate. Do not add an inflation forecast to cash flows and then discount them with an unrelated real assumption.

At an illustrative 8% nominal rate, the three-year discount-factor sum is 2.577096987. Present-value benefits are $30,925.16. Present-value costs are $20,000 + $4,000 × 2.577096987 = $30,308.39. NPV is $616.78, and the explicitly defined benefit-cost ratio is PV benefits / PV costs = 1.02035. Calculate from unrounded values and round displayed amounts only. An OMB Appendix C rate is not automatically appropriate for this business.

Keep ROI, payback and NPV as separate questions

A simple undiscounted return defined here as net benefit / total incremental cost is $4,000 / $32,000 = 12.5% over the full three-year horizon. It is not an annual return or an internal rate of return. Other ROI conventions can use a different denominator, so show yours rather than comparing unlabeled percentages. The ROAS-versus-ROI guide separately explains campaign return boundaries.

After two annual net receipts, $4,000 of the initial $20,000 remains unrecovered. Assuming benefits accrue uniformly within year three gives an interpolated simple payback of 2.5 years. Under the actual year-end-only timing in the table, recovery occurs at the third payment. Discounted payback must use discounted cash and still does not capture benefits beyond its cutoff. Use profitability index for that separate capital-ranking question, with its own numerator definition.

Test the fragile assumptions and nonfinancial conditions

At the same $8,000 annual net cash, illustrative rates of 4%, 8% and 12% give NPVs of $2,200.73, $616.78 and −$785.35. If annual gross benefit is only $10,000 while operating cost remains $4,000, NPV at 8% becomes −$4,537.42. The gross annual benefit required for zero NPV under these assumptions is about $11,760.67. These are original scenario calculations, not probability estimates.

The GAO cost-estimating guide recommends documenting assumptions and sensitivity rather than presenting one estimate without its drivers. Link each scenario to a question that can be investigated: supplier price, implementation delay, usable capacity or actual baseline spending. Keep service reliability, data-access requirements, accessibility and exit feasibility as explicit criteria. A positive NPV does not override a failed mandatory requirement, and unsupported scores should not be disguised as dollar benefits.

Approve a testable decision and review actual results

Record the chosen alternative, dated input evidence, cash owner, accepted uncertainty and stop conditions. Before a full commitment, a bounded pilot can test whether the assumed external-service payment really disappears and whether the replacement meets its delivery requirement. State what result would change the decision instead of selecting a threshold after the pilot outcome is known.

After launch, compare actual incremental payments and achieved benefits with the original baseline. Preserve the prior version when revising the forecast. Feed payment dates into the cash-flow forecast. Benefits can arrive late even when the three-year total looks acceptable. The worksheet is a manual evidence record, not an automatic financial appraisal.

Frequently asked questions

What is cost-benefit analysis?

It compares the additional costs and benefits of a defined option with a stated alternative, including timing, uncertainty and relevant nonfinancial effects.

Is the benefit-cost ratio the same as ROI?

No. This case defines BCR as present-value benefits divided by present-value costs, while its simple ROI uses undiscounted net benefit divided by total incremental cost.

Is an 8% discount rate recommended for my business?

No. It is a fictional teaching assumption. Select and support a rate consistent with the decision, price basis and risk treatment.

Should time saved be counted as cash saved?

Only when a supported cash consequence follows. If payroll continues, released time can be capacity rather than an avoided payment.

Does payback include every benefit?

No. Payback asks when the initial amount is recovered under its timing convention and can ignore later benefits and other decision effects.

Can a positive NPV override a legal or operational requirement?

No. Treat mandatory requirements and unmonetized consequences explicitly rather than allowing a financial subtotal to erase them.
