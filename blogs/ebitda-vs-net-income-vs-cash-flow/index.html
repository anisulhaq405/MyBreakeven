EBITDA, net income and cash flow answer different questions

EBITDA measures earnings before interest, income taxes, depreciation and amortization; net income retains those included expenses; operating cash flow reconciles profit with operating cash movements. They are different measures, not competing labels for money in the bank. In the original example below, the same year produces $130,000 EBITDA, $80,000 net income and $70,000 operating cash flow.

Use the EBITDA calculator to rebuild the earnings bridge. Use this guide to understand why the cash answer differs. All amounts below are fictional USD for a simplified nonfinancial business under a stated US-style classification. They are not margins or performance benchmarks.

The operating expenses contain $20,000 D&A and $270,000 other operating expenses. There are no additional non-operating gains, foreign-exchange items or discontinued operations in this simplified statement. Thus operating profit and EBIT agree here; that equality should not be assumed for every company.

The check is $1,000,000 − $600,000 − $290,000 − $10,000 − $20,000 = $80,000 net income. Keep inventory and COGS on a consistent accrual basis.

Reconcile net income to EBITDA

$80,000 + $10,000 interest + $20,000 income taxes + $15,000 depreciation + $5,000 amortization = $130,000 EBITDA. Dividing by $1,000,000 net revenue gives 13% EBITDA margin. Net margin is 8%; operating margin is 11%. These percentages use the same revenue denominator but different profit numerators.

Do not add back payroll, rent or materials merely because they are operating costs. Do not add back debt principal: principal was not an income-statement expense in the first place. If depreciation is already excluded from a starting subtotal, adding it again inflates earnings.

Cross-check from EBITDA: $130,000 − $10,000 operating interest paid − $20,000 operating income tax paid − $25,000 receivables increase − $10,000 inventory increase + $5,000 payables increase = $70,000 operating cash flow. This second route agrees because the example has no interest/tax payable changes or other adjustments. Do not subtract D&A again: EBITDA already excludes it.

Equipment and loans sit outside this operating subtotal

Suppose the business pays $40,000 for equipment and repays $15,000 loan principal, with no other investing or financing movements. Operating cash of $70,000 less those payments leaves a $15,000 total cash increase. The $40,000 purchase is investing; the $15,000 principal repayment is financing under the example policy. Neither is automatically a new full current-year operating expense.

Under a stated operating-cash-flow-minus-capital-expenditure convention, the intermediate amount is $30,000 before the principal repayment. A report calling a number “free cash flow” must disclose its definition. EBITDA is not that $30,000 amount. See the complete cash flow statement for reconciliation to the opening bank balance.

Choose the measure that matches the decision

Use the EBITDA bridge to compare defined earnings across matched periods, then review its exclusions. Use net income for the period’s bottom-line accounting result. Use operating and total cash-flow schedules for payment timing, asset purchases and funding. None alone establishes what owners can safely withdraw.

Compare the same company perimeter, reporting duration and revenue basis. A growth business can have improving EBITDA and deteriorating collections simultaneously. Keep that diagnosis separate from the sales volume needed to break even. The operating margin calculator answers the operating-profit percentage question.

Common mistakes that change the answer

Adding D&A twice, using cash tax paid as a substitute for accrued tax expense, treating all current accounts as operating working capital and subtracting equipment purchases twice can each create a false bridge. Reconcile missing data rather than entering a convenient zero.

If an Adjusted EBITDA report removes unusual costs or gains, read the add-back evidence guide. A claimed one-off does not disappear from bank cash simply because management adjusts an earnings measure.

Sources and reporting scope

SEC guidance on EBIT and EBITDA establishes the net-income starting point and distinct naming of adjusted measures. OpenStax explains the indirect cash-flow reconciliation. Our Harbor Supply statement and arithmetic are original.

The illustrative US-style interest/tax classification is explicit, not a universal IFRS presentation template. Apply the adopted reporting framework and professional advice where required. For actual dated collections and payments, use the 13-week cash forecast.

Frequently asked questions

Is EBITDA the same as operating cash flow?

No. EBITDA excludes selected expenses but does not automatically account for collections, inventory, payables or all operating cash adjustments.

Can EBITDA be positive while cash falls?

Yes. Uncollected sales, inventory investment, equipment payments and debt principal can absorb cash even when the earnings subtotal is positive.

Why is depreciation added back in the cash bridge?

Included depreciation reduced net income without being a cash payment in that period. The actual asset purchase is classified separately.

Does operating income always equal EBIT?

No. They agree in the fictional example because no separate non-operating items exist. Reconcile the actual statement before using either starting subtotal.
