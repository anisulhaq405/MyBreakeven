What Does It Cost to Start a Landscaping Business?

Published September 18, 2026Updated October 10, 2026

Published by MyBreakeven. Editorial standards and corrections. Report a calculation or content issue to support@mybreakeven.com.

Build a landscaping startup budget from opening purchases, monthly costs and ramp cash needs. Compare slower sales, payment timing and worker capacity.

LandscapingStartup CostsBreak-Even Planning

The maintenance business in this example needs $23,401 to open and cover its initial cash shortfall. That includes $19,000 of opening outlays, a $1,900 contingency and $2,501 to fund the assumed sales ramp.

Your purchase list will change with the equipment you own and the services you offer. The more useful number is what remains in the bank after buying the tools, paying deposits and meeting bills before enough customer cash arrives. The USD amounts here are illustrative; replace each with a quote or your own payment schedule.

Quick answer

List each opening purchase, deposit and setup cost, then separate recurring monthly bills and job-level expenses. Estimate paid job contribution and the sales ramp. Calculate the deepest cumulative cash deficit during that ramp, add it to opening outlays and an explicit contingency, and check that the required jobs fit crew capacity. Replace every placeholder with scoped quotes and records.

Choose the launch service before buying the equipment

Write the first service list on one page. If you are starting with mowing and routine maintenance, separate the tools needed for those jobs from equipment you might want for installation work later. Buy for the booked service plan rather than letting a discounted equipment bundle expand the scope.

Next, list what you already have and what is available to use reliably. An owned vehicle avoids a purchase payment today, but still needs fuel, maintenance and eventual replacement. A borrowed trailer may save opening cash while limiting which days you can work. Put that restriction into the schedule.

For each new item, keep the quoted price, delivery cost, payment date and included accessories together. A mower price without the transport or preparation needed to put it to work is an incomplete cash estimate. Replace allowances with quoted amounts as you obtain them.

The SBA startup-cost guide separates one-time and monthly expenses. Use those two groups for the first budget, then add a cash timeline. The landscaping figures below are an example of that process, not prices from the SBA.

Avoid spending the reserve because the tools came in under budget. Check the ramp and payment dates first. A smaller opening purchase bill does not remove payroll, storage or the owner’s draw during a slow first month.

Build the opening cash list

The example business starts a maintenance service using an already owned towing vehicle. It needs the following additional cash before the first paid job:

Opening item Fictional cash amount
Mower $6,000
Trailer $3,000
Handheld tools $2,000
Safety equipment and work supplies $500
Setup, registration and local approvals allowance $500
Initial insurance and storage deposits $1,000
Website, branding and launch materials $1,000
Opening consumables $1,000
Other scoped transport and equipment preparation $4,000
Total opening cash outlay $19,000

The approval allowance is a placeholder, not a statement of local requirements or fees. Obtain the applicable information for your location and service scope. Deposits are shown because they use cash even when they are refundable or not an immediate accounting expense.

Add a chosen 10% contingency on this opening list: $19,000 × 10% = $1,900. Ten percent is an illustrative choice, not a universal reserve rule. The contingency should address specified uncertainties and should not be confused with the operating cash required during the sales ramp.

Keep monthly and job costs separate

The example monthly fixed costs are $1,500, including the recurring items in the chosen plan. Owner pay is a separate $2,500 goal. The operating cash target before growth profit is therefore $4,000 per month. Any loan payment, tax cash reserve or additional commitment would need its own explicit cash-plan line; none is included in this simplified example.

Each completed maintenance job sells for $180 and uses $10 materials, $50 paid direct labor, $20 other variable costs and $10 acquisition cost. A 3% payment fee adds $5.40. Contribution is $180 − $90 − $5.40 = $84.60 per completed job.

This contribution is available for the monthly fixed-cost and owner-pay goal. It does not repay the $19,000 opening outlay automatically. If you want to recover that investment over a chosen period, model the additional monthly recovery goal separately and make clear that it is a planning target rather than an accounting expense.

Opening consumables also need consistent treatment. In a cash plan, show when supplies are purchased. In the job model, show the cost of supplies used. Do not charge the entire opening stock again to the first month while also charging each job its consumed share in the same cash forecast.

Calculate the sales-ramp cash requirement

For this example, assume customer cash is collected in the month the job is completed and variable costs are paid in that same month. The first three months complete 25, 40 and 55 jobs respectively.

Month Completed jobs Contribution Fixed costs plus owner pay Monthly cash change Cumulative cash change
1 25 $2,115 $4,000 −$1,885 −$1,885
2 40 $3,384 $4,000 −$616 −$2,501
3 55 $4,653 $4,000 $653 −$1,848

The deepest cumulative deficit is $2,501, at the end of month two. That is the operating shortfall for this base ramp under same-month payment assumptions. Do not add the positive third month's cash change as another loss.

A slower ramp of 20, 35 and 45 jobs gives contributions of $1,692, $2,961 and $3,807. Monthly changes are −$2,308, −$1,039 and −$193, creating a deepest cumulative deficit of $3,540. The launch budget would then be $19,000 + $1,900 + $3,540 = $24,440.

For a third controlled case, keep the 25, 40 and 55 job ramp but use $75 contribution because job costs are higher. Monthly changes become −$2,125, −$1,000 and $125. The deepest cumulative deficit is $3,125, so the opening-plus-reserve total becomes $24,025. These scenarios show why the same equipment list can require different launch cash.

Check payment timing before trusting the reserve

The base ramp assumes prompt collection. If month-one customers pay in month two, the cash balance can fall further even though the completed-job contribution has not changed. Build a cash timeline with actual expected receipts and payment dates when work is invoiced after completion or suppliers require advance payment.

The base launch total is therefore $19,000 opening cash + $1,900 contingency + $2,501 operating shortfall = $23,401. That amount covers the stated base scenario, not every possible delayed receipt or interruption. Extend the monthly timeline and test actual payment delays before using it as a funding target. Any extra reserve should be shown as a chosen buffer with its purpose stated, rather than hidden inside the equipment estimate.

Compare the required jobs with capacity

At $84.60 contribution and a $4,000 monthly goal, required volume is $4,000 ÷ $84.60 = 47.28, rounded up to 48 jobs. One worker at 35 weekly hours and 70% utilization provides 106.17 delivery hours per month. At 1.5 worker-hours per completed job, capacity is 70 whole jobs.

The base monthly goal fits this hour model, and the month-three 55-job ramp fits too. A fit does not establish demand. Travel, weather interruptions, access and scheduling must already be represented in the chosen job duration and utilization. If the service takes two hours instead, capacity falls to 53 whole jobs and the 55-job ramp becomes infeasible under the same hours.

If you add $500 monthly target profit, the goal becomes $4,500 and requires 54 jobs. That result matters before buying equipment on the assumption that the business can recover its opening spending quickly. Check both the operating target and the additional goal you actually intend to pursue.

The result should show $84.60 contribution, 48 required jobs and 70 whole-job capacity. This tests ongoing operations. Keep the opening purchase list and cumulative cash timeline alongside it; the break-even result does not calculate deposits, equipment purchases or collection delays for you.

Budget gaps to settle before launch

  • Using equipment cost as the entire budget. Opening deposits and operating shortfalls also use cash.
  • Treating existing assets as costless forever. Record their operating and replacement needs.
  • Adding every monthly loss without a cumulative timeline. Use the deepest cumulative deficit and actual payment dates.
  • Double-counting opening stock. Separate purchase cash from job-level consumption.
  • Assuming profitable jobs guarantee a workable ramp. Check demand, collection timing and delivery hours together.

Landscaping launch cash includes equipment and the wait for payment

A fictional launch needs $9,000 equipment, $1,000 initial materials, $2,000 deposits and $3,000 working cash, totaling $15,000. This is an illustrative category exercise, not a typical industry startup budget. Truck ownership, hired equipment and supplier terms can materially change the actual amount.

If a job leaves $250 contribution and monthly commitments total $5,000, operating break-even is 20 jobs. Customers paying after job completion can still create a funding gap before fuel, labor and materials are due. Put those payment dates in the cash schedule rather than assuming the twenty-job target pays every bill on time.

Separate reusable equipment from materials consumed on each job. A rental cost for one assignment can be direct; a committed monthly lease may be overhead under the chosen boundary. Test crew worker-hours and seasonality alongside volume. The initial funding and operating target answer different questions and should not be merged into one unexplained cost-per-job number.

FAQs

Can I start with equipment I already own?

Yes, put any new cash needed to prepare it in the opening list. Then include its operating costs and a separate replacement plan rather than treating it as free to use indefinitely.

Do deposits belong in the budget if I can get them back?

Include them in opening cash because you have to pay them before using that money elsewhere. Keep refundable deposits separate from immediate operating expenses in the records.

Why is the reserve $2,501 when month three makes money?

The cumulative operating balance reaches its lowest point at the end of month two. The $653 increase in month three replenishes part of that deficit; it does not remove the need to fund the earlier low point.

What if customers pay a month after the job?

Move the expected receipt to its payment month while keeping payroll and supplier payments on their due dates. The cash low point can deepen even though each completed job still has the same contribution.

How do I allow for recovering the opening investment?

Choose a recovery period and show the resulting amount as an additional monthly planning goal. Keep it separate from the recurring bills so the operating break-even calculation remains understandable.

Which purchase should I delay if the budget is tight?

Review whether the item is needed to deliver the launch services and confirmed workload. Compare deferring, hiring or using existing equipment with the full cost and scheduling limits of each option.

Does a profitable job eliminate the need for working cash?

No. Supplier and labor payments can precede collection from the customer. Model dates and payment terms separately from the job's contribution.

Takeaways

  • Price the equipment needed for the launch services you actually plan to sell.
  • Put deposits and payment dates in the opening cash list.
  • Fund the lowest cumulative cash balance during the ramp.
  • Check that the completed-job target fits worker-hours before adding a recovery goal.
