Cost Performance Index: Earned Value, Actual Cost and Forecast Limits

Quick answer

Cost Performance Index, or CPI, equals earned value divided by actual cost for the same project scope and status date. It measures the budgeted value of completed work per dollar of actual cost; it does not measure sales revenue or profit margin.

Read the source-backed application and limits. Worked business amounts are fictional teaching examples.

Every amount in the worked case is an original fictional USD planning assumption. It is not an industry benchmark, a customer result or a prediction.

Define planned value, earned value and actual cost

Planned value is the approved budget for work scheduled by the status date. Earned value is the approved budget for work actually completed by that date, using a defensible completion method. Actual cost records what that completed work has cost on a consistent accounting basis. Match scope, cut-off and currency before dividing.

CPI = EV ÷ AC. Below 1 means the measured work has consumed more actual cost than its budgeted value; above 1 means the opposite. A zero actual-cost denominator needs investigation, not a fabricated finite index. Missing supplier accruals can make CPI look artificially favorable.

The project has earned $0.80 of budgeted work for each actual dollar. CPI does not mean a 20% profit loss, and the schedule index does not directly convert into days late. In this example, cost performance and scheduled completion both require investigation, but they describe different dimensions.

Choose an estimate-at-completion assumption explicitly

If all remaining work can be delivered at its original remaining budget, EAC is AC + (BAC − EV): $62,500 + $50,000 = $112,500. If the same cost efficiency is assumed to continue across the total project, EAC is BAC ÷ CPI: $100,000 ÷ 0.80 = $125,000. The $12,500 gap is an assumption difference, not an arithmetic error.

A bottom-up remaining estimate of $58,000 would instead produce EAC of $120,500. Document supplier commitments, remaining quantities, rework and resource rates behind that estimate. Do not select the most reassuring formula without checking what is causing the variance. EAC is a forecast, not a confirmed final invoice.

Aggregate dollars before calculating portfolio CPI

Suppose work package A has EV $30,000 and AC $25,000, so CPI is 1.2. Package B has EV $20,000 and AC $37,500, so CPI is approximately 0.5333. The simple average of the two indices is 0.8667, but project CPI is ($30,000 + $20,000) ÷ ($25,000 + $37,500) = 0.80. Weighting follows the underlying totals.

Verify completion evidence rather than treating dollars spent as percentage complete. An expensive early purchase can increase actual cost while little eligible work is earned; a milestone-based rule can delay earned value. State those rules and apply them consistently.

Turn the ratio into a corrective project decision

  1. Confirm baseline, completion evidence and status-date cut-off.
  2. Investigate quantity, rate, productivity, scope and rework causes.
  3. Re-estimate the remaining work from supported inputs.
  4. Choose and fund the response; log approved scope changes.
  5. Review the next status date without erasing the original baseline history.

Use job-order costing for recorded job cost and budget variance analysis for driver-specific performance. CPI is a project earned-value measure, separate from a business operating margin.

Test the remaining efficiency needed to meet a chosen budget

CPI describes cost performance achieved to date. The separate to-complete performance index, or TCPI, asks what efficiency the remaining work would need to meet a selected funding target. PMI’s TCPI paper explains the remaining-work numerator and the different denominators for the original budget and an approved revised estimate.

Using the existing fictional status, $50,000 of budgeted work remains: $100,000 BAC minus $50,000 EV. Only $37,500 remains within the original budget: $100,000 minus $62,500 AC. Thus TCPI to the original BAC is $50,000 / $37,500 = 1.3333. That would require substantially better remaining cost efficiency than the measured 0.80 CPI; it is a requirement, not proof the team can achieve it.

If the selected revised target is the $125,000 EAC, its remaining funding is $62,500 and TCPI becomes $50,000 / $62,500 = 0.80. That matches the continued-efficiency assumption used to derive this EAC. A $120,500 bottom-up EAC gives $58,000 remaining funding and TCPI approximately 0.8621. Each denominator must identify the target being tested; changing the forecast does not erase the original approved baseline.

When the selected target has no remaining funding, this ratio is undefined or infeasible for positive remaining work. Investigate scope, accrual cutoff, remaining commitments and corrective resources before using the result. Do not interpret 1.3333 as a required percentage margin, a promised productivity improvement or a schedule completion date.

Frequently asked questions

Can CPI be used as a profit margin?

No. Earned value is budgeted completed work, not customer revenue. A CPI of 0.8 is not an 80% margin.

Does EAC equal BAC divided by CPI in every situation?

No. That formula assumes the measured cost efficiency continues. A remaining-at-budget or bottom-up estimate uses different assumptions.

Can I average work-package CPI values?

Calculate total earned value divided by total actual cost. A simple average of ratios can misstate the project because packages have different cost weights.

What happens when actual cost is zero?

The division is undefined. Investigate missing costs, timing or completion rules rather than presenting a finite CPI value.

Is TCPI the same as CPI?

No. CPI measures achieved earned value per actual cost. TCPI divides budgeted remaining work by funds remaining under a specified target.

What does 1.3333 TCPI mean in this case?

The original budget would require about $1.3333 of budgeted remaining work for each remaining actual dollar. It does not show that this required performance is attainable.
