A shorter term can raise payments while reducing interest and remaining debt. A longer term can lower payments while extending borrowing. Compare the current loan’s remaining months, each proposed term and cash plus debt at one common holding date.

Compare payment burden and debt remaining at the same sale date

For the same hypothetical $250,000 payoff and 5.5% new fixed note rate, the new loan term changes both monthly obligations and principal still owed after five years. Each version includes $6,000 upfront charges and is compared with keeping a 6.5% current mortgage with 300 months remaining.

Refinance to a shorter or longer term: make three separate checks

Check 1 — monthly affordability: can required principal and interest plus other housing obligations fit recurring cash flow? Check 2 — loan payoff: how many months remain on the current loan, and when would each new loan actually finish? Check 3 — planned holding period: what will you have paid and still owe on the same likely sale or next-refinance date?

Use months remaining, not the original mortgage term

A current 30-year mortgage with 25 years left has 300 remaining payments, not 360. Restarting it at 360 in the baseline gives the current option time it no longer has. The following original fictional comparison uses $250,000 outstanding at 6.5% with 300 months remaining. New fixed-rate quotes use assumed 5.5% for 180, 300 or 360 months, with $6,000 upfront fees, no financed charges or credits, and a 60-month holding date. No extra principal or other housing costs are included. Rates are illustrative, not current offers.

Distinguish affordability from comparative borrowing cost

A lower modeled cost cannot make an unaffordable monthly payment feasible. Conversely, a lower payment may be useful for liquidity but is not proof of lower total interest. Record your holding date, minimum reserve and other commitments before preferring a term. The comparison is undiscounted and assumes the same starting property and debt; it does not value the investment return on payment differences or apply tax deductions.

Reproduce each option by changing only new term in the refinance calculator. Review its cash and balance table, then download the complete monthly schedule. The guide’s examples use the same cents-rounded regular payment, unrounded internal interest and adjusted final payment convention.

Let payments stop at payoff

At a horizon beyond month 180, the shorter loan’s principal-and-interest payments stop after payoff. The other options may continue. At horizons beyond both payoff dates, include actual cumulative payments and zero remaining balances for both. Taxes, insurance and HOA do not disappear when the mortgage ends; enter supported housing-cost assumptions separately. The calculator does not infer early mortgage-insurance cancellation.

Keep fee financing and extra-payment ideas explicit

This comparison pays fees upfront. Financing them would change the new principal and schedule: use the closing-cost ledger to classify them once. Paying extra on a longer mortgage is another possible scenario, but this calculator does not model extra principal or prepayment penalties. Do not claim the scheduled 360-month row proves the result of an extra-payment strategy.

Common mistakes

Restarting the current loan at its original term, calling a lifetime total a five-year saving, ignoring debt remaining at sale and treating a higher payment as automatically worse are common errors. Read the break-even guide for why a shorter loan may have no simple payment-savings threshold while still reducing matched-date cost.

Sources and arithmetic conventions

OpenStax’s amortization explanation gives the fixed-payment principal-and-interest relationship. CFPB’s Loan Estimate explainer separates loan terms and P&I from other payment components. Our term scenarios were independently reconciled with a Decimal schedule. They are educational comparisons, not recommendations, underwriting decisions or savings guarantees.

Frequently asked questions

Can a shorter refinance cost less even if the payment rises?

Yes. Faster principal repayment can reduce interest and debt at the holding date, but the higher payment must be affordable.

Why compare balances after five years?

If you sell or repay then, the remaining principal still has to be settled. A lower payment may have deferred rather than eliminated that debt.

Does a longer term always cost more?

No universal rule covers all rates, fees and holding periods. In this example it costs more over its full life but less than keeping the old loan at the five-year comparison date.

Does the calculator include an extra-payment strategy?

No. These schedules use contractual payments only. Extra principal, payment recasting and prepayment penalties need a separate supported model.
