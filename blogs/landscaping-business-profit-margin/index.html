Landscaping Business Profit Margins Explained

Published September 20, 2026 · Updated September 20, 2026

Calculate landscaping business profit margin with gross, net, and contribution margins, owner pay, utilization, break-even math, and examples.

Landscaping · Profitability · Margin Planning

A healthy landscaping business profit margin is often about 10% to 20% after all operating costs, but your result depends on pricing, labor, route density, service mix, and how much capacity you sell. As a US owner-operator, the useful answer is the margin calculated from your own jobs, hours, equipment, and overhead.

Quick answer: Track gross margin on every job, then calculate net profit margin monthly after labor, materials, fuel, repairs, insurance, marketing, software, and a fair value for your labor. A 15% net margin can be workable, while 5% leaves little room for weather, breakdowns, unpaid estimates, or winter demand.

The direct answer

Your landscaping business profit margin is the share of sales left after the costs required to deliver work and operate the company. The headline measure is net profit margin:

Net profit margin = (revenue − all business expenses) ÷ revenue × 100

If you collect $20,000 in a month and spend $17,000 on the business, net profit is $3,000 and net margin is 15%. That is business profit before the owner’s personal income tax, distributions, or personal spending.

A practical planning range for an established operation is roughly 10% to 20% net margin. Treat that as planning guidance, not a promise or verified statistic. New companies may be lower while buying equipment and building a route.

Do not confuse profit with personal pay. Assign a reasonable wage to the hours you spend mowing, estimating, selling, scheduling, and managing; otherwise, unpaid labor gets mislabeled as profit. Your owner take-home may include wages, draws, or distributions, but those are cash-flow and tax decisions.

Three measures keep the picture honest:

  • Gross margin is what remains after direct job costs, including crew labor, payroll burden, plants, mulch, chemicals, subcontractors, disposal, and job-specific fuel. Gross profit divided by revenue gives gross margin.
  • Contribution margin is what each additional job contributes toward fixed overhead and profit after costs that change with that job. It is useful for pricing and break-even planning.
  • Net profit margin includes direct costs, overhead, and a fair cost for the owner’s work. Overhead may include insurance, equipment payments, software, advertising, accounting, licenses, rent, and replacement reserves.

For example, a $1,000 cleanup with $420 of direct costs has $580 of contribution profit and a 58% contribution margin. With $8,000 of monthly fixed overhead, you need about $13,793 in sales at that margin to cover overhead: $8,000 ÷ 0.58.

What changes the answer

Labor is often the largest swing factor. Include wages, payroll taxes, workers’ compensation, paid travel time where applicable, loading, and other nonproductive time. A crew paid for eight hours but billing six because of traffic and gaps has lower utilization than your estimate may assume. Price paid hours, not an idealized workday.

Capacity and utilization determine whether overhead gets covered. Capacity is the field work your available crew and equipment could perform. Utilization is the share that becomes productive, billable work. If a 40-hour week produces 26 productive hours, utilization is 65%. Better routing can increase profit without a price increase.

Route density matters. Ten properties in one neighborhood may require less windshield time than five scattered across a county. Price travel, setup, loading, and disposal as labor. Low-ticket mowing can fail when every stop requires a long drive.

Service mix changes margin and risk. Recurring mowing is predictable but may have limited price flexibility. Enhancements, pruning, irrigation repair, drainage, planting, and cleanups can create more contribution dollars but need accurate estimates for materials and callbacks. Review margins by service line.

Equipment costs are easy to understate. Fuel is visible, but depreciation, interest, maintenance, downtime, and replacement reserves also belong in the plan. An $18,000 mower lasting 1,800 productive hours has an illustrative capital allocation of $10 per productive hour before fuel and repairs.

Seasonality changes monthly results. Dormant lawns, drought, rain, snow, and local rules can alter demand. Build a twelve-month forecast and reserve cash during strong months. The SBA defines break-even as the point where revenue equals total cost.[1]

2–3 realistic worked scenarios

The following figures are illustrative planning examples, not statistics or case studies. Recalculate them with your invoices, payroll, hours, and bank statements.

Scenario 1: Solo recurring maintenance route

Suppose monthly revenue is $18,000. Direct costs are $4,200 for a helper and payroll burden, $1,350 for materials, $900 for fuel, $400 for disposal, and $450 for payment fees. Total direct cost is $7,300.

Gross profit is $18,000 − $7,300 = $10,700, so gross margin is 59.4%. Add $450 for insurance, $1,200 for truck and equipment, $250 for software, $450 for marketing, $300 for licenses, and $4,500 as owner wage. Overhead and owner labor total $7,150.

Net profit is $10,700 − $7,150 = $3,550, or 19.7% net margin. Without the owner wage, apparent profit would be $8,050 and the margin 44.7%. Use the recalculated 19.7% for pricing and growth.

Scenario 2: Enhancement and planting crew

Assume revenue of $32,000. Direct crew wages and payroll burden total $9,600; plants and hardscape materials, $8,000; subcontracted irrigation, $2,400; fuel and disposal, $1,200; and card fees, $800. Direct costs equal $22,000.

Gross profit is $10,000, so gross margin is 31.25%. Add $1,000 for insurance, $1,600 for equipment and vehicles, $700 for advertising, $450 for software, $600 for bookkeeping, $900 for repairs and reserves, and $3,500 for owner management. Total overhead and owner labor is $8,750.

Net profit is $1,250, or 3.9%. A 10% discount reduces revenue to $28,800; with costs unchanged, net result is −$1,950, or −6.8% margin. This shows how discounting can erase profit.

Scenario 3: Price and utilization improvement

Consider a route with 30 billable hours each week at $70 per hour. Four weeks produce $8,400 of revenue. At a variable cost of $28 per billable hour, contribution is $5,040 and contribution margin is 60%. With $4,500 of fixed overhead, net profit is $540, or 6.4%.

After tighter routing and improved pricing, the route produces 34 billable hours weekly at $75 per hour. Revenue becomes $10,200. Variable cost becomes $3,808, so contribution is $6,392 and contribution margin about 62.7%. After the same overhead, net profit is $1,892, or 18.5%. Check that extra work does not create overtime, quality problems, or unpaid travel.

How to run your own numbers

Use three to twelve months of bank statements, invoices, payroll reports, fuel receipts, and repair bills. Separate revenue by service line. Do not count an unearned deposit as completed revenue, and do not mix personal spending with business costs.

Build a job-costing sheet with quoted price, actual crew hours, driving and loading time, wages with payroll burden, materials, subcontractors, disposal, fuel, discounts, and payment fees. Compare estimates with actuals weekly to find leaks.

List monthly overhead, including insurance, permits, phones, software, accounting, advertising, rent, equipment payments, repairs, reserves, and interest. Convert annual bills to monthly planning amounts. Include a fair owner wage. The IRS says a deductible business expense generally must be ordinary and necessary, and personal expenses should be separated from business expenses.[2]

Calculate these three measures for each service line:

  1. Gross margin: (sales − direct costs) ÷ sales.
  2. Contribution margin: (sales − costs that change with the job) ÷ sales.
  3. Net margin: (sales − direct costs − overhead − owner labor) ÷ sales.

For a fast break-even check, divide monthly fixed overhead by contribution margin. If fixed overhead is $6,000 and contribution margin is 60%, break-even sales are $10,000. Add a buffer for weather, repairs, and late collections. Run your inputs in the landscaping break-even calculator and compare the output with your spreadsheet.

Stress-test the plan: ask what happens if productive hours fall 15%, materials rise 10%, or your average ticket increases $10. A resilient margin is more useful than a perfect forecast.

Common mistakes

Confusing markup with margin causes underpricing. A 30% markup on a $100 cost produces a $130 price, but margin is $30 ÷ $130, or 23.1%. To earn a 30% margin on $100 of cost, price is $100 ÷ 0.70 = $142.86.

Ignoring nonbillable time inflates your hourly rate. Include quoting, loading, callbacks, travel, and waiting. Capacity is not the same as billable utilization.

Treating owner draws inconsistently makes months impossible to compare. Track owner wages, draws, and distributions consistently, and keep taxes in a separate cash reserve.

Blending services hides weak work. A busy mowing route may subsidize underpriced planting. Review margin by service, crew, customer type, and route.

Counting sales tax, deposits, or loan proceeds as profit distorts results. Tax collected and unearned deposits are not operating revenue, while loan proceeds create cash but not profit.

Confusing cash flow with margin can still leave you short. Equipment purchases, loan principal, taxes, and inventory affect cash. Keep a cash forecast beside the income statement.

FAQs

What is a good landscaping business profit margin?

A practical target is roughly 10% to 20% net margin after direct costs, overhead, and a fair owner wage. It is not a guarantee; market and service mix can move it.

Is gross margin or net margin more important?

You need both. Gross margin tests the job; net margin tests the whole company. Strong gross margin cannot rescue excessive overhead or idle capacity.

Should I include my own labor in landscaping costs?

Yes. Include a reasonable wage for mowing, installing, estimating, scheduling, selling, and managing. Omitting your labor makes the economics look better than they are.

How does utilization affect profit?

Higher utilization spreads fixed costs across more productive work. If 160 paid hours produce 100 billable hours, raising billable time to 115 can improve profit without adding a truck, provided fatigue, travel, and quality are monitored.

What margin should I use when pricing a job?

Start with direct labor, materials, subcontractors, disposal, travel, and payment costs. Add overhead and price for your target contribution margin, then check scope and demand. Do not use a generic percentage without checking actual hours.

Can a landscaping business be profitable in the off-season?

It can, depending on climate and services. Plan month by month, then add snow, pruning, irrigation, cleanup, lighting, or commercial work only when pricing covers direct costs. Reserve peak-season cash for slow periods.

How often should I review my margin?

Review job margin weekly and company margin monthly; revise pricing before errors recur.

Takeaways

  • Track gross margin by job and net profit margin after every operating cost and a fair owner wage.
  • Treat utilization, route density, and service mix as profit drivers, not scheduling details.
  • Recalculate prices with actual labor, travel, equipment, materials, and overhead instead of relying on markup.
  • Use contribution margin and break-even sales to test slow-season, discount, and repair scenarios.
