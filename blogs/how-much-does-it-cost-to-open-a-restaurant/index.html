How Much Does It Cost to Open a Restaurant?

Published September 18, 2026 · Updated September 25, 2026

Estimate restaurant startup costs with an itemized opening budget, three worked examples, and a separate monthly sales target based on your own costs.

In the illustrative budgets below, opening cash is $90,500 for a counter-service shop, $188,000 for a small full-service restaurant, and $247,000 for a higher-rent concept. The number depends on the space, construction, equipment, staffing plan, menu, and opening cash needs. Opening cash and monthly break-even sales are separate calculations.

Every dollar figure below is illustrative, not a market statistic. Your lease, local rules, suppliers, and concept may produce a different budget.

Quick answer: Add one-time costs such as deposits, permits, construction, equipment, furniture, opening inventory, training, and launch marketing to find your opening cash requirement. Separately calculate monthly sales needed to cover recurring fixed costs, owner pay, and target profit after variable costs. Startup funding pays to launch the restaurant; break-even sales keep its monthly plan covered.

The direct answer: separate opening cash from monthly break-even

One-time startup costs include a lease deposit, permits, design, construction, ventilation, plumbing, equipment, furniture, signage, inventory, training, pre-opening wages, and launch marketing. Include a contingency for work you cannot price precisely yet.

Here is an illustrative budget for a small full-service restaurant:

The total is $188,000: $9,000 + $4,500 + $6,500 + $52,000 + $58,000 + $18,000 + $4,000 + $7,500 + $12,000 + $3,000 + $13,500.

That $188,000 is not the restaurant’s monthly break-even sales target. For the monthly calculation, separate costs that do not rise directly with each sale from costs that do. Suppose monthly fixed costs before owner pay are rent of $8,000, utilities of $1,500, insurance of $600, software of $400, manager and administrative wages of $9,500, loan payments of $2,000, and other overhead of $1,000. Those costs total $23,000.

Now add owner pay of $5,000 and target profit of $4,000. The required contribution from sales is:

$23,000 fixed costs + $5,000 owner pay + $4,000 target profit = $32,000

Assume variable costs use 32% of revenue. This could include ingredients, packaging, payment processing, waste, and other costs that move with sales. The contribution margin is 100% - 32% = 68%.

The monthly sales target is:

$32,000 ÷ 0.68 = $47,058.82

If the restaurant opens 26 days per month, the daily target is $47,058.82 ÷ 26 = $1,809.95. If the average guest check is $32, it needs $1,809.95 ÷ $32 = 56.56, or about 57 checks per day. The seats, hours, kitchen, and takeout process must handle that volume. The $188,000 startup requirement remains a separate funding question; you may also need cash for a slow first month, replenishment, payroll timing, taxes, and repairs.

What changes the answer

The condition of the space changes the buildout. A second-generation restaurant with a working hood, grease trap, electrical capacity, plumbing, restrooms, and usable finishes may need less construction than a raw retail unit. Ask for written estimates and confirm who pays for required work.

The service model changes equipment and labor. A counter-service shop may need fewer seats, tables, servers, and dining-room fixtures. A full-service concept may need more tableware, front-of-house staff, and service stations. A pizza shop, bar, bakery, and grill also need different equipment packages.

The owner’s role changes both cash needs and monthly costs. If you work every shift, you may hire fewer people before opening. Your labor is still a planning cost. Add owner pay or a replacement wage so the restaurant is tested against the work it must support if you take time off.

The menu changes the contribution margin. A high-volume item with low ingredient cost may contribute more toward rent than a premium item with expensive inputs, waste, packaging, or delivery fees. Cost recipes, then include channel-specific costs before deciding what percentage of sales is available for fixed costs.

Season and ramp-up change the cash reserve. A monthly break-even target does not mean each opening month will reach it. Plan cash by month for rent, payroll, inventory, taxes, debt payments, and repairs, including the gap between early sales and outgoing bills.

Three worked restaurant budgets

The following illustrative cases show how a different concept or space changes the startup budget and monthly sales requirement.

1. Lean counter-service shop

A counter-service concept might budget $6,000 for the deposit and initial rent, $2,500 for permits and professional fees, $26,000 for light buildout, $28,000 for kitchen equipment, $8,000 for the counter and furniture, $2,000 for POS and signage, $4,500 for opening inventory, $6,000 for training and pre-opening payroll, $1,500 for launch marketing, and $6,000 for contingency.

The opening total is $90,500. The arithmetic is $6,000 + $2,500 + $26,000 + $28,000 + $8,000 + $2,000 + $4,500 + $6,000 + $1,500 + $6,000 = $90,500.

Assume monthly fixed costs before owner pay are rent of $4,500, utilities of $800, insurance of $350, software of $250, support wages of $5,800, loan payments of $800, and other overhead of $800. These total $13,300. Add $4,000 owner pay and $2,500 target profit for a required contribution of $19,800. With variable costs at 30%, the contribution margin is 70%, so required sales are $19,800 ÷ 0.70 = $28,285.71 per month. Over 26 open days, that is $1,087.91 per day.

2. Small full-service restaurant

Use the $188,000 startup budget from the first example. Fixed costs before owner pay are $23,000 per month. Add $5,000 owner pay and $4,000 target profit, giving a required contribution of $32,000. At a 68% contribution margin, required sales are $32,000 ÷ 0.68 = $47,058.82 per month.

At 26 open days, the daily target is $47,058.82 ÷ 26 = $1,809.95. With a $32 average guest check, the restaurant needs $1,809.95 ÷ $32 = 56.56, or about 57 checks per day. That count must fit the dining room and kitchen.

3. Higher-rent chef-led restaurant

This concept budgets $24,000 for the deposit and initial rent, $7,000 for permits and professional fees, $10,000 for design, $70,000 for buildout, $72,000 for kitchen and refrigeration, $25,000 for furniture and fixtures, $5,000 for POS and signage, $10,000 for opening inventory, $15,000 for pre-opening payroll, $5,000 for launch marketing, and $4,000 for contingency.

Monthly fixed costs before owner pay are rent of $12,000, payroll of $18,000, utilities of $2,000, insurance of $800, software of $500, loan payments of $4,700, and other overhead of $1,500. Add $6,000 owner pay and $7,000 target profit: $39,500 + $6,000 + $7,000 = $52,500. With variable costs at 34%, the contribution margin is 66%, so required sales are $52,500 ÷ 0.66 = $79,545.45 per month. Over 26 open days, the target is $79,545.45 ÷ 26 = $3,059.44 per day.

That figure is a capacity test. If the dining room and kitchen cannot deliver it, change the plan before signing the lease. Compare the required checks with seating, kitchen throughput, and the service schedule.

How to run your own numbers

List one-time startup costs separately from monthly costs. For the monthly model, enter rent, recurring payroll, owner pay, insurance, software, utilities, debt payments, and other overhead. Estimate variable costs from recipes and sales channels, add target profit, and compare the sales requirement with open days, average check, seats, and kitchen capacity. Common mistakes

Putting the full equipment purchase into monthly break-even. An oven, hood, table, or POS system bought before opening belongs in startup cash. Only a recurring lease or financing payment belongs in the monthly cost line.

Leaving owner pay out. A restaurant that pays rent but pays you nothing has not met a plan that includes your income. Add owner pay separately, even if you cover the work yourself during the first year.

Using food cost alone as variable cost. Payment fees, delivery commissions, packaging, discounts, waste, and some hourly labor may also rise with sales. Include the costs that apply to each sales channel before calculating contribution margin.

Funding the buildout but not the ramp. A deposit and equipment budget can leave no cash for the first payroll, replenishment order, tax payment, or repair. Create a month-by-month cash plan instead of assuming opening day is the end of the funding need.

Rounding down the target. A $47,058.82 sales requirement is not covered by $47,000 in sales under the same assumptions. Keep the precise amount in the model and round daily check targets up to a whole check.

FAQs

Is $100,000 enough to open a restaurant?

It may cover a small counter-service concept in a fitted space with limited construction. In the illustrative example above, that shop requires $90,500 before any extra cash needed for a slow opening period. Get local quotes and build a monthly cash plan before deciding.

What is usually the biggest startup cost?

Construction and kitchen equipment can be large categories, but the largest item depends on the space. A raw unit can require ventilation, plumbing, electrical and code work; a fitted restaurant may shift spending toward rent, payroll or replacement equipment.

Does break-even include my salary?

Include owner pay if the plan must support your income. Add it to the monthly requirement, then add any target profit beyond that pay.

How much cash should remain after opening?

Build a month-by-month cash plan covering payroll, rent, inventory, taxes, repairs and debt payments. Fund the periods when expected cash receipts fall short; no single reserve amount fits every restaurant.

What if I do not know my variable-cost percentage?

Start with an explicitly labeled assumption. Replace it with recipe-level costs, card fees, packaging and delivery commissions as you collect quotes and sales data.

Can a restaurant be profitable before it reaches break-even sales?

Not under the same cost definition. A month can pay some bills while falling short of the owner pay or profit goal included in the model.

Takeaways

  • Keep one-time startup cash separate from recurring monthly costs.
  • Include owner pay and target profit when setting the monthly sales requirement.
  • Convert required sales into daily checks and check the kitchen and seating capacity.
  • Update the budget with local quotes before committing to a lease.
