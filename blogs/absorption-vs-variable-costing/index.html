Absorption vs Variable Costing: Inventory and Profit Reconciliation

Absorption and variable costing differ in how fixed manufacturing overhead enters product cost and current-period expense. Reconcile production, sales and inventory before explaining a profit difference between the two reports.

All amounts and operating results below are original fictional teaching examples in USD. They are not customer results, industry averages or forecasts. Use your own dated records and applicable accounting policies.

Separate manufacturing cost from selling and administration

In the simplified methods compared here, both reports include variable manufacturing cost in units produced. Absorption costing also assigns fixed manufacturing overhead to inventory; variable costing charges that fixed manufacturing amount to the period instead. Selling and administrative costs remain outside manufacturing inventory in this case. Fixed marketing expense must not be absorbed merely because it is fixed.

OpenStax’s comparison explains the basic timing difference. The following two-month USD case is original fictional teaching material, not a company result. It assumes one homogeneous product, no work in process, returns, scrap, taxes, financing, valuation write-down or overhead spending variance. Actual financial reporting needs the applicable inventory policy and classification review; the exercise does not establish tax treatment or compliance for a particular entity.

Calculate one supported manufacturing rate

Assume direct materials $12, direct labor $5 and variable manufacturing overhead $3 per completed unit. Variable manufacturing cost is therefore $20. Fixed manufacturing overhead is $6,000 per month. The selected normal-capacity illustration is 1,000 units each month, equal to actual production in both months, giving $6 fixed manufacturing overhead per unit. Absorption manufacturing cost is $26 per unit; variable manufacturing cost is $20.

Selling price is $40 per unit sold. Variable selling cost is $2 per unit sold, and fixed selling/administrative cost is $3,000 monthly. Keep the production denominator separate from the sales denominator: manufacturing allocation uses the specified production/capacity basis, while variable selling expense follows sales in this case. Direct labor is assumed variable for this exercise only; a real workforce arrangement can behave differently and must be classified from its facts.

Reconcile the physical units before comparing income

Month one starts with zero finished goods, produces 1,000 units and sells 800, leaving 200. Month two starts with those 200, produces another 1,000 and sells 1,100, leaving 100. The identity beginning units + units completed − units sold = ending units holds for each month. No unit can be sold twice or disappear solely because the reporting method changes.

Prepare the two reports from the same facts

Month-one sales are $32,000. Absorption COGS is 800 × $26 = $20,800. After $1,600 variable selling and $3,000 fixed selling/administration, operating profit is $6,600. Variable costing deducts $16,000 variable manufacturing COGS and $1,600 variable selling to reach $14,400 contribution, then deducts $6,000 fixed manufacturing and $3,000 fixed selling/administration, giving $5,400.

Explain the profit bridge through inventory

Use amounts, not only the direction of unit movement: absorption profit − variable profit = fixed manufacturing overhead in ending inventory − fixed manufacturing overhead in beginning inventory, under this case’s assumptions. In month one the bridge is 200 × $6 − $0 = $1,200, so $6,600 − $5,400 = $1,200. Absorption defers that amount in the 200 units still held.

Month two starts with $1,200 of fixed overhead in inventory and ends with 100 × $6 = $600. The bridge is $600 − $1,200 = −$600, matching $10,200 − $10,800. More prior fixed overhead leaves inventory through sales than enters the closing balance. Across both months, absorption profit is $16,800 versus $16,200 variable profit; the remaining $600 difference equals fixed overhead still included in the final inventory. This is an expense-timing difference, not $600 newly collected cash.

Do not inflate inventory by ignoring capacity or exclusions

Normal capacity is not interchangeable with whatever production denominator produces the most favorable profit. The Australian standard setter’s AASB 102 paragraphs 12–13, an IFRS-aligned reference for the specified Australian reporting scope, describe production-overhead allocation and normal-capacity treatment, including unallocated overhead when relevant. Do not silently treat that framework-specific reference as a US tax rule. For US nongovernmental GAAP, confirm current applicable requirements in the FASB Accounting Standards Codification; pre-Codification summaries are not the current authority.

If actual output falls or idle capacity appears, reassess the allocation and expense treatment instead of automatically dividing the full pool by fewer units. Likewise, a management decision to produce extra stock requires demand, storage, obsolescence and funding evidence. Higher absorption profit caused by inventory growth does not show that customer sales or economic performance improved. The example assumes actual equals the selected normal capacity specifically to isolate timing without an unresolved volume variance.

Use the comparison for the decision it can support

A variable-costing report can make cost behavior and contribution visible for a defined internal decision, but it must not omit fixed commitments when assessing total profitability. An absorption unit cost includes assigned fixed manufacturing cost, so it is not automatically the incremental cost of one extra order. Identify spare capacity, additional setup, avoidable resources and opportunity cost before accepting or rejecting work.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main difference between absorption and variable costing?

In the simplified comparison, absorption includes fixed manufacturing overhead in product cost; variable costing expenses that fixed manufacturing amount in the period.

Why is absorption profit higher in month one?

The $1,200 fixed manufacturing overhead in the 200 unsold units remains in absorption inventory instead of becoming current-period expense.

Why is absorption profit lower in month two?

Opening inventory releases $1,200 fixed overhead while closing inventory retains $600, making absorption profit $600 below variable profit in that month.

Should fixed selling expense be included in inventory?

Not in this case. The fixed manufacturing versus nonmanufacturing boundary matters; being fixed does not itself make a cost inventoriable.

Can the inventory unit change always be multiplied by today’s overhead rate?

Only when the relevant fixed-overhead content and other assumptions make that shortcut valid. Different opening cost layers require their actual amounts.

Does an absorption-profit increase prove cash generation?

No. Inventory timing can change reported profit while using cash to fund unsold goods. Review demand, capacity, carrying value and cash separately.
