Restaurant Menu Pricing: Ingredients to Order Contribution

Published September 17, 2026Updated October 7, 2026

Published by MyBreakeven. Editorial standards and corrections. Report a calculation or content issue to support@mybreakeven.com.

Price restaurant menu items from usable portions, waste, packaging and fees. Compare order contribution, monthly targets and kitchen worker-hour capacity.

RestaurantPricingProfit Planning

Price a menu item from its usable ingredient portions, order-related costs and the contribution it needs to leave for the restaurant. Food-cost percentage is useful, but it does not include every cost of selling the order. In the example below, a $22 dish has $6 of ingredients and leaves $11.34 after all listed variable costs and fees.

That restaurant needs 1,764 comparable orders for a $2,000 monthly profit goal. The following fictional USD examples separate recipe cost, waste, packaging and compensation so the same expense is not counted twice.

Quick answer

Calculate ingredient cost from usable yield and portion size, then add any separate waste allowance, packaging, truly variable labor, acquisition and fees. For a chosen contribution, divide those fixed-dollar order costs plus the contribution goal by one minus the fee rate. Check that customers, menu mix and kitchen capacity support the resulting price and order target.

Cost the portion the kitchen serves

Start with the recipe and actual portion. Record the purchased quantity, usable yield, unit cost and number of portions. A supplier's kilogram price is not the same as the cost of a kilogram after trimming, cooking loss or unusable pieces.

Suppose an ingredient costs $8 per purchased kilogram. A three-kilogram purchase costs $24. With 80% usable yield, it supplies 2.4 usable kilograms. At 0.15 kilograms per portion, that is 16 portions, making this ingredient $1.50 per serving.

Compare a lower 70% yield: the same purchase supplies 2.1 kilograms, or 14 portions, and costs about $1.71 per serving. The supplier price has not changed, but each usable portion costs more. Validate the yield with kitchen records rather than a rounded guess.

Repeat that calculation for the other ingredients, including sauces, garnish and oil assigned to the dish. The example's full recipe totals $6 per serving. If measured yield already includes a particular preparation loss, do not add that same loss again in a separate waste allowance.

Use invoices and actual serving records from comparable periods. A bulk purchase discount may lower unit cost but also increase spoilage or cash tied in stock. Keep those effects visible rather than treating the cheapest purchase price as the cheapest usable portion.

Keep food cost and full order cost separate

Input for one comparable order Amount
Net modeled customer price $22
Usable ingredients from the recipe $6
Separate waste allowance, beyond measured recipe yield $0.50
Packaging $1
Truly variable additional order labor $2
Acquisition cost $0.50
Payment fee 3%

The $6 ingredient amount is 27.27% of the $22 price. Ingredients plus the separate waste allowance are $6.50, or 29.55%. Those are explicitly defined food-cost ratios in this example.

The $10 of total direct costs before fees includes packaging, labor and acquisition too. Calling $10 ÷ $22 a food-cost ratio would hide the distinction. The payment fee adds $0.66, making full variable costs $10.66 and leaving $11.34 contribution.

The $2 labor represents genuinely additional order-linked labor under the illustrative staffing arrangement. Committed payroll is in monthly overhead. If your kitchen staff are paid the same committed wages regardless of this order count, do not add another variable wage for those same hours.

This guide uses one average completed order containing the modeled dish and no extra revenue. If the actual bill includes drinks or several dishes, build a consistent weighted order with all associated costs and worker-hours. Do not put a whole table's bill against one plate's ingredients.

Turn the cost into a contribution-based price

Suppose you want the order to leave $12 after the listed variable costs and percentage fee. The required price is ($10 + $12) ÷ 0.97 = $22.6804, rounded upward to $22.69. At that price, contribution is $22.69 × 0.97 − $10 = $12.0093.

The denominator matters because the 3% fee applies to the selling price. Adding 3% to the dollar cost does not necessarily fund a 3% deduction from the final revenue. If a fixed processor charge also applies, include it in the fixed-dollar order costs before solving.

An ingredient-only approach gives a different question. With a chosen 30% ingredient-cost ratio, $6 ÷ 0.30 = $20. That tells you the price associated with that recipe-cost ratio, but it says nothing by itself about packaging, fees, payroll, rent or your income.

At $20, the full example leaves $20 × 0.97 − $10 = $9.40 contribution. The dish could meet that ingredient percentage while needing far more orders to cover the restaurant. Choose the ratio and contribution target consciously, then check customer value and alternatives before adopting the price.

Check the monthly plan

Monthly overhead is $15,000, including the committed payroll not already in direct costs. Owner pay is $3,000, and the additional profit goal is $2,000. The required monthly contribution is therefore $20,000.

At the $22 base price, $20,000 ÷ $11.34 = 1,763.67, rounded up to 1,764 comparable orders. Revenue is $38,808. Monthly profit after owner pay is 1,764 × $11.34 − $18,000 = $2,003.76.

The operating threshold without the additional profit goal is $18,000 ÷ $11.34 = 1,587.30, rounded up to 1,588 orders. Keep the two targets labeled. Covering the bills and chosen owner pay is different from also creating the desired surplus.

Four workers with 35 weekly hours and 75% delivery utilization have 455 monthly worker-hours using 52 ÷ 12 weeks. At 0.2 combined worker-hours per comparable order, the capacity ceiling is 2,275 orders.

An order served by several workers can use more worker-hours than its elapsed ticket time. The 0.2-hour input means their combined work, not twelve minutes for every person separately. Kitchen stations, seats and peak service periods may impose tighter limits than this monthly worker-hour model.

The $24 case uses $24 × 0.97 − $10 = $13.28. The waste case uses $22 × 0.97 − $11 = $10.34. Both change the required volume, even before any effect on demand.

The last case assumes a 25% platform fee and 3% processor fee both apply separately to the same $22 revenue base. Under that explicit simplified assumption, contribution is $22 × 0.72 − $10 = $5.84. It needs 3,425 orders, beyond the 2,275-order worker-hour ceiling.

Your actual platform statement may include processing, use another charge base or apply fixed fees. Reconcile it rather than automatically adding 25% and 3%. The delivery-app commission guide handles that separate channel decision.

Review menu mix before changing every price

An ingredient-heavy special can lower average contribution if it becomes a large share of orders. A drink or side may lift the basket, but include its preparation, ingredients and fees. Weighted revenue without weighted costs gives a misleading result.

Review collected price, product mix, waste and completed orders together. If a promotion increases covers while reducing contribution per order, calculate whether the added contribution covers the same monthly budget and any new labor commitment.

The restaurant food-cost guide explains ingredient reporting, while the prime-cost guide considers food and payroll at business level. Keep those measures separate from per-order contribution instead of giving several different totals the same label.

Common mistakes

  • Using purchased weight instead of usable yield when costing portions.
  • Calling ingredients, packaging and labor together a food-cost percentage.
  • Adding a waste allowance for a loss already included in recipe yield.
  • Entering committed payroll again as avoidable per-order labor.
  • Counting a platform's included processing charge twice.
  • Comparing a whole-table receipt with one dish's cost and time.

FAQs

Is food-cost percentage enough to price a dish?

It describes the ingredient cost relative to the price under a defined basis. It does not include every order or monthly expense. Check full contribution and monthly sales requirements alongside it.

Should packaging be counted as food cost?

Keep it labeled separately so the ingredient ratio stays understandable. Packaging still belongs in full order-variable cost. The distinction helps you compare eat-in and takeaway channels.

How should I estimate waste?

Use preparation, spoilage and service records where available. Separate a loss already included in usable recipe yield from any additional waste allowance. Do not charge for the same loss twice.

Where do fixed staff wages belong?

Committed payroll belongs in monthly overhead or a reconciled allocation. Truly additional order-linked wages can belong in direct cost. Choose a consistent method that agrees with your actual compensation arrangement.

Can I simply add platform and payment fee percentages?

Only when they are separately charged on the same revenue base. Some arrangements include processing or use different bases. Check the statement and model the actual charges once.

Does the calculated price predict customer demand?

No, it shows what the stated costs and contribution goal require. Customer response, competing offers and perceived value remain separate questions. Test a price and menu mix before treating the assumed order volume as realistic.

Takeaways

  • Cost usable portions rather than purchased weight alone.
  • Label ingredient cost and full order cost separately.
  • Reconcile payroll, waste and channel fees once.
  • Check price against contribution, demand and operational capacity.
