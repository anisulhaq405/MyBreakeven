A business continuity plan identifies the minimum essential service to maintain or restore, who can act, the dependencies required and how recovery will be tested. It turns disruption priorities into a usable operating sequence rather than a list of risks alone.

The numerical case below is an original fictional planning example, not a customer result, market benchmark or professional determination. All monetary figures use USD. Use the same unit and period throughout, and replace assumptions with source records.

A business continuity plan names the minimum service to restore

A business continuity plan explains how an organization will maintain or resume its most important operations during disruption. Start with the service customers need, the point at which interruption becomes unacceptable and the people authorized to act. A directory of emergency contacts is useful, but it does not establish that orders, payroll or essential records can be recovered.

Keep three tasks connected but distinct. A risk assessment considers possible events and their consequences. Emergency response protects people and addresses immediate hazards. Continuity planning specifies the minimum operating service, dependencies, workarounds and recovery sequence. The business risk assessment guide owns the risk comparison; this guide owns the practical operating recovery record.

Use a business impact analysis to set priorities

List critical activities and ask what happens as each remains unavailable. Record missed obligations, customer disruption, safety implications, contribution lost, extra operating costs and cash delays separately. The impact of losing a scheduling system at payroll cutoff can differ from losing it during a quiet afternoon. State the timing and workload instead of assigning a generic severity label alone.

Name the dependencies required to deliver the minimum service: staff skills, workspace, equipment, suppliers, communications, power, access permissions and usable records. Include external providers whose restoration you cannot control. The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security describes continuity as a lifecycle that includes analysis, strategies, documented responsibilities and testing. Our operational cases below are independent teaching examples, not a certified continuity standard.

Distinguish recovery time from acceptable data loss

A recovery time objective is the targeted time to restore a defined service level. A recovery point objective describes the acceptable point to which data can be restored, often expressed as a maximum period of data loss. Neither is the same as a backup frequency promise, the time to start working on the problem or the time at which every backlog item is completed.

For an illustrative order-entry service, management sets a six-hour recovery-time objective and a two-hour recovery-point objective. A test restores usable service after eight hours and restores a snapshot from three hours fifteen minutes before interruption. The test misses the service objective by two hours and the data objective by one hour fifteen minutes. Record these as gaps; do not change the objectives simply to make a failed test look successful.

Worked example: lost jobs, deferred jobs and extra costs

A fictional service business normally completes 40 jobs per day, each with $90 revenue and $55 avoidable variable cost. Contribution is $35 per completed job. A two-day disruption affects 80 planned jobs. A tested manual workaround completes 12 on day one and 20 on day two, or 32 jobs. The shortfall against the original two-day plan is 48 jobs.

Of those 48,18 customers cancel and 30 agree to a later date. The business plans six extra jobs per day over the next five days to recover those 30 deferred jobs, with separate evidence that people and equipment can provide the extra capacity. Workaround activation costs $320 and the additional recovery effort costs $240. These are assumed incremental costs, not wage rates, legal overtime calculations or supplier quotations.

Do not count delayed revenue as permanently lost profit

The normal 80-job plan would contribute $2,800. The 32 jobs completed during disruption contribute $1,120; the 30 later completed jobs contribute $1,050. Combined contribution is $2,170 before the additional $560 response cost, leaving $1,610. Compared with the original $2,800, the adverse effect is $1,190. This cross-check assumes the deferred jobs retain the original revenue and variable cost and no further work is canceled.

The $2,700 deferred collection is a dated cash issue, not an extra permanent loss on top of the $630 canceled contribution. If a customer later cancels, change the classification with evidence. Put the actual collection dates and response payments in the cash-flow forecast. Do not assume insurance pays the shortfall, covers these costs or pays before bills fall due; policy and claim terms require separate review.

Write a workaround that can be used under pressure

For each critical service, record the activation condition, named decision role, backup role, required access, minimum inputs, step sequence, approval and return-to-normal checks. Store an accessible copy in a way that does not depend entirely on the service being unavailable. Keep confidential credentials and personal contact details out of a public worksheet or an uncontrolled printed pack.

A manual order log needs a unique temporary identifier, customer commitment, status and later reconciliation owner. When the primary system returns, reconcile the manual items before importing them to avoid duplicate work or duplicate customer charges. The product lifecycle management record can identify which released product instructions remain valid during a change; the continuity pack should refer to an approved version rather than an obsolete attachment.

Validate people, training and recovery capacity

A backup person must have the relevant training, permissions and actual availability. Merely writing a second name does not demonstrate that the person can approve payments, restore a machine or complete an inspection. Use the small-business HR workflow to keep role and training records aligned with the continuity plan. Respect separation between requesting, approving and reconciling an emergency payment.

A recovery promise also needs a deliverable schedule. The six extra jobs in the example must fit after ordinary commitments, with materials and inspection time available. The capacity planning guide tests that load against bottlenecks. Payroll recovery has another boundary: gross pay versus net pay distinguishes earnings, deductions and employee payment, so restoring a net bank file alone is not proof that the underlying payroll record is complete.

Exercise the plan, record failure and close the actions

Choose a realistic scenario and a defined test scope. A tabletop exercise checks whether people understand the decision sequence; a restore test checks whether usable records and service can actually return. Record start time, achieved service level, restored-data date, missing permissions, reconciliation errors and customer commitments. Do not describe an unperformed restore as tested recovery.

Assign each gap a named role, due date, interim control and retest criterion. Review the pack when suppliers, systems, staff responsibilities or product versions change. Archive the superseded plan with its review record and clearly mark the current version. A continuity plan is useful when it exposes an unworkable assumption early, not when every box is marked complete without evidence.

Keep an editable decision record

Open the printable business continuity plan record. Record the evidence, calculation, responsible role and next review before making a commitment. This is a manual worksheet: fields accept typing, but do not calculate, submit information or save after reload. Print or save a PDF while the entries are present. Use non-sensitive aliases rather than employee identifiers, account numbers or confidential customer records.

Frequently asked questions

What belongs in a small-business continuity plan?

A defined minimum service, business impacts, recovery priorities, dependencies, authorized roles, tested workarounds, reconciliation steps and a review record.

How does a continuity plan differ from a risk assessment?

A risk assessment compares possible events and consequences. A continuity plan explains which essential service resumes first and how people, records and dependencies support that recovery.

Are recovery time and recovery point the same?

No. Recovery time concerns how quickly a stated service level is restored. Recovery point concerns the acceptable age or loss of restored data. Each needs its own test evidence.

Why is $2,700 deferred collection not added to the $1,190 loss?

The 30 deferred jobs are assumed to be completed later at their original economics. Their receipts move in time; the permanent contribution reduction comes from 18 canceled jobs plus the specified extra response costs.

Does a backup prove the business can continue?

No. The records must be accessible, restorable and usable by authorized people alongside the other operating dependencies. A backup file alone does not establish recovered service.

How often should the plan be reviewed?

Choose a review cycle appropriate to the operation and update it when important people, systems, suppliers, duties or product versions change. This guide does not prescribe a universal legal testing interval.
