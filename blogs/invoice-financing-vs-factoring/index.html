Invoice Financing vs Factoring: Fees, Reserves and Cash Timing

Invoice financing commonly uses receivables to support borrowing; factoring commonly involves selling receivables to a factor. Product labels vary, so read who owns the receivable, who collects, what cash is advanced and what remains at risk. Reconcile the advance, held reserve and fees before comparing apparent funding percentages.

All numerical examples are original fictional USD teaching cases. They are not market rates, customer results, eligibility determinations or forecasts. Replace assumptions with dated records.

Separate borrowing against an invoice from selling it

In a borrowing arrangement, the business commonly records an advance and a repayment obligation secured or supported by receivables. In a factoring arrangement, a factor commonly purchases qualifying receivables and may handle collection. Agreements differ on notification, servicing, security, recourse and accounting consequences. The phrase “invoice finance” can also be used as an umbrella term, so the contract matters more than the marketing label.

The OCC’s receivables-financing material provides the secured-lending and factoring background. It does not establish the terms of a current commercial offer. Keep the underlying invoice and the funding agreement as separate records.

Reconcile a $20,000 factored invoice

Assume a valid, undisputed $20,000 B2B invoice is purchased under a hypothetical agreement. The factor advances 80% ($16,000), holds $4,000 as reserve, and charges a fixed 2% fee on invoice face value for the stated 30-day period. No other charges apply. The customer pays the factor in full on day 30, and the factor releases the reserve less the fee.

The business receives $19,600 in total, not $16,000 plus another $20,000 collection. The customer’s payment to the factor settles the purchased receivable under the stated arrangement; adding it again as business cash would double-count the invoice. Whether accounting treats a specific transaction as a sale or borrowing requires the agreement and applicable standards, not just this cash illustration.

The same fee percentage can describe a different cost

The $400 fee above is 2% of invoice face value, but 2.5% of the $16,000 initial advance. If a different offer charged 2% only on the advance for the same period, its charge would be $320, assuming no other differences. Always identify the fee base and covered dates.

For perspective only, $400 ÷ $16,000 × 365 ÷ 30 = about 30.42% as a simple annualized fee-to-initial-advance comparison. This is not a quoted APR, effective annual rate or complete legal disclosure: it does not incorporate the actual daily balance, compounding, timing of all charges or other contractual conditions. Use the entire cash schedule and appropriate disclosures when comparing real offers.

If the customer pays on day 45, the cost depends on the contract. A second full 30-day fee would make the hypothetical total $800; a prorated extension or grace period would produce a different result. Neither follows automatically from a “2%” headline. Get the fee trigger and stop date in writing.

Compare a borrowing arrangement on matching cash and dates

Now consider a separate hypothetical invoice-backed advance of $16,000 with a stated $320 charge for 30 days and no reserve mechanism. The business collects the $20,000 invoice from its customer and repays $16,320. Across the period, cash received is $16,000 advance plus $20,000 collection less $16,320 repayment, leaving $19,680. The $16,000 principal is financing cash, not additional sales revenue.

This case has an $80 lower charge than the factoring example, but it does not prove financing is always cheaper. The two providers may differ in servicing, eligibility, collection risk, guarantees, minimum fees and timing. A fair decision compares the same usable advance, actual payment dates and retained obligations. Ask whether a lockbox or controlled collection account applies instead of assuming the customer always pays the business directly.

Review recourse, disputes and invoice eligibility

Nonrecourse is not a universal promise that every unpaid invoice is protected. The agreement may distinguish a specified debtor credit event from a service dispute, fraud, return, credit note or invoice ineligibility. Record what triggers repayment, repurchase or a reduction in availability, and who bears the loss in each case.

Suppose a $2,000 credit is issued against the original $20,000 invoice before customer payment. The collectible balance is now $18,000. Do not continue treating $20,000 as an unquestioned funding base or assume the factor absorbs the credit. Notify the provider according to the agreement and reconcile any reserve adjustment or repayment demand. Receivables process controls and credit-loss review address related invoice records without determining financing eligibility.

Use invoice funding to solve a dated gap, not hide weak contribution

Document the cash payment that needs bridging and the invoice collection expected to repay it. If a job leaves $600 contribution before a $400 funding charge, only $200 remains before fixed costs and any other unentered expenses. An advance changes timing; it does not increase the job’s revenue or repair an underlying loss.

Use working-capital funding analysis for the size and repayment source of a short gap, the funding comparison guide for complete offer costs, and the 13-week cash forecast to place the advance, customer receipt and settlement on their dates. Match the receivable ledger to the provider’s statements so reserves do not disappear from the review simply because they were not immediately paid out.

Frequently asked questions

Is an 80% advance a 20% fee?

No. The withheld 20% can be a reserve released after settlement, less the actual agreed fee and adjustments. Reconcile the contract’s reserve separately.

Does factoring always transfer every risk?

No. Recourse, credit protection, invoice disputes and exclusions are contract-specific. Nonrecourse does not establish universal protection for every nonpayment.

Is a 2% monthly fee the same as 2% APR?

No. The fee base, time period and payment schedule differ. A simple annualized comparison is also not automatically a quoted APR.

Who should collect from the customer?

The agreement determines the payment route, servicing, notification and any controlled collection account. Do not infer those terms from the product name alone.

Can I finance a disputed invoice at face value?

Do not assume so. Provider eligibility rules, credits, disputes and customer concentration can reduce the qualifying balance or create recourse obligations.

Does an invoice advance create more sales?

No. It changes funding and cash timing. Keep invoice revenue, borrowing or sale proceeds, fees and customer settlement distinct in the records.
