Accrual Accounting: Revenue, Expenses and Cash Timing

Compare earned revenue, incurred expenses and cash timing using one reconciled service-business example. Track receivables, prepayments and customer advances.

Quick answer

Accrual accounting records revenue and expenses in the periods to which they belong under the accounting framework, rather than using cash dates alone. Separate the delivery, billing and settlement evidence before comparing profit with a bank balance.

Read the source-backed application and limits. Worked business amounts are fictional teaching examples.

All amounts and operating results below are original fictional teaching examples in USD. They are not customer results, industry averages or forecasts. Use your own dated records and applicable accounting policies.

Identify what happened before choosing the date

For this introductory financial-reporting example, assume an accepted service gives an unconditional right to payment and all relevant recognition conditions are satisfied in November. The invoice is issued later. The invoice date by itself does not determine when revenue is earned. If payment depends on additional performance, a contract asset or another treatment may be relevant; do not automatically call every unbilled amount accounts receivable.

Likewise, distinguish a service consumed, an asset purchased and an advance paid. A December payment for November work can settle an existing obligation. Insurance paid in advance can provide coverage over several periods. These distinctions organize the evidence; they are not an election of a permissible US tax method.

The insurance initially creates a $1,200 prepaid asset. One month of coverage is then consumed, leaving $1,100. The $2,400 advance is assumed refundable or deliverable under the contract and wholly unearned at November 30. It is not extra November sales merely because money arrived. No sales tax, bad-debt adjustment, depreciation or other expenses are included in this deliberately limited example.

Reconcile November profit, cash and balances

Earned revenue is $5,000. Included expenses are $1,800 payroll plus $100 insurance, so accrual profit is $3,100. Operating receipts less payments in this case are $2,400 − $1,200 = $1,200. The $1,900 difference between profit and this operating cash movement is explained by $5,000 new receivable, $1,100 remaining prepaid, $1,800 unpaid wages and $2,400 customer advance: $3,100 − $5,000 − $1,100 + $1,800 + $2,400 = $1,200.

Add a separate $10,000 owner cash investment at the beginning. Closing cash becomes $11,200, not $3,100. Assets are $11,200 cash + $5,000 receivable + $1,100 prepaid = $17,300. Liabilities are $1,800 wages + $2,400 advance = $4,200. Equity is $10,000 investment + $3,100 profit = $13,100. Thus $17,300 = $4,200 + $13,100.

This is a financial-reporting illustration. A simple cash receipts/payments view of these transactions is not a complete tax-basis income computation. Loan principal, equipment purchases, deposits, constructive receipt and applicable tax rules can require additional analysis.

Carry the balances into December without counting twice

On December 15, collecting the $5,000 reduces the receivable and increases cash; it does not recognize the same service revenue again. Paying $1,800 wages settles the November payable. Consuming another month of insurance reduces the prepaid asset by another $100. Recognize the advance as earned only when the assumed December service conditions are satisfied, not automatically because the calendar changed.

Preserve references from each settlement to its original transaction. Use the receivable workflow for collection allocation and the liability schedule for unpaid obligations. A balanced entry can still contain the wrong period or classification.

Use a cutoff review before reading the result

At month end, inspect accepted work, invoices issued before or after cutoff, services received but unbilled, unused prepayments and unfulfilled customer promises. Document the reporting basis and reviewer. Reconcile opening balances plus movements to closing schedules; do not replace missing evidence with an arbitrary journal to make profit look reasonable.

The accounting cycle connects adjustments to statements. Bookkeeping for small business covers the routine evidence collection, and the cash forecast handles future payment timing. IRS Publication 538 is the separate official reference for tax accounting methods; this guide makes no blanket eligibility or change-of-method claim.

Check recognition, billing and settlement against separate evidence

The OpenStax adjustment framework distinguishes accrued items from deferred items. Apply that distinction to the four case records: accepted work supports the revenue assessment; payroll evidence supports the wage accrual; the insurance contract supports the unused coverage; and the customer agreement supports the unearned advance. A bank statement establishes a receipt or payment, but does not establish that every recognition condition was satisfied.

For a real close, create one row per event with the performance or consumption date, invoice date, due date, settlement date and account reference. Explain any difference rather than forcing all five dates to match. If an invoice includes work still to be delivered, review the separate obligations before assigning the entire invoice to revenue. If an amount is disputed, identify whether the problem concerns the transaction price, the recognition conditions or collectibility; those are different questions.

The case’s $3,100 profit, $1,200 operating cash movement and $11,200 closing cash answer three defined questions. The owner’s $10,000 investment belongs in the funding bridge, not service revenue. Compare each reconciliation with the same reporting cutoff and included transactions. IRS Publication 538 is a separate federal tax-method reference; it does not turn this introductory financial-reporting case into a tax return.

Frequently asked questions

Does accrual accounting wait for the invoice?

Not necessarily. Recognition depends on the framework and transaction facts; an invoice date alone is insufficient.

Is an advance payment always revenue?

No. In this example it remains an obligation until the service is earned under the stated assumptions.

Does collecting a receivable increase revenue again?

No. It normally converts a previously recognized receivable into cash.

Does this determine which tax method I may use?

No. Tax eligibility and changes of method need a separate review of current official rules and your circumstances.

Can a customer invoice contain both earned and unearned amounts?

Yes. Assess the underlying delivery obligations and applicable recognition rules; the invoice total alone does not settle that question.

Which evidence supports the insurance adjustment?

The coverage period and amount in the insurance agreement, together with the payment and prior prepaid balance, support the allocation.
