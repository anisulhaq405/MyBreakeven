Operating Expenses: Examples, Formula and COGS Comparison

Operating expenses are the costs of supporting a business's operations under the reporting convention you use. This guide uses OPEX excluding cost of goods sold: selling and administrative expenses are deducted after gross profit. That boundary matters because some reports use “operating costs” more broadly to include both COGS and OPEX. Check the line definitions before comparing amounts.

For the original fictional monthly example below, net revenue is $50,000, COGS is $20,000 and OPEX is $18,000. Gross profit is $30,000; operating income is $12,000. The purpose is to classify each amount once and reconcile the result, rather than label every bank payment an expense. These USD figures are invented teaching inputs, not industry averages or tax guidance.

Operating expenses formula: define the subtotal first

OPEX = gross profit − operating income = net revenue − COGS − operating income. Use the formula only when the income statement has that same boundary. An independently reported operating charge outside your selected OPEX subtotal must be reconciled separately. Reversing a statement is not a substitute for checking its account classifications.

In the example, $50,000 − $20,000 − $12,000 = $18,000. Total included operating costs are $38,000: $20,000 COGS plus $18,000 OPEX. Calling the full $38,000 “OPEX” and then subtracting COGS again would reduce operating income to −$8,000 incorrectly. The operating profit margin calculator keeps COGS and OPEX separate in its statement-building mode.

Operating expenses list: classify the function and behavior separately

The same bill can have different classifications in different businesses. “Direct or indirect,” “COGS or period expense” and “fixed or variable” answer different questions. A factory's building depreciation may enter production cost, while head-office depreciation may be an administrative expense. Neither label proves that the amount is avoidable if one order is lost.

Assume a fictional business records the following six OPEX categories for one month. Administrative payroll is already loaded with the employer costs included in this example. The depreciation line is outside COGS. No category below is added again as another operating charge.

Reconcile $8,000 + $3,000 + $2,500 + $1,000 + $1,500 + $2,000 = $18,000. Then connect the schedule to the income statement: $50,000 net revenue − $20,000 COGS = $30,000 gross profit; $30,000 − $18,000 = $12,000 operating income. Operating margin is $12,000 ÷ $50,000 × 100 = 24%. A $1,000 non-operating gain is outside this operating subtotal and does not change its margin.

COGS, OPEX and capital expenditure: three different boundaries

For a reseller, purchasing inventory does not necessarily create COGS immediately: unsold items remain in inventory under the chosen accounting basis. COGS relates to the goods sold during the period. OPEX in this guide is the separate operating-period subtotal. A capitalized equipment purchase creates an asset, with subsequent depreciation recognized over its useful life according to the applicable policy.

Suppose the business pays $12,000 for office equipment and recognizes $2,000 depreciation this month under the explicitly assumed schedule. Do not add $12,000 equipment cash and $2,000 depreciation together into this month's OPEX merely because both relate to the same asset. The assumed $2,000 is part of the existing schedule, not an additional charge. Whether a purchase qualifies for capitalization and its actual depreciation schedule require the business's policy and supporting facts.

Likewise, a loan principal payment reduces debt; it is not OPEX. Loan interest is a separate non-operating expense in this example. The master budget connects operating results, asset purchases, debt and payment dates. A classification example does not establish a tax deduction or universal filing treatment.

Operating expense ratio: name what is in the numerator

OPEX ratio excluding COGS = $18,000 ÷ $50,000 = 36%. COGS is 40% of revenue, so the complete reconciliation is 40% COGS + 36% OPEX + 24% operating margin = 100%. A report using all operating costs in its numerator would instead show 76%. Both fractions can be calculated, but calling them the same metric would conceal a different cost scope.

The phrase “operating expense margin” is ambiguous. State the numerator instead of inventing a new profitability measure. Real-estate operating-expense ratios can use different income and expense conventions and are outside this trading-business example. At zero revenue the ratio is unavailable; zero revenue does not mean expenses are zero. Compare matching periods and consistent accounts before interpreting an increase.

A reclassification can change gross margin without changing operating income

Move $2,000 of a correctly identified operating cost from OPEX to COGS in a comparison with otherwise identical totals. COGS becomes $22,000, OPEX $16,000 and gross profit $28,000. Operating income remains $12,000 and operating margin stays 24%. Gross margin changes from 60% to 56%; the OPEX ratio changes from 36% to 32%. The business has not saved $2,000. Document justified policy changes and restate comparisons where appropriate.

This is why a low OPEX ratio alone does not establish efficiency. Review the cost boundary, recognition period and actual work performed. Do not move expenses between accounts simply to display a preferred ratio.

Close the expense schedule before changing the budget

  1. Match accounts to a documented COGS/OPEX/non-operating/capital classification.
  2. Check unpaid accrued services, prepayments and credits against the reporting period.
  3. Reconcile each schedule category to its supporting ledger and total the selected accounts.
  4. Check depreciation appears once and inventory cash payments are not substituted for COGS.
  5. Compare the approved budget with actual expenses at a suitable activity level.
  6. Record explanations and an owner for justified corrective actions.

For an operating expense budget, forecast the actual drivers and commitments rather than applying the same sales growth rate to every category. The flexible-budget guide distinguishes activity effects from spending differences. Frequently asked questions

Is COGS an operating expense?

In a broad operating-cost total it can be included. This guide uses OPEX excluding COGS and subtracts those two subtotals once each. Follow the actual statement labels.

Is depreciation an operating expense?

Operating depreciation belongs in the appropriate operating cost function. It may already be included in COGS; do not add it again as administrative OPEX.

Are interest and income tax operating expenses?

They are outside operating income in this nonfinancial-business illustration. Specialized reporting can differ, so check the actual presentation.

Are restructuring costs always non-operating?

No. An unusual or infrequent cost is not automatically outside operations. Check its nature and the applicable presentation; this calculator does not create an adjusted earnings figure.

Is rent fixed or an operating expense?

Those labels describe different dimensions. Office rent is administrative OPEX in the example and fixed within its lease range; production rent may be in manufacturing cost.

What about bad debt and SG&A?

SG&A is generally an operating-period subtotal under this presentation. Bad-debt treatment depends on the business and reporting policy; avoid deducting the same effect in both net revenue and expenses.
