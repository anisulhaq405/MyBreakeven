Gross Profit and Gross Margin: Formula and Reconciliation

Published November 17, 2026Updated November 17, 2026

Calculate gross profit and gross margin from net revenue and matched COGS. Reconcile a checked inventory case and separate fees, contribution and net income.

Gross profit is net revenue minus the cost of goods sold or cost of sales on the stated reporting basis. Gross margin is that gross profit divided by net revenue. Reconcile both inputs for the same period before interpreting the percentage, and keep selling costs, financing, tax and customer cash separate from the gross-profit subtotal.

All numerical cases are original fictional USD teaching examples, not market benchmarks, customer results, offers or forecasts. Replace each assumption with dated evidence.

State what your gross-profit subtotal includes

Start with the business, period and accounting policy. A retailer’s inventory purchases do not necessarily equal its cost of goods sold for the period, because some stock remains unsold. A service business needs a supported cost-of-sales policy for delivery labor and other included costs. Consistency matters when comparing periods or companies; the same label can conceal different cost classifications.

Use net revenue after the applicable sales deductions as the denominator, not gross billings or bank deposits. The net-revenue guide owns the sales reconciliation. This guide then subtracts the matched cost of sales and interprets the resulting subtotal. If you need to solve a selling price from a target margin, use the existing margin-versus-markup calculator rather than treating this explanatory page as another competing price tool.

Write down whether factory or delivery overhead is in cost of sales and whether payment fees are presented as selling expenses. Do not move a recurring cost below gross profit simply to make the percentage rise. A comparison is useful only after you understand the reporting boundary and any genuine change in the policy.

Build the net-revenue side of the example

A fictional retailer has $50,000 gross earned sales for a month, $2,000 sales discounts and $3,000 recognized customer returns. Assume these are complete, non-overlapping deductions under the stated case. Net revenue is $50,000 − $2,000 − $3,000 = $45,000. The return figure is the customer sales value, not the inventory cost of the goods coming back.

A goods return can have a separate stock-cost entry if returned items qualify for recovery. The closing inventory record in the next section already includes any supported recovered stock, so no second retail-price deduction is made from COGS. Damaged goods and refund obligations need their own evidence and applicable accounting treatment. A simple revenue reduction does not explain the entire physical inventory movement.

Reconcile credited sales with dated invoices, approved discounts and return records. Separate customer refunds from supplier purchase credits. Separate tax collected for a government under the stated agency arrangement from earned sales. Customer acquisition analysis and forecasts should begin with the same defined sales basis instead of switching between headline orders and collected amounts without explanation.

Reconcile inventory to cost of goods sold

The retailer begins with $18,000 inventory, records $26,000 purchases and $2,000 qualifying freight-in, and receives $1,000 supplier purchase credits. A supported closing count and cost valuation gives $14,000 ending inventory. Available cost is $18,000 + $26,000 + $2,000 − $1,000 = $45,000. Subtracting closing inventory leaves $31,000 COGS. The amounts are original assumptions, not an industry example copied from a source.

Investigate missing counts, duplicate supplier invoices, unsupported freight classification or receipts booked after the cut-off. A percentage produced from an unreconciled stock balance is not a reliable pricing signal. Keep physical units and monetary costs traceable, even when a summary statement displays only dollars.

Calculate gross profit, margin and the different markup base

Gross profit is $45,000 − $31,000 = $14,000. Gross margin is $14,000/$45,000 × 100 = 31.11% after presentation rounding. If someone instead divides the same $14,000 by $31,000, the result is 45.16%. That second ratio is markup on the defined COGS base, not gross margin. The profit numerator is the same; the denominator changes the question.

Use full precision for intermediate values and label the displayed rounding. Adding item-level gross-margin percentages without weighting their revenue does not reproduce the business margin. For a mixed range, combine net sales and matched costs first, or calculate a properly revenue-weighted result. A change in product mix can move gross margin even if no individual product price changed.

If net revenue is zero, a gross-margin percentage is not defined. A positive sales figure with COGS above sales gives negative gross profit and a negative gross margin. Do not turn an undefined ratio into zero, and do not suppress the loss because it looks unusual. Preserve dollar totals next to every ratio so a reader can see scale.

Show what gross profit has not paid for

Assume the $14,000 gross profit must also cover $1,350 payment fees, $4,000 selling payroll and $2,500 premises rent, all outside COGS under this particular case’s classification. Operating profit is $14,000 − $1,350 − $4,000 − $2,500 = $6,150. After a separately assumed $500 interest expense and $1,000 income-tax expense, net income is $4,650. The tax amount is a teaching input, not a tax-rate calculation or filing instruction.

These subtotals explain why a business can have a healthy-looking gross margin and modest final earnings. They do not establish what is in the bank: sales can remain uncollected, inventory purchases can precede sales, and loan principal or equipment payments can use cash without being the same period’s operating expense. The earnings-to-cash comparison follows those separate boundaries.

Gross margin is also not automatically contribution margin. Suppose $4,000 of the $31,000 COGS is fixed production overhead and the remaining $27,000 changes with the modeled activity. If the $1,350 payment fees are the only other variable costs, contribution is $45,000 − $27,000 − $1,350 = $16,650. This different subtotal supports a different decision; see contribution versus gross margin.

Turn the reconciliation into a useful operating review

When gross margin moves, compare net selling price, discounts, returns, stock costs and mix on a consistent period basis. Check a matched set of products or jobs before concluding that all prices need to increase. A supplier-credit timing issue, a stock write-down or an unusual returned order can explain a change without indicating a new sustainable margin.

For a proposed operating change, differential cost asks which future amounts actually change. For an added location, expansion planning adds capacity and cash timing. For a potential sale, valuation reviews normalized transferable earnings rather than applying a price multiple blindly to this month’s gross profit. The gross-profit bridge remains one controlled input to those decisions, not a substitute for their other records.

Frequently asked questions

What is the gross-profit formula?

Net revenue minus matched cost of goods sold or cost of sales, using a defined business, period and reporting basis.

What is the gross-margin formula?

Gross profit divided by net revenue, multiplied by 100 for a percentage. Net revenue must be nonzero for the ratio to be defined.

Are margin and markup the same?

No. Margin uses net revenue as its denominator; markup uses the stated cost base. A 31.11% margin in this example is a 45.16% markup on COGS.

Do purchases equal COGS?

Not necessarily. Opening inventory, purchases and qualifying adjustments must reconcile with ending inventory and supported goods outflows.

Does gross profit equal bank cash?

No. Collection dates, inventory payments, financing and capital spending can separate the income-statement subtotal from cash movements.

Is gross margin the same as contribution margin?

No. Gross margin follows the cost-of-sales reporting boundary; contribution separates variable costs under the relevant operating model.
