Quick answer

Customer profitability analysis compares a customer’s net revenue with the direct and shared costs of serving that customer during the same period. Show the defined service subtotal and allocated profit separately before deciding whether a relationship should change.

Read the source-backed application and limits. Worked business amounts are fictional teaching examples.

Every amount in the worked case is an original fictional USD planning assumption. It is not an industry benchmark, a customer result or a prediction.

Define the period and the cost boundary

Start with a customer-month, quarter or year. Use earned revenue after discounts, credits and returns for that period. Keep collections in a separate dated view: an unpaid invoice can create accounting revenue without available cash. Define whether your result includes acquisition spending, account support, general overhead and owner work.

Defined customer service subtotal = net revenue − directly traceable delivery and service costs. Then subtract assigned shared service costs to obtain the specifically defined allocated result. Label the boundary; do not call a partially costed margin company net income. A customer’s lifetime value uses a different horizon and needs retention assumptions.

The support pool is $2,400 divided by 60 practical support hours, giving $40 per hour. General overhead is allocated by revenue only to reconcile the remaining $1,800. Its allocation is a reporting choice, not evidence that the cash disappears when a customer leaves. Total revenue of $12,000 less $5,600 delivery, $2,400 support and $1,800 overhead equals $2,200.

Choose drivers that explain actual work

Use order lines for processing, support time for assistance, shipments for dispatch and documented collection effort for chasing overdue balances. Do not allocate every pool by sales simply because sales is available. Record the pool amount, source ledger, included activity, denominator and each customer’s usage. The sum of allocations must equal the amount assigned; idle capacity or unassigned overhead should remain visible.

For acquisition cost, choose and disclose a treatment. Expensing all campaign spend in the acquisition month may be useful for that month’s result; spreading it over an assumed relationship produces a different managerial measure. Neither choice should silently mix lifetime acquisition cost with one month’s revenue.

Test an action instead of automatically dropping the customer

Customer C looks loss-making after allocation. Suppose a standard-order policy removes ten support hours. At the chosen $40 rate, the ledger shows $400 less assigned support and a zero customer subtotal after assigned support. But if salaried staff are still paid, near-term cash does not improve by $400. Instead, released hours may let the same team serve better work.

Before repricing or exiting, list contract commitments, collection risk, rework, spare capacity and costs genuinely avoided. Compare a scoped service level, paid rush requests, minimum order or staged price change with the current account. A negative allocated result is a reason to investigate, not an automatic customer termination rule.

Build a monthly account review that can be audited

  1. Reconcile customer net revenue to the sales ledger.
  2. Trace delivery, refunds and customer-specific effort from source records.
  3. Allocate only identified service pools using documented drivers.
  4. Show unassigned overhead and reconcile the complete business result.
  5. Choose an action, name an owner and review the next period’s actuals.

Use the agency client profitability guide for a service-account application, and the cleaning contract profit calculator for a contract-specific cost boundary. Check those records against the broader contribution income statement.

Test the incremental effect before removing an account

The customer ledger above is a management allocation, not a standard contribution-margin income statement. A conventional contribution margin subtracts variable costs from sales; a traceable customer cost can be fixed, and a support allocation can remain payable after the customer leaves. The OpenStax relevant-cost framework supports comparing future amounts that actually differ between alternatives.

Extend Customer C with another explicitly fictional assumption: its $1,200 direct delivery cost disappears if the account ends, but all support salaries and general overhead remain. Removing C loses $2,000 revenue and avoids only $1,200 cost. The business result falls by $800, from $2,200 to $1,400, before any replacement work. The account’s negative $700 allocated result therefore does not mean closure saves $700.

Now test released capacity separately. If the team can sell replacement work, list its achievable revenue, additional materials, delivery hours and collection date. Deduct transition costs or contract penalties where supported. Compare that complete alternative with retaining C; do not count both a salary saving and revenue from the same retained employee without explaining the staffing arrangement.

Keep three columns in the decision record: amount assigned in the reporting ledger, cash actually avoided by the decision, and capacity released. Ask the delivery manager to confirm each assumption and reconcile the chosen action to the next month’s actual cost and customer service evidence.

Frequently asked questions

Is a high-revenue customer always profitable?

No. Discounts, delivery effort, exceptions, support and collection work can consume the apparent margin. Compare consistently costed results for the same period.

Should shared overhead determine whether to drop a customer?

Not by itself. Test avoidable costs and released capacity; allocated overhead may remain after the account leaves.

Which cost driver should I use?

Choose a documented measure that explains the activity, such as support hours or shipments. Reconcile the allocated pool and show any unassigned cost.

Can profit be positive while collections are late?

Yes. Accrual revenue and the defined customer service subtotal do not establish available cash. Track invoice due dates and receipts separately.

Why can dropping a customer reduce business profit despite a negative allocated result?

The customer may still cover costs that remain payable after departure. In the added fictional case, removing $2,000 revenue avoids only $1,200 delivery cost, reducing the business result by $800.

Is a directly traceable cost automatically variable?

No. Traceability identifies the customer or activity; cost behavior describes how the amount changes with activity or a decision. A dedicated salary can be traceable and fixed.
