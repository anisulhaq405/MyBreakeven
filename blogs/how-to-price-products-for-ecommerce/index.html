How to Price Products for Your Online Store

Published September 17, 2026 · Updated September 25, 2026

Learn how to price ecommerce products from landed cost, fees, shipping, returns, and profit goals with worked examples and a free calculator.

If you sell products online, start with your cost per order, not a competitor's sticker price. The right price covers product and fulfilment costs, payment fees, returns and marketing per order. Across a realistic number of orders, the remaining contribution must cover monthly overhead, owner pay and a profit target. A price that clears supplier cost alone can still fail this test.

Quick answer: Add order-level costs and choose a contribution target. If non-payment variable costs are $30.75, the illustrative payment fee is 2.9% plus $0.30 and you want a 40% contribution margin, the price is about $54.38: ($30.75 + $0.30) ÷ (1 − 0.029 − 0.40). Check demand and the orders you can fulfil.

How to price an ecommerce product from its order costs

Use this sequence: add landed cost, packaging, pick-and-pack labour, shipping subsidy, returns, order-level marketing, and payment fees. Set a contribution margin target, then check the price against customer demand and the orders your store can fulfil.

Percentage fees make a simple markup unreliable. All USD figures below are illustrative assumptions, not provider quotes or market averages. Check your own rates and order records. Suppose an order has these costs:

  • Product landed cost: $18.00
  • Packaging: $1.25
  • Pick-and-pack labour: $2.00
  • Shipping subsidy: $3.50
  • Returns allowance: $1.00
  • Advertising cost per order: $5.00
  • Payment fee: 2.9% of the selling price plus $0.30

The costs other than payment processing total $18 + $1.25 + $2 + $3.50 + $1 + $5 = $30.75. If the target contribution margin is 40%, let the selling price be P. The contribution is P − ($30.75 + 0.029P + $0.30), or 0.971P − $31.05. Set that equal to 40% of the price:

0.971P − $31.05 = 0.40P

0.571P = $31.05

P = $54.38

At $54.38, the payment fee is $1.88 after rounding, total variable cost is $32.63, and contribution is $21.75. Divide $21.75 by $54.38 and the contribution margin is 40.0% after rounding. The direct pricing formula is (non-percentage order costs + flat payment fee) ÷ (1 − percentage payment fee − target contribution margin). Here it is ($30.75 + $0.30) ÷ (1 − 0.029 − 0.40) = $54.38.

That is a price for a 40% contribution target. If competitors charge $39, compare shipping, product cost and advertising before matching them.

Next, calculate the orders needed. If monthly fixed costs are $6,000 and you want $2,000 for owner pay plus $1,000 in profit, the store needs $9,000 of contribution. At $21.75 per order, that is 9,000 ÷ 21.75 = 413.79, so you need 414 orders. At $54.38 each, those orders produce $54.38 × 414 = $22,513.32 in revenue. Contribution, not revenue, covers fixed bills and target pay.

What changes the answer

Landed product cost: include supplier price, inbound freight, duties and preparation before sale.

Shipping terms: include carrier cost even with free shipping. If buyers pay shipping, include both that revenue and the carrier cost.

Fees and acquisition: include percentage and flat payment fees, marketplace commissions and advertising per acquired order.

Returns and discounts: estimate unrecovered refund, return-shipping and replacement costs. Recalculate at the discounted price before a promotion.

Order mix and capacity: a $20 accessory can use almost as much packing time as a $100 bundle. Compare contribution dollars and required order volume by product.

Three worked ecommerce pricing examples

Scenario 1: A low-priced accessory

A phone accessory has a landed cost of $8.00. Packaging is $0.75, fulfilment is $1.50, the average shipping subsidy is $2.75, returns allowance is $0.50, and advertising costs $3.50 per order. The non-payment variable costs total $8 + 0.75 + 1.50 + 2.75 + 0.50 + 3.50 = $17.00.

At a selling price of $29.00, payment processing is ($29 × 0.029) + $0.30 = $1.14 after rounding. Total variable cost is $17.00 + $1.14 = $18.14, so contribution is $29.00 − $18.14 = $10.86. The contribution margin is $10.86 ÷ $29.00 = 37.4%.

If monthly fixed costs are $3,000, owner pay is $2,000, and the profit target is $1,000, required contribution is $6,000. The store needs 6,000 ÷ 10.86 = 552.49, or 553 orders. That means about 553 ÷ 26 = 21.3 orders per selling day on a 26-day month. Being above product cost is not enough; required daily volume is the test. If the price falls to $25 with the same order costs, the fee is $1.03 after rounding, contribution is $25 − $17 − $1.03 = $6.97, and the same $6,000 target takes 6,000 ÷ 6.97 = 860.83, or 861 orders. The $4 discount therefore requires 308 more orders than the $29 price, assuming demand and costs otherwise stay the same.

Scenario 2: A higher-priced bundle

A skincare bundle costs $42.00 landed. Packaging is $1.50, fulfilment is $3.00, shipping subsidy is $6.00, returns allowance is $2.00, and advertising is $8.00 per order. Those costs total $42 + $1.50 + $3 + $6 + $2 + $8 = $62.50 before payment processing.

At $119.00, the payment fee is ($119 × 0.029) + $0.30 = $3.75 after rounding. Total variable cost is $62.50 + $3.75 = $66.25; contribution is $119 − $66.25 = $52.75, and contribution margin is $52.75 ÷ $119 = 44.3%.

With $9,000 in monthly fixed costs, $3,500 of owner pay, and a $2,500 profit target, required contribution is $15,000. The order requirement is 15,000 ÷ 52.75 = 284.36, so it needs 285 orders. Revenue at that volume is $119 × 285 = $33,915. If the bundle were priced at $99, the payment fee would be $3.17, contribution would be $99 − ($62.50 + $3.17) = $33.33, and the same target would require 15,000 ÷ 33.33 = 450.05, or 451 orders. The $20 cut adds 166 monthly orders.

Scenario 3: A subscription-style replenishment product

A replenishment box costs $16.00 landed. Packaging is $1.00, fulfilment is $2.25, shipping is $4.25, expected refunds and replacements are $1.25, and retention marketing allocated to each renewal is $2.00. The non-payment total is $16 + $1 + $2.25 + $4.25 + $1.25 + $2 = $26.75.

At $59.00, the payment fee is ($59 × 0.029) + $0.30 = $2.01 after rounding. Total variable cost is $28.76; contribution is $30.24, and contribution margin is $30.24 ÷ $59 = 51.3%. If fixed costs and target owner pay together require $8,000 of monthly contribution, required renewals are 8,000 ÷ 30.24 = 264.55, or 265 renewals.

If 10% of billed subscriptions fail or cancel before renewal, 265 successful renewals require roughly 265 ÷ 0.90 = 294.45, so plan for at least 295 renewal opportunities to obtain 265 successful renewals under that assumed 90% success rate. That does not tell you how many new customers to acquire: also measure cancellations, failed payments, and first-order conversions separately.

Common mistakes

Using supplier cost as the whole variable cost. The missing lines are usually fulfilment, shipping, fees, returns, and advertising. Add them per order before you decide that a product has a healthy margin.

Applying a markup to a price that includes percentage fees. A 50% markup on cost does not mean a 50% contribution margin. Percentage fees rise with the price, so solve for the price after including them.

Treating free shipping as free. The customer may see $0 at checkout, but the carrier still invoices you. Put the average shipping subsidy in the order cost.

Ignoring the discount version of the offer. A product can work at $60 and fail at $45 during a promotion. Recalculate contribution at the actual sale price, then check whether the extra volume would really cover the lost dollars per order.

Blending first orders and repeat orders. Acquisition cost is often concentrated on the first purchase. If repeat buyers cost less to acquire, calculate first-order contribution separately and avoid using a blended average that hides an unprofitable entry offer.

Pricing every product with one percentage markup. Bulky, fragile, low-priced, and high-return products consume different resources. Price by order economics instead.

Key takeaways

  • Build the price from landed cost and every cost that rises with an order, including shipping, returns and acquisition.
  • Solve percentage fees and your contribution target together; a markup on supplier cost does not give the same margin.
  • Test the required whole-order volume at full price and at every proposed promotion price.
  • Use your own payment terms, order mix and capacity; the worked figures are illustrative assumptions.
