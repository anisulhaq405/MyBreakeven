Financial Contingency Plan: Cash Triggers, Actions and Funding

Quick answer

A financial contingency plan defines what the business will do when cash assumptions fail: the trigger, decision owner, dated response and funding actually available. It complements the cash forecast and operational continuity plan; it is not a promise that emergency finance will be approved.

Read the source-backed application and limits. Worked business amounts are fictional teaching examples.

Every amount in the worked case is an original fictional USD planning assumption. It is not an industry benchmark, a customer result or a prediction.

Connect a risk to a date and an accountable action

Choose a planning horizon that captures your payment commitments. List opening usable cash, expected receipts and committed payments by week or date. Separate restricted balances, unapproved borrowing and expected insurance recoveries from cash available to pay bills. Identify which receipts depend on customer acceptance, processing delays or unresolved disputes.

For each risk, record the event, a measurable trigger, the owner, the response deadline, the action’s cash effect and the dependency that could prevent it. A reserve target should follow the business’s downside timing and tolerance, not an unexplained percentage copied from another company.

Use a chosen $5,000 minimum cash floor for this case. The base case stays $1,000 above it. The customer-delay case needs $5,000 of timely improvement to restore the floor, and the combined downside needs $7,500. These are aggregate four-week checks; a payroll payment before the week-four receipt may create an earlier, larger need. The dated worksheet must test that ordering.

Deferring a payment shifts the obligation into a later period; it does not eliminate the cost. Add the new payment date and debt service to later weeks. Avoid showing credit as ordinary revenue. If the lender can suspend the line or the business fails its conditions, the draw must not appear as dependable cash.

Write an escalation ladder with a decision owner

An illustrative green level is forecast cash at or above the chosen floor, with a weekly review. Amber begins when a dated forecast falls below that floor or a material receipt slips: the finance owner confirms collection status and pauses uncommitted discretionary spending. Red is an inability to meet a critical due payment: the owner escalates immediately, verifies financing and contacts the affected counterparties before making unsupported promises.

The numbers and color levels belong to this fictional case. In a real plan, set them around payroll, critical suppliers, legal commitments, funding terms and operational resilience. Do not advise missing tax or wage obligations as a routine liquidity tactic.

Rehearse the plan and reconcile it after each review

Test a delayed receipt, a lost customer, an equipment failure and a funding source that is unavailable at the same time. Keep source evidence for cash, counterparties, approvals and facility conditions. Check after the event whether the action occurred on time and whether a temporary fix created another shortage.

Use the 13-week cash forecast for dated payments, the cash-flow recovery guide for causes and responses, and your business plan for the assumptions and owners. The broader business continuity task also covers staffing, systems, premises and recovery priorities; this article owns the financial response.

Turn the four-week closing balance into a dated funding test

The SBA emergency-preparedness material covers risk assessment, a business-specific response plan and practice. It supports preparation; it does not establish this example’s $5,000 cash floor or promise financing. Those amounts must follow the business’s actual commitments and decision policy.

A positive month-end balance can conceal an earlier shortage. Extend the fictional base with dated movements: begin at $18,000, pay $12,000 in week one, then $8,000 in week two, and receive $8,000 in week four. Closing cash is still $6,000, but week-two cash reaches −$2,000. Restoring the selected $5,000 floor before that payment requires $7,000 of timely additional cash or agreed payment changes, rather than the $1,000 month-end buffer suggested by the aggregate base.

Apply the same timing test to each response. If a $4,000 credit draw can arrive only in week three, it does not solve the week-two payment. If a supplier agrees to defer $1,500, record the replacement due date and include it in the next forecast period. Distinguish removing a genuinely uncommitted purchase from merely postponing an existing obligation.

Keep the last evidence date beside every available-funding line. Check draw conditions, approvals and settlement delays before treating it as usable. Assign an alternative action if any condition fails, and rehearse the decision before the critical payment date. Disaster assistance, insurance claims and hoped-for receivable collections remain conditional until their amount and timing are supported.

Frequently asked questions

How large should the emergency reserve be?

Base it on dated downside cash needs, commitments, available financing and risk tolerance. The illustrative $5,000 floor is not a universal recommendation.

Can expected insurance or a new loan be counted as available cash?

Only with a supportable amount and timing, and after relevant conditions are met. Pending approval is not immediately available cash.

Does rescheduling a supplier bill remove the cost?

No. It moves the cash obligation to another date, subject to agreement. Include that later payment when checking the next forecast periods.

How often should the plan be reviewed?

Choose a cadence that matches the payment risk, and review immediately when a material assumption fails. The example uses more frequent escalation under stress.

Can month-end positive cash hide an earlier negative balance?

Yes. The added fictional sequence closes at $6,000 but reaches −$2,000 in week two before its week-four receipt.

Does a later credit draw fix an earlier missed payment automatically?

No. Cash must be usable by the payment deadline, and any draw conditions or settlement delays must be considered.
