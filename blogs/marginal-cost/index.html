Marginal Cost: Calculate Added Output and Capacity Steps

Calculate marginal cost from matching changes in cost and output. Reconcile a fictional batch example, a capacity step and an additional-order decision.

Marginal cost measures the change in a defined total cost divided by the change in output. State the interval: a batch average can differ sharply from the cost of the very next unit when a capacity commitment is triggered.

All amounts and operating results below are original fictional teaching examples in USD. They are not customer results, industry averages or forecasts. Use your own dated records and applicable accounting policies.

Define both changes before dividing

OpenStax distinguishes marginal cost from average cost: one compares changes in cost and output; the other spreads a defined total across output. For a finite interval, marginal cost = (new total cost − original total cost) / (new quantity − original quantity). Use quantities of the same product, quality and period. If output does not change, this quotient is undefined rather than zero.

Write the decision beside the calculation. Increasing monthly production from 200 to 250 comparable units is different from replacing a machine or discontinuing a service. A historical cost comparison can describe what happened without identifying a causal cost function. Changes in input price, product mix or specifications must be explained before attributing the entire difference to added output.

Build an explicit fictional production-cost schedule

The original USD case assumes one homogeneous product and a single month. Up to 300 units, included production cost is $4,800 committed period cost plus $18 for each unit. Producing any amount above 300 and up to 360 requires an additional $1,200 capacity commitment for that month. The $18 unit rate is unchanged. These are constructed teaching assumptions, not supplier quotes or an industry cost benchmark.

At 200 units, total cost is $4,800 + $3,600 = $8,400. At 300, it is $10,200. At 320, it becomes $4,800 + $1,200 + $5,760 = $11,760. The 300-to-320 increase therefore costs $1,560, not simply 20 × $18.

Separate an interval average from the next unit

For 300 to 320 units, $1,560 / 20 = $78 per additional unit on average across that batch. The 301st unit alone triggers the full $1,200 capacity payment plus $18 unit cost: its modeled incremental cost is $1,218. Units 302 through 320 then add $18 each, once the same capacity has been obtained. Twenty units total $1,218 + 19 × $18 = $1,560.

Calling $78 the cost of every individual added unit would hide the step. Conversely, treating $1,218 as the repeating cost of units 302 onward would charge for the same commitment repeatedly. Compare the actual feasible batch with its alternative, and record whether the capacity commitment has already been made. This piecewise schedule does not have a smooth derivative at the threshold.

Ask which commitments the decision really changes

ACCA’s relevant-cost guidance distinguishes unchanged existing allocations from additional commitments caused by a decision. In this case, the original $4,800 is payable under either monthly output alternative. It cancels from the comparison. The new $1,200 does not cancel when comparing 300 units with a proposal that requires capacity above 300.

After that $1,200 has become unavoidable for the stated month, a later decision within the obtained 360-unit limit has a different starting position. Do not silently use that later position to approve the original capacity purchase. Also do not assume capacity can be cancelled, renewed or reused on favorable terms without evidence. A fixed cost can be unchanged for one decision and avoidable for another horizon.

Test the whole additional order, not one attractive unit rate

Assume a separately accepted offer pays $65 for each added unit and initially adds no other selling or fulfillment cost. A 20-unit order above the 300-unit baseline yields $1,300 additional revenue against $1,560 additional production cost: the difference is −$260. Comparing $65 with the existing 300-unit average of $34 would incorrectly approve this production-only case.

A 60-unit order using the same new capacity yields $3,900 additional revenue. Cost rises from $10,200 at 300 to $12,480 at 360, an increase of $2,280; the difference is $1,620 before omitted order costs. If accepting it displaces another order with $300 otherwise obtainable contribution, the incremental advantage falls to $1,320 before those other costs. That opportunity effect is a decision comparison, not a second supplier invoice.

Check the information the quotient cannot supply

Confirm the delivery deadline, staffing, usable capacity, material availability and accepted specifications. At 361 units this model supplies no cost estimate; extending its last $18 rate beyond the 360-unit limit would invent evidence. A new shift, overtime rate or subcontractor quote needs a new alternative and cost schedule. Separate the purchase date from consumption, expense recognition and payment timing when converting the case into accounting or cash records.

The order example deliberately excludes financing, tax and any additional marketing, warranty, packing or delivery costs. Add actual decision-changing amounts before approval. A positive added-order difference does not show that the entire business earns a profit, pays every bill on time or should buy a long-lived asset. Neither market demand nor a sustainable selling price comes from dividing a cost change by units.

Preserve an auditable incremental-cost record

Record both output alternatives, the matched period, cost categories, capacity threshold, existing commitments and new commitments. Reconcile the beginning and ending cost totals to their underlying assumptions. Retain a dated quote or operating record for each material change, an owner for unresolved items and a decision deadline. Compare actual added cost and accepted output after delivery without rewriting the original approval assumptions.

Use average cost per unit for the total spread across output, cost control for investigating actual price and usage changes, and fixed versus variable costs for classification. The CVP guide tests a stated operating range, while growth scenarios connect extra demand to resources. Keep the worksheet’s incremental decision separate from the full-period result.

Frequently asked questions

Is marginal cost always total cost divided by units?

No. That is average cost for a defined total. Marginal cost uses the cost change divided by the output change.

Is the $78 batch cost the cost of the 301st unit?

No. The fictional 301st unit triggers $1,218. The $78 figure averages $1,560 over the full additional 20 units.

Are fixed costs always excluded?

No. Unchanged commitments cancel from a decision comparison, but a new avoidable capacity commitment caused by the order must be included.

Can the formula be used when output change is zero?

The finite-change quotient is undefined when the denominator is zero. Investigate the cost change on its own basis.

Does a price above historical average cost guarantee a useful order?

No. Check the actual incremental batch cost, new capacity, other order costs and any displaced alternative.

Can I use the last unit rate beyond the capacity limit?

Not without evidence. The example stops at 360 units; a higher output requires a supported new cost alternative.
