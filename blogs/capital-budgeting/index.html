Capital Budgeting: Compare Business Investment Projects

Capital budgeting evaluates long-term uses of business cash, such as equipment or expansion. Build incremental project cash flows, discount them at a justified rate and compare value, recovery time, funding limits and execution risk before choosing an alternative.

All numerical examples are original fictional teaching cases in USD, not market benchmarks or promised outcomes. Assumptions are stated beside the calculations; replace them with your own dated evidence.

Compare the project with a credible do-nothing alternative

Define the decision first: replace equipment, add capacity, outsource or continue with the current setup. Count the cash changes caused by the decision. Include installation, training, incremental maintenance, working capital, disposal costs and forgone alternatives where relevant. A sunk feasibility study paid last month is not a new cash outflow caused by today’s choice.

A forecast needs dates as well as amounts. Set a common evaluation horizon or handle different lives explicitly. State whether flows are nominal or real, before or after tax and available to the firm or only equity owners. Match the discount rate to that basis. Depreciation is not itself a cash payment, although tax consequences may change cash flows in a fully specified model.

Compare two mutually exclusive equipment projects

A fictional workshop can select one of two machines or keep its existing setup. Project A requires $24,000 now and provides $10,000 net cash savings at each of the next three year-ends. Project B requires $18,000 now and provides $7,200 at those same dates. The savings are incremental cash after operating costs. For this deliberately simplified, no-tax case, assume no additional working capital, salvage value, financing cash flows or later maintenance outlays. The 8% discount rate is a stated illustrative assumption, not a current market estimate.

NPV = initial signed cash flow + Σ[year-t cash flow / (1 + rate)^t]. At 8%, the three-payment present-value factor is 2.577096987. A’s inflows have present value $25,770.97, leaving NPV $1,770.97. B’s inflows have present value $18,555.10, leaving NPV $555.10. A produces $1,215.87 more modeled value, provided the extra funding and operational requirements can be met.

Resolve value and affordability separately

Simple payback is 2.4 years for A and 2.5 for B if each year’s savings accrue evenly within the year. If savings arrive only at year-end, both first recover their initial outlay at the end of year 3. State which timing interpretation is being reported; do not present a fractional recovery date with a year-end-only cash assumption.

If the available project budget is $20,000 with no additional financing, A is infeasible despite its higher NPV. B fits the limit, but accepting it still depends on the credibility of the savings and the cash reserve the business needs outside the project. An indivisible $24,000 machine cannot be funded by allocating 83% of its forecast cost and assuming the full savings.

Do not deduct interest in an unlevered project cash flow and also use a financing-inclusive discount rate without reconciling the framework. WACC describes a firm-capital benchmark; a project with different risk may need a different justified rate.

This is a useful supplier-and-operations question: what observable throughput, labor reduction or defect improvement supports that saving?

Keep an approval and post-investment review record

Retain competing quotes, implementation milestones, cash-flow sources, discount-rate rationale and sensitivity cases. Assign owners for installation timing and operating benefits. If the claim is labor savings, distinguish cash payroll actually avoided from hours released for other work; spare hours are not automatically cash inflow.

Set a review date after commissioning to compare actual incremental cash with the approved forecast. Explain variance from volume, price, downtime and maintenance rather than changing the original forecast retrospectively. A positive modeled NPV is conditional evidence, not a promise of realized return or a reason to disregard liquidity and execution risks.

Frequently asked questions

What is capital budgeting?

It is the process of evaluating and choosing long-term business investments using forecast cash flows, required return, funding and implementation considerations.

Why use cash flows rather than accounting profit?

Project value depends on the timing of cash committed and recovered. Accounting allocations such as depreciation need reconciliation to the actual cash and tax effects.

Which project has the higher NPV in the example?

At the assumed 8% rate and three-year horizon, A has $1,770.97 NPV and B has $555.10. A needs $6,000 more upfront funding.

Why might the business choose B?

If the project budget is strictly $20,000 with no further funding, A is not feasible. B still requires a credible savings case and adequate remaining liquidity.

Does simple payback replace NPV?

No. Payback highlights cash-recovery timing but ignores time value and cash after recovery. NPV evaluates the full specified cash schedule at a required return.

Can I use company WACC for every project?

Not automatically. The discount rate must fit project risk, currency, tax basis, timing and the type of cash flow being discounted.
