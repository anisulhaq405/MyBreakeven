Cash Flow Problems: Causes, Solutions and a Recovery Plan

A cash flow problem occurs when available cash on the required dates cannot cover payments you must make. A profitable month can still contain a payroll shortfall. Cash flow management means maintaining reliable records, forecasting collection and payment dates, assigning corrective actions and checking whether those actions actually worked.

Start with the next uncovered obligation, its date and the evidence behind expected receipts. Do not begin with “increase sales” or count an unapproved loan as available cash. This guide uses an original four-week USD example where a business ends the month with $7,000 yet falls to −$2,000 in week two. The remedy is tested against the timing gap and a chosen $3,000 operating floor, not a universal reserve rule.

Find the first shortfall before choosing a solution

  1. Reconcile usable opening cash. Check cleared balances, transfers, restricted funds and any amounts unavailable for operating payments. Keep an undrawn facility or loan application separate from cash already received.
  2. List the next dated obligations. Include payroll, supplier settlements, tax, debt service, owner withdrawals and committed purchases. Record the actual due date and whether a change is agreed.
  3. Review receipt evidence. An invoice issued, a sale booked, a payment promised and a cleared receipt are different stages. Separate confirmed, expected and uncertain collections.
  4. Calculate the lowest balance. Compare every dated step with zero and with your chosen cash floor. Check daily ordering when a weekly subtotal is close.
  5. Assign the response. Give each action an owner, deadline, evidence needed and fallback if it fails.

Warning signs include repeated unpaid invoices, a rising overdue supplier list, emergency borrowing for routine payments, inventory that cannot be sold promptly, and a forecast that changes without explanations. These are investigation triggers, not proof that the company has failed. Begin with source records so a missing bank entry is not mistaken for a commercial problem.

A temporary timing gap has an identifiable receipt or event that could close it. A structural gap persists even after realistic collections and payment dates are used because prices, costs or fixed commitments do not support operations. Many businesses have both. Separate them before accepting a funding proposal as the whole solution.

Test a recovery plan without double-counting money

Consider two explicit changes: collect $4,000 in week two instead of week three, after confirming the customer’s payment, and move the $4,000 discretionary equipment payment from week two to week three if operationally and contractually feasible. These are timing changes. Neither creates extra sales or permanently saves the purchase cost.

The owner field creates responsibility; it does not prove an action was completed. Keep a status such as proposed, agreed, cleared or failed. If obligations cannot be met, obtain timely professional help on the actual business circumstances rather than silently treating overdue liabilities as free funding.

Build a weekly cash flow management routine

Close the completed week. Compare the forecast closing balance with reconciled actual cash on the same account basis. Separate timing, amount, missing-event and classification differences. Preserve the earlier forecast so changes remain explainable.

Roll the horizon forward. Update the remaining 13 weeks with outstanding receipts, actual supplier terms, payroll and tax dates, committed spending and documented funding conditions. Maintain a downside case for delayed collections. A facility limit is not the same as funds available at the needed time.

Review the drivers. Track overdue receivables, aged stock, near-term obligations, contribution by offer, lowest cash balance and forecast accuracy. Use comparisons consistent with seasonality and scope; avoid inventing a universal reserve, ratio or “safe” number of negative months.

Record a decision. Approve the action, owner, deadline and fallback. At the next review, compare the claimed saving or accelerated receipt with what cleared. When growth or pricing is the real problem

Suppose a job sells for $500 but its relevant product, delivery, fees and acquisition costs total $520. The contribution is −$20 before monthly overhead. Ten extra jobs add $200 to that shortfall even if customers eventually pay in full. Faster collection changes timing; it does not make the job contribution positive. Recheck the cost scope, price and capacity before scaling the offer.

A positive-contribution offer can still require funding when inventory and labor are paid before customers settle. Test deposits, milestones and order staging against customer acceptance, refund obligations and delivery capacity. A deposit can improve near-term cash but creates work or refund obligations; it is not automatically earned revenue. Frequently asked questions

Can a business have cash flow problems while making a profit?

Yes. Profit recognition and cash collection occur at different times. Stock purchases, receivables, capital spending and debt payments can create cash shortfalls even when a period shows profit.

What should I do first if cash is short?

Reconcile available cash, list imminent obligations and verify expected receipt dates. Calculate the first uncovered payment and assign evidence-based actions with a fallback rather than relying on an unapproved loan or unconfirmed receipt.

Does more sales revenue always solve a cash shortage?

No. Negative-contribution sales worsen the economic gap, and growth can require inventory or payroll cash before collection. Check price, costs, timing and capacity separately.

How much cash reserve should every small business hold?

There is no single reserve suited to every business. Choose and document a floor based on actual obligations, volatility and access to funding, then stress-test payment dates. The example’s $3,000 is an assumption, not a benchmark.

Can I simply delay supplier, payroll or tax payments?

Do not assume a delay is permitted. Check obligations, terms and consequences, discuss feasible changes early and obtain appropriate professional advice when mandatory payments cannot be met. The worked deferral is a discretionary purchase under an explicit feasibility assumption.
