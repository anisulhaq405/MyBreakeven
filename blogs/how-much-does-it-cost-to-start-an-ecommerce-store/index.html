Ecommerce Startup Costs: New Store or Online Extension?

Published September 18, 2026Updated October 6, 2026

Published by MyBreakeven. Editorial standards and corrections. Report a calculation or content issue to support@mybreakeven.com.

Budget ecommerce startup costs for setup, inventory and a slower sales ramp. Compare a new store with an online extension and check fulfillment capacity.

E-commerceStartup CostsBreak-Even Planning

Starting an ecommerce store needs a budget for setup purchases, the extra cost of fulfilling orders and cash for the first slow months. An existing physical shop can have a smaller additional cash requirement than a brand-new business because some stock, equipment and overhead are already in place.

Our lean online-extension example needs $750 of one-time setup spending plus $765 to cover the deepest modeled operating shortfall: $1,515 before an additional chosen contingency. These are fictional USD assumptions, not a typical launch price or a complete quote for your store.

Quick answer

Separate one-time launch spending from recurring overhead and costs per completed order. For an existing shop, identify which resources are already paid for and which online costs are genuinely additional. Forecast the early sales ramp and cumulative shortfall. Keep stock purchases and payment dates in a cash schedule rather than treating operating break-even as the entire startup budget.

Make two budgets before buying anything

The first budget asks what cash the launch needs. It includes actual new purchases, deposits, setup work and payment dates. The second asks whether completed orders can cover recurring expenses and your chosen owner compensation.

You need both. A cheap platform setup can lead to an expensive fulfillment operation. Conversely, a business can have positive contribution on every order while needing cash to pay for stock long before customers buy it.

The SBA startup-cost guidance recommends identifying startup expenses and distinguishing one-time and ongoing costs. The numbers below are independent illustrative budgets. Replace each amount with your own quote, account record or explicit assumption.

Keep a note beside every line showing when it is paid and what it covers. “Marketing $500” is ambiguous: does that buy setup work, monthly advertising or acquisition across a defined number of orders? Assign it to one treatment so the same spending is not entered three times.

An existing shop adding online orders

Suppose a shop already owns $2,000 of suitable stock and has a computer and packing bench. The following launch budget includes only its genuinely new online setup purchases:

New one-time purchase Cash amount
Store setup work $300
Product photography for the launch range $250
Small shipping-label printer $200
Total new setup spending $750

The existing stock is not another $2,000 launch cash payment. However, products sold online still have a cost and must eventually be replenished. Being already owned does not make that stock free in the order-profit calculation.

The example assumes replacement stock and packing materials are paid for as orders ship. That makes the operating shortfall an approximate cash requirement under these stated dates. If you instead buy a large stock batch or packing supply upfront, build the actual cash schedule and remove duplicated payments from the ramp calculation.

Monthly additional overhead is $400 for the online extension. Existing shop costs stay in the original shop budget for this incremental view. A separate whole-business check should include the full rent, committed payroll and other obligations when you judge overall viability.

The owner assigns another $500 monthly pay to the additional online work. That is a budgeting choice. Do not add it if the same work is already fully compensated in an unchanged owner-pay plan; instead explain how the added workload will be handled.

What one completed order contributes

Online order input Amount
Average modeled order revenue $50
Product cost $20
Packing materials $2
Paid direct fulfillment labor $4
Shipping paid by the store $6
Acquisition per completed order $5
Payment fee 3%

Here the $4 labor pays a helper, while monthly owner pay covers separate owner work. The fee is $1.50, so contribution is $50 − $20 − $2 − $4 − $6 − $5 − $1.50 = $11.50 per order.

The modeled revenue is consistent with shipping: any amount charged to the customer for shipping must be included if it is part of the price on which these costs and fees are calculated. Taxes collected for another party are excluded from this simplified revenue figure.

The $900 combined overhead-and-owner-pay requirement needs $900 ÷ $11.50 = 78.26, rounded up to 79 orders. Adding a $200 profit goal raises the requirement to $1,100 ÷ $11.50 = 95.65, rounded up to 96 orders.

At 96 orders, contribution is $1,104 and profit after the additional owner pay is $204. This is the online extension's modeled operating surplus. It does not automatically pay back launch purchases or establish that the entire physical shop is profitable.

Budget for the sales ramp

Assume orders gradually increase while monthly overhead and additional owner pay remain $900. The reserve calculation uses operating shortfalls without the optional $200 profit goal, because that goal is a desired surplus rather than a required payment.

Month Orders Contribution Operating balance Cumulative balance
1 30 $345 −$555 −$555
2 60 $690 −$210 −$765
3 100 $1,150 $250 −$515

The deepest cumulative shortfall is $765 after month two. New setup spending of $750 plus that modeled reserve gives $1,515. The month-three surplus starts rebuilding cash; it does not remove the need to fund the earlier low point.

This is a lower-bound planning example under its payment assumptions. A contingency, stock purchase, processor payout delay or supplier deposit can raise the cash need. Identify those items rather than presenting $1,515 as a universal startup cost.

If the ramp is slower at 20, 40 and 70 orders, operating balances become −$670, −$440 and −$95. The cumulative low point is $1,205, so setup plus modeled reserve becomes $1,955. None of those volumes is a demand forecast; they are scenarios to replace with your own evidence.

Compare another launch model

A new business cannot assume it already owns the shop's resources. Suppose it spends $1,600 on setup and equipment and commits $3,000 to an opening stock purchase. That is $4,600 before any operating reserve, but the stock payment must be handled correctly.

If you use an accrual contribution model that deducts product cost as sales occur, the separate cash schedule must recognize when the inventory was paid for. Do not add the full stock purchase and then assume every product-cost expense is another identical cash payment during the same period.

A preorder or made-to-order model has different collection and supplier timing. It may reduce inventory held, but it does not eliminate customer-service work, supplier risk or fulfillment costs. Write those dates and commitments before comparing it with an in-stock launch.

For a small test, launch a narrow range you can cost and fulfill consistently. Keep a real record of order revenue, payment fees, packing time, shipping and unrecovered return losses. Expand the budget after the test rather than spreading an assumed average across products you have not shipped.

Check whether your team can fulfill the target

For the incremental online work, one worker provides ten hours per week at 75% delivery utilization. That gives 32.5 monthly worker-hours using 52 ÷ 12 weeks. At 0.3 worker-hours per order, capacity is 108 whole orders, so the 96-order goal fits the assumption.

If order handling rises to 0.4 hours, capacity falls to 81. The $200 profit goal then does not fit. Packing, customer messages and dispatch preparation must be included in your time definition, with the same team-hours counted once.

How to run your own numbers

Open the ecommerce break-even calculator and enter price $50, materials $20, labor $4, other variable costs $8 for packing and shipping, acquisition $5 and fees 3%. Set fixed costs $400, owner pay $500 and target profit $200.

Use one worker, ten weekly hours, 75% utilization and 0.3 hours per order. The calculator should show 96 required orders and 108 whole-order capacity. Keep the $750 setup spending and dated cash ramp outside recurring overhead. Cost Builder can help separate packing, labor and other order costs before applying them. Other currencies are supported.

Common mistakes

  • Treating existing stock as another new purchase, or as free goods sold.
  • Counting setup marketing and acquisition spending twice.
  • Paying the same owner hours through direct labor and owner pay.
  • Adding a whole stock purchase to a cash reserve that already pays for those goods again.
  • Treating a profit goal as an unavoidable reserve payment.
  • Assuming platform setup is the complete online-launch cost.

FAQs

Is an online extension cheaper than a new store?

It can need less additional cash when stock and equipment already exist. Include all genuinely new expenses and check the whole business separately. Resources you already own still have operating and replacement costs.

Should I include my existing rent?

Include it in a whole-business viability model. For an incremental online-extension budget, include the rent increase or additional space cost caused by the launch. State which view you are using so rent is not silently omitted from the business as a whole.

Where does the opening stock purchase belong?

It belongs in the cash schedule on its payment date. Goods sold still need a cost in the contribution model. Reconcile the two views so you do not count the same purchase twice as cash leaving the business.

Why is the operating reserve $765?

That is the deepest cumulative deficit in the 30, 60 and 100-order ramp. Month three adds $250 back after the earlier shortfalls. Payment delays and a chosen contingency would need additional modeling.

Does the calculator include launch purchases?

Its monthly model covers contribution, recurring expenses, owner pay, profit goals and capacity. Keep one-time purchases and cash timing in a separate launch schedule. Do not paste every opening purchase into monthly overhead.

Can I use another currency?

Yes, the calculator supports other currencies. Keep revenue and costs in the same currency and use your own current supplier and shipping amounts. The USD examples here are illustrations rather than local price quotes.

Takeaways

  • Separate launch cash from monthly operating viability.
  • Define whether you are modeling an extension or the whole business.
  • Count existing stock cost when sold without duplicating its cash payment.
  • Fund the deepest ramp shortfall and check fulfillment hours.
