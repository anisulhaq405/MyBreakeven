Payroll for Small Business: Reconcile Wages, Liabilities and Cash

Payroll is the controlled process of recording work, calculating authorized pay, applying supported deductions and employer costs, paying employees and reconciling liabilities and filings. Gross wages, take-home pay and total employer cost are different amounts. Use current federal, state and local requirements for actual payroll; the examples below demonstrate records and reconciliation, not tax withholding calculations.

All numerical examples are original fictional USD teaching cases. They are not market rates, customer results, eligibility determinations or forecasts. Replace assumptions with dated records.

Set up the payroll record before the first pay run

Define the employer, employee role and classification, pay basis, workweek, pay period, authorization process and payment calendar. Collect required forms and payment instructions in an appropriate private payroll system. Do not keep employee identifiers, bank details or actual withholding forms in a public worksheet. A worker’s job title or a contractor label alone does not resolve the applicable classification and pay rules.

Separate the employee record from the time record and the approved pay-rate record. Assign who submits hours, who approves changes, who releases payments and who reconciles the results. One person may perform several tasks in a small business, but a dated approval trail still helps expose duplicate or unauthorized amounts.

From approved time to gross wages

Check the actual workweek and applicable overtime rules before grouping hours into a pay period. For one fictional covered, nonexempt employee, assume a regular rate of $20 and a workweek with 40 regular hours plus five overtime hours paid at one-and-one-half that rate. Regular earnings are $800 and overtime earnings $150, giving $950 gross wages. No bonus, differential, tip credit or other regular-rate adjustment exists in this simplified case.

The Department of Labor explains the federal overtime and recordkeeping context. Actual coverage, exemption, regular-rate adjustments and stricter state or local requirements need their own review. Do not apply this simple example to every worker or average one long week against one short week to erase an overtime obligation.

Reconcile approved hours × supported rates with the payroll register. If a time correction arrives after payroll is released, retain the original record and a documented correction rather than silently editing away the difference.

Reconcile a full pay-period register

For a separate original pay-period illustration, total gross wages are $4,000. Assume the correctly determined employee withholding deductions total $800 and employer-only payroll costs total $400. These are invented dollar amounts, not calculated statutory rates or actual withholding instructions. There are no employee benefit deductions, reimbursements or other adjustments in this case.

Employee withholding is part of the employee’s gross wage, held for remittance; adding it again on top of gross pay would overstate employer cost. Employer-only charges are additional. The $1,200 remittance combines the assumed employee and employer amounts, so it is not another $1,200 expense on top of $4,400.

Real benefit deductions, reimbursements and tax balances can require additional separate accounts. The point is to reconcile each item once, not to force every real register into the simplified six-line example.

Net pay and tax remittance can leave the bank on different dates

Suppose the $3,200 net payroll leaves the bank this Friday and the $1,200 liabilities are remitted later according to the applicable schedule. The bank may still hold that $1,200 between dates, but it is not free operating cash. Show the liability and its payment date in the cash forecast. Paying employees does not by itself settle the remittance obligation.

The IRS separates depositing employment taxes from reporting them; filing a return and making a payment are not the same action. It describes applicable deposit schedules and directs employers to the current instructions for their situation. Do not infer the actual tax due date from the employee pay date, or assume a payroll provider’s use removes the need to verify the business’s own confirmations.

Use the 13-week cash forecast for the dated outflows and the payroll-before-customer-payment example to see why a profitable contract can still need temporary cash.

Close payroll against the register, bank and ledger

  1. Match employee counts, approved rates and approved hours to the register.
  2. Reconcile gross pay minus employee deductions plus any separately supported adjustments to net pay.
  3. Reconcile employer-only costs to the appropriate expense and liability records.
  4. Match net payments, remittances and provider charges to bank confirmations.
  5. Investigate voided, rejected or duplicated payments without counting a corrected payroll twice.
  6. Keep filing acknowledgments and evidence of deposits with the relevant period.

If the provider withdraws one combined amount, obtain its breakdown rather than classifying the entire debit as wages. If an employee payment fails and is reissued, the replacement is not automatically a second wage expense. Maintain the outstanding-payable record until the payment is actually resolved.

Keep records for the right rule and purpose

DOL’s FLSA fact sheet distinguishes payroll records, generally retained at least three years, from supporting wage-computation records, generally at least two years. IRS employment-tax guidance states a separate at-least-four-year retention requirement and identifies longer retention for certain credits. These categories should not be collapsed into a universal “keep everything for one number of years” instruction; other applicable rules can require more.

Keep access controlled and use the actual retention schedule that applies to each record. The printable record linked below is only a non-sensitive planning reconciliation. Frequently asked questions

Are gross wages and net pay the same?

No. Net pay reflects employee deductions and any other supported adjustments. Gross wages remain the starting wage amount before those deductions.

Should employee withholding be added to employer cost again?

Not when it is already part of gross wages. Employer-only costs are additional; the example separates those from employee deductions.

Does filing a payroll return mean tax was deposited?

No. Reporting and depositing are separate obligations and need separate confirmations under the applicable instructions.

Can this article calculate actual withholding?

No. The deduction and employer-cost dollars are fictional assumptions. Use current applicable payroll rules, forms and a suitable payroll process.

What if a payroll payment is rejected?

Track the unresolved payable and documented replacement. Do not treat a reissued payment as a second expense without reconciling the original.

Can I enter real employee details in the printable record?

No. It is a public planning record, not a payroll system. Keep identifiers, bank details and actual employee forms in an appropriate private system.
