Inventory Valuation Methods: FIFO, Weighted Average and Profit

Inventory valuation assigns supported costs to the units sold and the stock still held. FIFO, weighted average and specific identification can allocate the same purchase cost differently. First establish the accounting framework and inventory record; then reconcile cost of goods sold plus ending inventory to the total available cost.

All numerical examples are original fictional USD teaching cases. They are not market rates, customer results, eligibility determinations or forecasts. Replace assumptions with dated records.

What the main inventory valuation methods mean

FIFO assigns the earliest available costs to sold units. Weighted average spreads a supported average cost across interchangeable units. Specific identification traces an individual item’s actual cost, useful when the item can be reliably distinguished. LIFO assigns later costs first where the applicable framework permits it; it is not an IFRS option. The physical shelf-picking order and the accounting cost-flow assumption are related operating decisions, but they are not automatically the same record.

Use the inventory movement guide to establish what was received, sold, returned or lost. This page starts after those quantities reconcile. A costing method cannot repair an inaccurate count, and choosing a method solely to produce a preferred margin is not a sound close process.

Follow two purchase batches through a month-end sale

A retailer begins with 100 identical units costing $10 each and purchases 100 more at $14. Assume no freight, rebates, returns, damage, tax or other acquisition adjustments. There are 200 units with $2,400 total cost. The retailer sells 150 units for $20 each, giving $3,000 revenue, and physically counts 50 remaining units. All purchases occur before the sale, so periodic weighted average and a moving average updated before this sale have the same result here.

The $100 gross-profit difference is matched by the $100 difference in ending inventory. It is an allocation difference within the same $2,400 pool, not an extra $100 bank receipt. Neither gross profit figure deducts selling expenses, payroll outside COGS, interest or income tax. The COGS bridge connects this allocation with the income statement.

Why periodic and moving averages can diverge

Now consider a different sequence using the same opening stock and purchase prices: sell 50 units before the second purchase, buy the 100 units at $14, then sell another 100. A perpetual moving-average record first expenses 50 × $10 = $500. The remaining 50 old units cost $500; adding the $1,400 receipt leaves $1,900 over 150 units, or $12.666666… each. The next 100 sales release $1,266.67 after presentation rounding, leaving $633.33 inventory. Total COGS is $1,766.67.

A periodic average calculated once across the entire period still produces $1,800 COGS and $600 ending inventory. The two schedules have different averaging dates. Retain full precision in the ledger and reconcile the displayed cents to the $2,400 control total; do not average the two quoted purchase prices without weighting their quantities. Record transaction dates before comparing systems.

Keep IFRS, US reporting and tax choices separate

IAS 2 uses specific identification for appropriate distinct items and FIFO or weighted average for ordinarily interchangeable inventory. Its measurement rule compares cost with net realisable value, which considers expected selling proceeds less completion and selling costs. US reporting and US tax rules have their own requirements; LIFO availability in a US context is not permission to use it under IFRS. The IRS also has method-change procedures and particular small-business inventory provisions.

Identify which statements or return you are preparing, the documented policy and any required approval before changing methods. A planning spreadsheet is not an accounting-policy election. Apply the same supported basis consistently to comparable inventory; explain a real policy change instead of silently replacing last month’s costs. Product and period costs addresses which costs enter inventory in the first place.

Test recoverability separately from the cost-flow method

In a separate IFRS illustration, the 50 closing FIFO units carry $700 cost. New evidence supports $12 expected selling price per unit and $1 selling cost, with no completion cost. Net realisable value is 50 × ($12 − $1) = $550. The assumed comparison therefore indicates a $150 write-down. Under the $600 average-cost carrying amount, the difference to the same $550 recovery estimate would be $50. This is a separate valuation adjustment, not another purchase or an immediate cash payment.

Document the evidence date, affected items, estimated recovery and applicable framework. Do not spread one damaged product’s estimate across unrelated healthy inventory. US measurement can depend on the inventory method and reporting rules, so do not apply this IFRS illustration as a universal US tax instruction.

A practical inventory-valuation close

  1. Reconcile opening quantity plus supported receipts minus supported outflows to the counted closing quantity.
  2. Build the cost pool from the policy’s qualifying costs and supplier adjustments, each counted once.
  3. Apply the documented method to dated transactions; keep original source invoices.
  4. Check COGS plus closing inventory against available cost before separate adjustments.
  5. Review loss, obsolescence and recoverability evidence, and distinguish write-downs from physical losses.
  6. Reconcile the expense with accrual timing and supplier cash payments separately.

If the control total fails, resolve the missing movement or duplicated cost before using the resulting margin to change prices. The margin-versus-markup guide then helps interpret the correctly defined cost base.

Frequently asked questions

Is FIFO always the best inventory method?

No. Framework permissions, item characteristics, reliable records and consistent policy matter. A method producing a higher profit this month is not automatically the appropriate one.

Is weighted average the average of supplier price quotes?

No. Weight supported cost by the corresponding quantities. In the case, $2,400 over 200 units is $12 per unit.

Does a higher inventory value create cash?

No. The example reallocates the same purchase cost between COGS and remaining stock. Customer and supplier cash payments need a separate schedule.

Can LIFO be used under IFRS?

No. IAS 2 does not permit LIFO. A US reporting or tax choice must be evaluated under its own rules rather than transferred to IFRS.

Why can moving average differ from periodic average?

Moving average updates after receipts and applies the rate to subsequent issues. Periodic average uses the whole period’s cost pool. Intervening sales can therefore change the allocation.

Does a write-down change the quantity on the shelf?

Not by itself. A valuation adjustment changes carrying value. A physical loss also needs a supported quantity movement and must not be counted twice.
