What Adjusted EBITDA changes

Adjusted EBITDA starts with a defined EBITDA subtotal and applies separately identified adjustments. A cost adjustment usually increases the subtotal; removing an included gain decreases it. The resulting measure depends on the chosen definition and is not automatically comparable across businesses.

Begin with the EBITDA calculator and preserve the standard earnings bridge before adding adjustments. This guide evaluates the evidence behind that second bridge. It does not endorse an adjustment for a transaction, covenant or public filing.

Keep standard and adjusted earnings separate

Standard EBITDA adds back the included interest, income taxes, depreciation and amortization to net income. A proposed litigation, owner-pay or restructuring adjustment is a further policy choice; it does not belong silently inside the standard formula.

The calculator labels base EBITDA and Adjusted EBITDA separately. A negative adjustment is allowed because unusual income may need to be removed. Do not use the same positive-only “add-back” field for every event and hide its direction.

This independent scenario assumes both the $12,000 expense and $5,000 gain already affected the net income underlying base EBITDA. The legal-cost adjustment is an illustrative proposal, not a conclusion that all legal fees are nonrecurring. If the gain did not enter the starting earnings, subtracting it here would be a second adjustment.

At $1,000,000 matched net revenue, base margin is 13% and adjusted margin is 13.7%. The $7,000 increase is a change in the selected performance measure, not $7,000 new cash received.

Reproduce the bridge: in the EBITDA calculator, retain the base example, open “Adjusted EBITDA — separate signed items”, then add a uniquely described $12,000 expense adjustment and a −$5,000 gain adjustment. The base must remain $130,000 while the separately adjusted result becomes $137,000. Renaming the same invoice does not make a duplicate economic adjustment valid.

Retain the ledger reference, invoice, approval and written rationale with the calculation. Unique descriptions reduce duplicate entries but software cannot verify whether an event is genuinely unusual or whether a buyer will accept the adjustment.

Owner compensation needs a replacement-cost view

An owner’s salary is not automatically removable. If the owner performs necessary work, someone must still deliver it after a sale or management change. An adjustment based on replacement economics may remove only an evidenced excess over comparable necessary compensation, with employer costs and role scope considered.

For example, $90,000 included owner compensation and a documented $70,000 replacement cost suggest a $20,000 difference under that chosen normalization policy, not a $90,000 saving. This is a hypothetical arithmetic example, not a salary benchmark or accepted valuation adjustment. Owner draws are not another wage expense merely because cash leaves the business.

Repeated one-offs and future savings need scrutiny

A category called “one-time” every year may be part of the ongoing cost of operating. A future system saving is not necessarily a valid adjustment to historical earnings. Show expected savings in a separate forecast with implementation costs and a start date.

Do not remove ordinary rent, marketing, payroll or maintenance simply to reach a target margin. Stock compensation, impairment, acquisition costs and restructuring can have purpose-specific treatment; this simple tool does not decide the appropriate policy or contractual EBITDA definition.

Adjusted EBITDA is not cash flow or a valuation

Even a well-documented adjustment does not erase cash already paid. Debt service, working capital and capital investment remain separate. Read the reconciled profit-versus-cash example before treating adjusted earnings as distributable money.

A valuation also needs a defensible approach, risk assessment, debt/cash treatment and transaction perimeter. The calculator supplies no market multiple and no automatic sale price. For scenario planning, keep projected benefits separate from actual historical records.

Review the bridge before using it

Check that the starting EBITDA reconciles, every signed item affected that starting figure, recurring economics remain visible, gains are treated consistently with expenses, and no item appears twice. Compare prior periods using the same definitions or explain a change.

Keep base and adjusted subtotals side by side. If an item is disputed, show the scenario with and without it instead of presenting a single adjusted number as an independently verified fact. Return to the itemized calculator to test the arithmetic.

Source and methodology

SEC non-GAAP interpretations, including sections 100 and 103, discuss potentially misleading adjustments and the naming/reconciliation of EBIT, EBITDA and adjusted measures. Public-company rules have a defined scope; this guide does not imply every private-company worksheet is an SEC filing.

The numbers, adjustment register and commentary here are original teaching material. Consult the actual agreement and qualified accounting advisers for a reporting or transaction decision.

Frequently asked questions

What is an EBITDA add-back?

It is an identified adjustment that increases a specified EBITDA measure, normally for an expense already included in the starting earnings. Whether it is appropriate depends on evidence and purpose.

Can Adjusted EBITDA be lower than EBITDA?

Yes. Removing included unusual income or applying other signed deductions can lower the adjusted subtotal.

Can I add back all owner salary?

Not automatically. Necessary owner work may require paid replacement; draws are not the same as included salary expense.

Does the calculator approve my adjustments?

No. It checks arithmetic and input structure. It does not verify records, recurrence, contract terms or professional acceptance.
