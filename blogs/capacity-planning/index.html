Capacity planning matches a defined workload with the usable resources and time needed to complete it. In the worked case, labor supports 50 jobs but a shared machine limits the week to 40; demand for 46 jobs therefore needs a real change in routing, resources or promises.

The numerical case below is an original fictional planning example, not a customer result, market benchmark or professional determination. All monetary figures use USD. Use the same unit and period throughout, and replace assumptions with source records.

Capacity planning starts with a deliverable unit

Capacity planning compares the work customers need with the resources and time that can deliver it. Define a completed unit first: one inspected repair, one finished batch or one accepted client assignment. An inquiry is demand evidence, not completed output. A paid hour is an input, not a sale. Without those boundaries, a team can appear fully booked while promising more completed work than its process can release.

Choose one planning window and write its opening backlog, new demand, due dates and completion standard. A monthly total is useful for commitments, but a weekly or daily schedule exposes concentrated deadlines. Keep capacity available in the period separate from capacity already committed to existing customers. Do not count an unfinished job as both opening backlog and new demand.

Separate theoretical, practical and committed capacity

Theoretical capacity assumes the resource is available for every stated hour. Practical capacity removes identified time that cannot produce the defined unit. Committed capacity is the portion already promised. These are different records, not competing answers. If maintenance is explicitly removed from machine hours, do not also apply a utilization allowance that removes that same maintenance again.

Count worker-hours for each person working an hour. Two people on a three-hour assignment use six worker-hours, even though only three elapsed hours pass. Machine time and inspection time may run partly alongside labor, so document which resource is occupied at each stage. A total-hours estimate alone cannot establish the slowest stage.

Worked example: enough labor, but a machine bottleneck

A fictional repair team has three technicians, each scheduled for 35 paid hours in the week: 105 worker-hours. Identified leave uses nine hours, training six hours and administration 15 hours, leaving 75 productive worker-hours. Each completed job consumes 1.5 technician worker-hours. Labor could therefore support 50 jobs. These are stated planning assumptions, not wage, productivity or industry benchmarks.

The process also needs one shared machine and a final inspection. The machine has 24 usable hours and needs 0.6 hour per job, supporting 40 jobs. Inspection has 18 usable hours and takes 0.3 hour per job, supporting 60. Assuming every job follows these stages and no other constraint applies, practical weekly throughput is the smallest stage capacity: 40 completed jobs.

Compare feasible throughput with demand and deadlines

Suppose confirmed demand is 46 jobs for that same week. The bottleneck can support only 40, leaving six beyond the base plan. Buying more technician hours does not by itself release those six jobs because labor is not the limiting resource. Before accepting the due dates, check machine scheduling, alternate routing, subcontracting or a revised customer promise.

Write the gap as six jobs and 3.6 machine-hours: 46 × 0.6 minus 24. That shortfall is not 3.6 technician hours. Keep material availability and the inspected completion standard unchanged when comparing options. A busy upstream stage can create a queue at the machine without increasing finished output. NIST’s supply-chain research illustrates why bottleneck and flow-time analysis concern the process, not just isolated resource use.

Test the cost of the actual constraint-relieving option

Assume a vetted subcontractor can perform only the machine step for six jobs, with no extra internal machine time. The additional charge is $28 per job, or $168. The 46 jobs still require 69 technician hours and 13.8 inspection hours, within the stated 75 and 18. If contribution before that additional charge is $65 per job, the base 40-job plan contributes $2,600. Completing 46 with the subcontract step contributes $2,990 less $168 = $2,822.

The incremental contribution is $222 before any extra transport, quality failures, overhead or other unentered cost. This is a conditional comparison, not a reason to outsource automatically. Record supplier capacity, turnaround, acceptance checks and responsibility for defects. Use relevant differential costs to compare actual alternatives rather than treating every shared allocation as avoidable.

Allow for changing mix, rework and peak loading

Averages can hide unequal resource use. A job family needing twice as much machine time cannot be scheduled as if it were an ordinary job. List demand by routing and resource-hours, then sum each resource’s load. If rework occurs, measure its actual resource consumption and check whether the original standard already includes it. An unexplained cushion is harder to review than a documented range.

A promised completion date also depends on sequence and available inputs. Forty jobs spread evenly through the week differ from 40 all arriving on Friday afternoon. Preserve the daily schedule and queue age alongside the weekly arithmetic. Identify which assumptions are commitments and which depend on an unconfirmed order, delivery or employee availability.

Connect the resource plan to controlled people and product records

A skills matrix matters because employees are not interchangeable hours. Document who can perform the constrained step, who can approve inspection and what supervised training is still needed. The small-business HR workflow owns role, training and approval records; the capacity plan consumes their current approved availability. It should not silently redefine pay, leave or employee classification.

Product or service revisions can change processing time. Link the routing to the released version described in product lifecycle management. If a new revision needs a longer test, update the capacity standard when that revision takes effect. Keep the labor-cost question separate too: gross pay and net pay explain a paycheck boundary, not how many productive hours a person can deliver.

Review the plan after disruption and before expansion

A broken machine can move the constraint or remove the base plan entirely. The business continuity plan should state which work must resume first and which alternate route has been tested. Do not count an emergency workaround as normal capacity merely because it exists on paper. Record its activation time, usable output and full operating costs.

For expansion, compare added output with supported demand, incremental contribution and payment dates. Equipment bought this month may require commissioning and training before releasing any work. The business expansion guide connects that investment to cash and capacity. At each review, preserve the original plan, record actual completed output and investigate differences in mix, availability, rework or cycle time before replacing the assumptions.

Keep an editable decision record

Open the printable capacity planning record. Record the evidence, calculation, responsible role and next review before making a commitment. This is a manual worksheet: fields accept typing, but do not calculate, submit information or save after reload. Print or save a PDF while the entries are present. Use non-sensitive aliases rather than employee identifiers, account numbers or confidential customer records.

Frequently asked questions

What is capacity planning in a small business?

It is the comparison of demand and deadlines with the usable people, equipment and process time needed to deliver a defined completed unit.

Why is capacity not the same as utilization?

Capacity describes feasible output under stated assumptions. Utilization describes how much of a resource’s available time or output capacity is used. High utilization at a non-bottleneck does not prove higher finished throughput.

How is the bottleneck found in the example?

Labor supports 50 jobs, the machine 40 and inspection 60. With the stated shared routing and no other constraint, the machine limits the process to 40 completed jobs.

Should leave and a utilization allowance both reduce capacity?

Only if they represent different unavailable time. Removing the same leave, maintenance or administration twice understates capacity.

Does the subcontract option guarantee a better result?

No. The fictional comparison adds $222 contribution after the stated $168 extra charge, but depends on supplier capacity, quality, timing and the absence of further unentered costs.

Can gross pay be used as the productive-hours denominator?

No. Gross pay is an earnings amount, while productive hours measure usable work time. Keep pay, employer cost, time availability and completed output as separate records.
