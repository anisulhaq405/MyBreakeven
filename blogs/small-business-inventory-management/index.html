Small-Business Inventory Management: Cash and Stock Records

Small-business inventory management tracks what stock exists, where it is, whether it can be sold and what purchasing will commit in cash. A reliable routine reconciles movements to physical counts before turning the balance into buying decisions.

All numerical examples are original fictional teaching cases in USD, not market benchmarks or promised outcomes. Assumptions are stated beside the calculations; replace them with your own dated evidence.

Start with one stock identity and a movement record

Give each distinct item a stable SKU, unit of measure and location. A case of 12 and a single unit cannot share a quantity field without a conversion rule. Record receipts when accepted, sales when stock leaves the relevant stage, returns when assessed and damage or other adjustments with reasons. Identify who owns each entry and source document.

Do not rely on an invoice list alone. A supplier invoice can arrive before the goods, and a customer order can reserve units before shipment. Keep physical on-hand, committed stock, available stock and incoming purchase orders distinct. The same carton should not be counted in transit, in receiving and on the shelf simultaneously.

Reconcile a one-SKU stock movement

A fictional retailer starts a week with 120 units of one SKU. It accepts 80 purchased units, ships 140 sold units, receives five customer-returned units that pass inspection and can be resold, and writes off three damaged units. All units carry the same $12 cost, so this case deliberately avoids competing valuation methods, freight adjustments, taxes and currency effects.

Quantity reconciliation: 120 + 80 − 140 + 5 − 3 = 62 units. Cost reconciliation: $1,440 + $960 − $1,680 + $60 − $36 = $744. The net cost released on sold goods is $1,620; the $36 damage loss is separately identified in this teaching record. The returns’ selling-price refunds and sales recognition belong in the sales ledger and are not calculated from the $12 cost.

Investigate the difference between the ledger and the shelf

At the count, physically observed stock is 60 units instead of the expected 62. The two-unit difference represents $24 at the uniform cost. Do not immediately label it theft or add a balancing purchase. Freeze the relevant movement window and check receiving, shipments, unit conversions, returns and locations for missing or duplicated entries.

Suppose the team finds two shipped units whose dispatch record never reached the inventory ledger. Post the supported shipment movement through the normal sales/cost workflow. If no cause is established, follow the business’s approval policy for the count adjustment and retain the unresolved reason. The revised 60-unit balance carries $720, but the classification of the $24 difference depends on the evidence.

Separate available stock from a purchasing promise

Use the reconciled 60 on-hand units for this separate availability decision. Fifteen are already committed to unshipped customer orders. Available to promise is 60 − 15 = 45 units. A purchase order for 50 more units is incoming, not available on the shelf. The inventory position for a defined replenishment model may include that incoming quantity, but customer promises must reflect receipt timing and reliability.

Connect stock decisions to cash and product condition

Slow stock can tie up cash while the inventory ledger still reports a positive balance. Review age, expiry, condition and actual demand by SKU. An inventory balance is not cash ready to spend, and damaged or obsolete units should not remain available to promise simply because they still have a system quantity.

Count discrepancies, returns and stockouts can expose different operational causes. A recurring receipt mismatch needs a receiving fix; repeated stockouts may indicate lead-time or demand errors. Holding cost per SKU considers the cost of keeping stock. The COGS bridge connects the cost ledger to sold units without treating all purchases as immediate expense.

Use a practical daily, weekly and monthly control routine

Daily, record accepted receipts, dispatches, reservations and inspected returns with source references. Check negative quantities and duplicate SKUs before accepting new orders. Keep damaged or quarantined units distinguishable from sellable stock. For multiple sales channels, confirm that a sale updates the shared availability record in time to prevent double selling.

Weekly, cycle-count selected high-value or high-movement items, reconcile open orders and review upcoming supplier payments. Monthly, reconcile the stock subledger to accounting, review aging and adjustment approvals, and document any policy changes. These frequencies are illustrative starting points, not universal rules; item value, perishability and transaction volume should shape the routine.

A spreadsheet can support a small operation if responsibilities, backups, access and reconciliation are clear. More software does not repair undefined units or unrecorded returns. Use the worksheet to document the movement bridge and an action owner before deciding whether a process or system change is required.

Frequently asked questions

What should a small-business inventory record include?

A stable SKU, unit of measure, location, dated receipts and issues, returns, adjustments, commitments and supporting references.

How is closing stock calculated in the example?

120 opening + 80 receipts − 140 shipments + 5 resellable returns − 3 damaged units = 62 expected units.

Why is the physical count only 60?

The example investigates a two-unit difference rather than inventing a cause. A supported missing shipment or an approved unexplained adjustment requires different evidence.

Are incoming purchase orders available stock?

Not yet on hand. Track their expected receipt and reliability separately when making customer promises and replenishment decisions.

Does a supplier purchase commitment reduce cash immediately?

Not necessarily. Payment timing depends on terms. The case tracks the $600 commitment separately from receipt, inventory recognition and cash payment.

Is this an inventory valuation-method guide?

No. The worked record uses a uniform $12 cost to isolate movement control. Different valuation and impairment policies require their own accounting analysis.
