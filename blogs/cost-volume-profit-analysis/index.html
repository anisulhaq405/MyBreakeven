Quick answer

Cost-volume-profit analysis tests how price, variable costs, fixed costs and sold volume combine to produce operating profit. Break-even is its zero-profit case; the useful business decision also checks demand, product mix and the capacity range.

Read the source-backed application and limits. Worked business amounts are fictional teaching examples.

Every amount in the worked case is an original fictional USD planning assumption. It is not an industry benchmark, a customer result or a prediction.

Start with one consistent unit and cost boundary

Operating profit = units × (net price − variable cost per unit) − fixed operating costs. Contribution per unit is net price less variable cost. The contribution ratio divides that contribution by price. Break-even units equal fixed costs divided by positive unit contribution. Required units for a profit target equal (fixed costs + target operating profit) divided by unit contribution.

If contribution is zero or negative, selling more of the unchanged offer does not cover positive fixed costs. Keep every amount in the same period and currency. Owner wages included in fixed costs must not be added again as a separate profit goal. Drawings and financing require separate cash treatment.

Unit contribution is $48, the contribution ratio is 40%, and exact break-even is 200 units or $24,000. A $4,800 operating-profit target requires 300 units. A $5,000 target would require 304.1667 units mathematically; 305 whole units produce $5,040 profit under the unchanged assumptions.

Check the capacity step before trusting the target

Suppose existing resources can deliver only 260 units. Selling 300 requires another $2,400 monthly capacity commitment. The fixed-cost base becomes $12,000 and 300 units now produce $2,400, not $4,800. To recover the original $4,800 target at the same price and variable rate requires 350 units. That second target also needs its own capacity and demand check.

A discounted price of $110 leaves only $38 contribution at the original $72 variable rate. With the original $9,600 fixed base, break-even becomes 252.6316 exact units, or 253 whole units. These comparisons isolate a changed assumption; combining them without rebuilding the full model would misstate the answer.

A sales mix changes what an average unit means

For two offers contributing $40 and $70 per unit, a 3:2 unit mix creates a five-unit bundle with $260 contribution. At $10,400 fixed cost, break-even is 40 bundles: 120 units of the first offer and 80 of the second. The weighted unit contribution is $52. If the mix changes, that average no longer applies.

Know when the straight-line model stops answering the question

Simple CVP assumes stable price and variable cost per unit, a relevant fixed-cost range, meaningful cost classification and a stated mix. Inventory changes, capacity tiers, waste, payment fees and channel commissions can require a more detailed model. Profit is not available cash: collection delays, inventory purchases, debt principal and capital expenditure must also be scheduled.

Use flexible budget variance analysis to compare actual performance at actual volume rather than blaming a revenue shortfall on cost control. Treat a feasible profit target as a plan to test, not proof that buyers will order it.

Measure the safety margin inside the relevant capacity range

The ACCA CVP explanation treats margin of safety as the excess of budgeted or actual sales over break-even sales. It should use the same price, cost assumptions and sales mix as the break-even calculation. It is a sales buffer under that model, not a cash reserve or a probability of avoiding loss.

At 250 units in the original fictional case, sales are $30,000 and break-even sales are $24,000. The margin of safety is 50 units or $6,000. Dividing $6,000 by $30,000 gives 20%. A loss of 50 sales units reaches zero operating profit under the original $48 unit contribution and $9,600 fixed-cost base. This statement assumes no simultaneous price, mix, variable-rate or fixed-cost change.

A capacity change requires a fresh calculation. The case’s additional $2,400 commitment raises fixed costs to $12,000, so the revised break-even is 250 units at $48 contribution. At 300 units, the revised result is $2,400 and the revised margin of safety is 50 units, or 16.67% of 300. The old 200-unit threshold no longer explains that committed cost structure.

Record exactly when the extra commitment becomes unavoidable. If a capacity purchase has already been agreed, returning to a lower sales volume may not restore the old fixed-cost base immediately. Show the applicable range, commitment date and exit terms beside each scenario rather than selecting whichever fixed-cost assumption produces the preferred answer.

Frequently asked questions

Is CVP the same as break-even analysis?

Break-even is the zero-profit case within CVP. CVP also compares target profits and changes in volume, price, costs and mix.

Why can a correct target still fail?

It may exceed capacity, rely on unsupported demand or cross a fixed-cost step. A profit model also does not establish cash availability.

What if contribution per unit is negative?

With the same price and variable rate, additional units deepen the contribution shortfall. Resolve the economics before dividing fixed costs by contribution.

Why does the product mix matter?

Each offer can contribute a different amount. The weighted contribution is valid only for the stated mix and cost range.

Does a 20% margin of safety mean a 20% chance of loss?

No. It describes the modeled sales reduction to break-even using a stated denominator, not a probability. Demand uncertainty requires separate analysis.

Can I retain the old break-even after committing to more fixed capacity?

No. Recalculate using the applicable committed fixed costs and check whether reducing sales also removes that commitment.
