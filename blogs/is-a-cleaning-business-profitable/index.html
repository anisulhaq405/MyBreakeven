Cleaning PROFITABILITY GUIDE

Is a Cleaning Business Profitable? Check the Visits and Hours

Published September 20, 2026Updated October 10, 2026

Published by MyBreakeven. Editorial standards and corrections. Report a calculation or content issue to support@mybreakeven.com.

Test cleaning business profit from visit contribution, monthly bills and owner pay. Compare labor overruns, recurring work, re-cleans and staff capacity.

CleaningProfitabilityMargin Planning

A cleaning business can be profitable when its completed visits cover delivery costs, monthly bills and the owner’s income. In the example below, a $160 visit contributes $63.20, but the 95 visits needed for the income goal exceed the two-person team's assumed capacity.

That is why a full diary deserves a closer look. Check what each visit leaves and how many worker-hours it uses, then see whether the monthly target can fit. The USD figures here are illustrative; use your own completed visits, paid labor and route records.

Quick answer

Calculate contribution for one complete visit, including paid worker time, supplies, travel-related costs, acquisition and payment fees. Divide the monthly fixed-cost, owner-pay and profit goal by that contribution and round up to whole visits. Compare the required visits with worker-hour capacity and realistic paid demand. A plan is stronger when the same assumptions fit all three checks.

Start with the visits you finish and get paid for

Separate recurring maintenance cleans, first visits and move-out jobs. Their preparation and task lists can differ enough to make one average misleading. For each type, record the agreed scope, price, actual worker-hours and any return visit needed to complete the work.

Count the crew's time, not only the customer's appointment length. Two cleaners working 1.5 hours use three worker-hours. If paid travel and setup sit outside that time, add them to both the cost and duration used in the plan.

Use completed paid visits as the sales unit. Keep inquiries, bookings and cancellations in separate columns. A booking that drops out may still cost acquisition money or leave a gap in the route, but it does not produce the full completed-visit revenue in this example.

When the customer changes the requested work, update the visit record and quote before assuming the same margin. Another room or a task outside the scope can increase supplies and time even if the diary still shows one appointment.

For owner income, settle the pay method once. This example includes paid staff labor per visit and a separate owner-pay goal. A solo cleaner may instead put their income in owner pay; including the same wage again in direct labor would overstate the cost.

With those definitions in place, you can compare the quote with the visit you actually delivered. A short job note about access, travel or added tasks is enough to explain many of the differences without turning the review into a long report.

Calculate the contribution from one cleaning visit

Per-visit input Example input
Selling price $160
Materials and cleaning supplies $8
Paid direct labor: 3 worker-hours × $22 $66
Other variable cost, including allocated travel expense $10
Acquisition cost per completed visit $8
Payment fee at 3% $4.80
Contribution $63.20

Before the percentage fee, direct costs total $92. Contribution is $160 − $92 − $4.80 = $63.20 per completed visit. The contribution margin is $63.20 ÷ $160 = 39.5%. This does not mean the owner keeps 39.5% as final profit.

The supply input should represent the portion used on one visit, not an entire restocking purchase. Acquisition cost should reflect the completed paid visits used in your planning period. A recurring customer's initial acquisition cost can be allocated across an explicitly chosen number of visits, while a new one-time customer may carry a different cost.

The $10 other-cost input is an assumed job allocation. If it includes vehicle expenses that are already fully charged in monthly fixed costs, remove the overlap. Re-clean costs are set to zero in the base visit and tested separately below. That makes the starting calculation transparent rather than quietly assuming no job will ever need additional work.

Show the monthly profit after owner pay

Assume monthly fixed costs $2,500, owner pay $3,000 and target profit $500. The goal is $6,000. At $63.20 contribution, required volume is $6,000 ÷ $63.20 = 94.94, rounded up to 95 completed visits.

At exactly 95 visits, revenue is $15,200 and contribution is $6,004. After fixed costs and owner pay, planning profit is $504. The planning profit margin is $504 ÷ $15,200 = 3.32%. This shows the difference between a 39.5% contribution margin and the much smaller amount remaining after the monthly requirements.

At 100 visits, contribution is $6,320 and planning profit after those same requirements is $820, or 5.13% of $16,000 revenue. At 80 visits, contribution is $5,056 and the business is $444 short of covering fixed costs plus the $3,000 owner-pay goal. The individual visit still has positive contribution in all three cases; monthly volume changes the overall result.

Keep cash timing separate when commercial customers pay after invoicing. Completed work can be profitable on the job worksheet while payroll and supply payments arrive before customer cash. Record receipts and payment dates instead of assuming every invoiced dollar is available immediately.

Compare price, labor and re-clean scenarios

Scenario Price Direct cost before fee Contribution Visits for $6,000 goal
Base three-hour visit $160 $92 $63.20 95
Same visit at $175 $175 $92 $77.75 78
Labor rises to four worker-hours $160 $114 $41.20 146
Base visit plus $5 expected re-clean cost $160 $97 $58.20 104

The price increase leaves $14.55 more contribution per visit after the fee adjustment. It is a controlled offer scenario, not a prediction of customer acceptance. If the higher price includes extra work, include that work's costs and hours before claiming the same benefit.

The labor-overrun case adds one worker-hour at $22 and removes delivery capacity. Its required volume rises to 146 visits. The $5 re-clean allowance instead represents an expected cost allocated across completed visits. If it comes from actual records, use the frequency and average additional cost consistently. If it is an untested assumption, label it and compare a higher-cost case.

Re-cleans can consume schedule time as well as cash. The table isolates the contribution effect; the capacity scenario must include the expected extra worker-hours when testing the full plan. A cost allowance without time can hide the disruption caused by returning to a property.

Compare required visits with staff capacity

Two workers with 35 weekly hours each at 70% utilization provide 2 × 35 × 52 ÷ 12 × 70% = 212.33 monthly delivery worker-hours. At three worker-hours per visit, whole-visit capacity is 70. The base requirement of 95 visits therefore exceeds capacity even though every visit contributes positively.

At four worker-hours, capacity falls to 53 while required volume rises to 146. The labor overrun hurts both sides of the plan. Changing only the hourly capacity figure or only the labor cost misses part of the problem.

At the $175 price, 78 visits are still more than the same 70-visit capacity. If the scope can be delivered in 2.5 worker-hours without compromising the agreed work, capacity would be 84. That is a separate operational scenario that requires evidence from completed jobs, not an instruction to rush the service.

Three workers at 35 weekly hours and 70% utilization would provide 318.5 hours and 106 whole three-hour visits. The hour requirement could then fit, but additional fixed costs, hiring constraints and paid demand might change the goal. Recalculate the full plan when adding people rather than treating capacity as free.

Use recurring work without overstating the benefit

A repeat customer may reduce acquisition and scheduling effort, but recurring pricing can also be lower. Compare both sides. Suppose repeat visits carry $3 in allocated acquisition rather than $8 while the price remains $160. Contribution improves to $68.20. If the recurring price falls to $150, contribution becomes $58.50 with that same $3 acquisition cost.

The recurring label alone therefore does not establish a better margin. Track actual visit duration, cancellations, route grouping and retained paid customers. When a customer leaves, replacement acquisition can increase again. Keep first visits and ongoing visits separate before building a weighted average.

A route with several nearby properties can reduce travel, but only if the completed schedule supports the allocation. If one customer cancels, the remaining trip may need more time or cost per visit. Record the actual route outcome rather than assuming every recurring slot produces the same efficiency.

Set two workers, 35 weekly hours per worker, three total worker-hours per visit and 70% utilization. The example should show $63.20 contribution, 95 required visits and 70 whole-visit capacity. What a full cleaning diary can hide

  • Equating bookings with completed paid visits. Cancellations and unpaid work change revenue and acquisition cost.
  • Ignoring crew-hours. Two workers multiply labor even when the customer sees a short appointment.
  • Calling contribution take-home profit. Fixed costs and owner income still need coverage.
  • Reducing time without evidence. Capacity assumptions must preserve the agreed service scope.
  • Assuming recurring work is automatically better. Compare price, acquisition, route time and retention together.

FAQs

Can every visit make money while the business misses its income goal?

Yes, positive contribution from a visit is only part of the test. The month also needs enough completed work to cover fixed bills and the chosen owner income, within the available worker-hours.

How many completed visits does this example need?

The $6,000 combined goal needs 95 visits at $63.20 contribution each. The assumed two-worker schedule supports 70 whole visits, so the base plan needs a change in price, cost, scope or available delivery hours.

What happens when a three-hour visit takes four worker-hours?

At the example’s $22 hourly labor cost, contribution falls from $63.20 to $41.20. The longer duration also reduces whole-visit capacity from 70 to 53 under the same team schedule.

How should I record a re-clean?

Link its additional time and costs to the original completed visit. Use the recorded frequency and cost to create an allowance, and include the expected hours when checking capacity.

Should I automatically discount recurring visits?

Compare the reduction in acquisition, travel or service time with the contribution lost to the discount. The example’s $150 recurring visit leaves less contribution than the $160 base visit despite its lower acquisition allowance.

What should I review before adding another cleaner?

Recalculate paid labor, any added fixed costs and the jobs the larger team can deliver. Then check whether enough paid work is available to use those hours; the extra capacity alone does not cover its costs.

Takeaways

  • Use completed paid visits, with a separate record of cancellations.
  • Count all workers' delivery and relevant travel time.
  • Include re-clean costs and the hours needed to return.
  • Compare the monthly income target with paid demand and deliverable visits.
