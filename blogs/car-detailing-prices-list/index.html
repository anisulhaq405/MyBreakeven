Car Detailing Price List: Scope, Costs and Worker-Hours

Published September 17, 2026Updated October 7, 2026

Published by MyBreakeven. Editorial standards and corrections. Report a calculation or content issue to support@mybreakeven.com.

Build a detailing price list from scope, supplies, labor, travel and fees. Compare repeat visits, larger services, extra work and route capacity.

Mobile detailingPricingProfit Planning

Build a car detailing price list from service scope, vehicle condition and complete delivery cost. In the fictional maintenance-detail example below, a $160 price leaves $54.20 after supplies, paid labor, travel, acquisition and card fees. That contribution must fund overhead and owner income. A public list should show what the price buys and when a condition-based quote is needed. These USD figures illustrate a cost model; they are not current local detailing prices or industry averages.

Quick answer

For each service, define vehicle size, included work and expected worker-hours. Add consumables, direct labor, travel, acquisition and payment fees, then calculate contribution at the proposed price. Check the monthly job target against route capacity. Use starting prices only where the included scope and conditions for a revised quote are clear.

Build the maintenance-detail price first

Assume a maintenance detail uses two worker-hours at a loaded $25 per hour, or $50. Consumables cost $12, vehicle expense and parking cost $14, and acquisition averages $25 per completed job. Cost before percentage fees is $12 + $50 + $14 + $25 = $101.

At $160 with a 3% fee, processing costs $4.80. Contribution is $160 − $101 − $4.80 = $54.20. This is the amount available for monthly overhead and owner pay before any extra profit goal. It is not the owner's take-home from the appointment.

Assume $1,000 monthly overhead, $2,500 owner pay and a $500 additional profit goal. The requirement is $4,000. At $54.20 contribution, 73 jobs produce $3,956.60 and miss that goal. Seventy-four produce $4,010.80, leaving $510.80 after overhead and owner pay.

Suppose one technician has 35 weekly hours at 75% utilization. Using 52 ÷ 12 weeks, usable monthly time is 113.75 worker-hours. At two hours per job, only 56 whole jobs fit. The $160 list price does not support the selected income plan at this scope and time budget, even if every available appointment sells.

With 56 jobs, required contribution is $4,000 ÷ 56 = $71.4286. The cost-based price floor is ($101 + $71.4286) ÷ 0.97 = $177.7614, rounded upward to $177.77. A $180 quote leaves $73.60 contribution and needs 55 jobs. Fifty-six jobs leave $621.60 after overhead and owner pay.

Demand must be tested at the new price. You cannot assume the old booking volume remains unchanged. The mobile detailing break-even guide connects job contribution with the monthly route, while the price list communicates the service a customer can buy.

Turn the estimate into a usable price list

Use named service rows with explicit inclusions: vehicle size category, interior or exterior scope, expected condition, location coverage and quoted extras. A maintenance detail and restoration work should not share a cost assumption simply because both involve cleaning the same car.

Keep the number of categories manageable. A customer needs to know whether the listed price applies to a compact car in normal condition, a larger vehicle or a heavily soiled interior. State when photographs or an inspection are needed before confirmation. Avoid a vague starting price that never describes the service available at that price.

For an actual booking, carry the chosen list row into the job sheet. Record arrival, setup, work and pack-down time, plus any separately modeled travel. Compare the result with the estimate after completion. If a category repeatedly overruns, revise its scope or price rather than relying on the technician to rush.

If you are the owner-technician, choose a consistent pay treatment. This example uses incremental paid technician labor and separate owner income for management or ownership. If the same owner performs all labor and puts their compensation in owner pay, do not also pay the same time through the direct wage line. Hours remain part of capacity regardless of the cash treatment.

Four variables that move the service price

Vehicle condition. Embedded pet hair, stains or residue can extend labor and product use. Estimate the added work before promising a fixed price. Use a defined extra or revised quote tied to scope rather than assuming every large car is equally dirty.

Service duration. Worker-hours cover the entire modeled delivery process. A four-hour elapsed visit with two technicians can consume eight worker-hours. For mobile work, route time must either be included in job time or deducted from available time, consistently.

Distance and access. Tolls, parking, travel labor and setup differ by location. The mobile travel-fee guide isolates that decision. Do not enter both a full travel allowance and the same vehicle expense again without checking what the allowance covers.

Repeat bookings. A maintenance-plan visit may have lower acquisition and a narrower service scope. Those savings can justify a different price. The maintenance-plan pricing guide keeps the recurring commitment separate from a one-off full detail.

Use actual supplier and booking records for these inputs. A competitor's price list can inform what customers see elsewhere, but it does not reveal their labor boundary, overhead or route. Your floor needs your own costs and delivery assumptions.

Three service scenarios

A repeat maintenance visit

Keep the $160 price, $12 supplies, $50 labor and $14 vehicle expense, but acquisition falls to $5 per completed visit. Cost before processing becomes $81. Contribution is $160 × 0.97 − $81 = $74.20.

Funding $4,000 requires 54 visits because 53 contribute $3,932.60 and 54 contribute $4,006.80. This fits the 56-job workload ceiling. The improvement relies on the lower acquisition being real and the service staying within two worker-hours; it is not a reason to silently expand the included scope.

A full detail with longer delivery time

Assume a full detail sells for $300 and takes four worker-hours. Paid labor is $100, supplies $25, vehicle expense $14 and acquisition $25. Cost before percentage fees is $164. Contribution is $300 × 0.97 − $164 = $127.

The $4,000 requirement needs 32 jobs. At 113.75 usable hours, only 28 whole four-hour jobs fit. Twenty-eight contribute $3,556, leaving $56 after the example's $3,500 overhead and owner pay. The bigger ticket does not meet the additional $500 target at this workload.

To fund $4,000 with 28 jobs, the price floor is ($164 + $4,000 ÷ 28) ÷ 0.97 = $316.3476, rounded upward to $316.35. A $320 quote leaves $146.40 and needs 28 jobs. Customer demand and actual route timing still need evidence.

An extra hour of interior work

A $180 maintenance quote requires one additional paid hour at $25 and $3 more supplies. Cost rises from $101 to $129. Holding the price leaves $180 × 0.97 − $129 = $45.60, down from $73.60.

To preserve $73.60 contribution, quote ($129 + $73.60) ÷ 0.97 = $208.8660, rounded upward to $208.87. A $210 revised quote leaves $74.70. But three worker-hours per job reduce the capacity ceiling to 37 whole jobs. Repricing one appointment does not repair a monthly plan built on two-hour work if every appointment now takes three.

Common mistakes

  • Posting a maintenance price without describing vehicle size and normal condition.
  • Omitting travel or pack-down from either costs or capacity.
  • Charging the same owner labor in direct wages and the monthly owner-pay goal.
  • Assuming a larger ticket automatically produces more contribution per hour.
  • Applying lower repeat-visit acquisition to every first-time customer.

FAQs

Are these current detailing market prices?

No, they are fictional USD worked examples. Replace them with your own costs and local demand evidence. The guide explains how to test a price rather than publishing a market survey.

Should I show a starting price?

Use one only when the customer can see the included vehicle category and service condition. Explain how additional work is quoted before acceptance. The price should describe an available scope rather than an undefined minimum.

Is contribution the same as profit?

Contribution is revenue after the listed variable job costs. Monthly overhead and owner income still need to be paid. The remaining result depends on completed jobs and the costs included.

How do I handle a heavily soiled car?

Estimate additional worker-hours and supplies before confirming the scope. Recalculate the contribution at the revised quote. Keep extra time in the capacity model as well as the cost model.

Can a repeat customer have a lower price?

Lower acquisition, tighter routing or a smaller maintenance scope can change the cost floor. Verify those savings and the appointment duration. A repeat booking does not remove all delivery costs.

What if I offer several services each month?

Use the actual mix of service contribution and worker-hours. Do not average prices alone while keeping the cheapest service cost. Confirm the mixed workload against the route and calendar.

Takeaways

  • Connect every price-list row to a defined scope.
  • Calculate contribution after full job costs and fees.
  • Check larger services against worker-hours, not ticket size alone.
  • Update prices or scope when actual jobs repeatedly overrun.
