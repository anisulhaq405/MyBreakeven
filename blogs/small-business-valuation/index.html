Small-Business Valuation: Methods and Assumption Ranges

A small-business valuation is a supported estimate of what a defined business interest is worth at a stated date. State whether the figure concerns operating assets, enterprise value or the owners’ equity. Compare methods on compatible earnings, cash and debt assumptions, and report a range with evidence instead of treating one multiple as a market price.

All numerical cases are original fictional USD teaching examples, not market benchmarks, customer results, offers or forecasts. Replace each assumption with dated evidence.

Define the business interest before calculating a value

Start with a written perimeter: the company or assets involved, percentage interest, valuation date, purpose and assumed transaction. A buyer of operating assets may not acquire the seller’s bank account or assume every debt. A share purchase may carry a different package of obligations. A planning estimate for a potential sale is not automatically suitable for a tax return, court dispute, secured loan or minority investment.

List what must remain in the business to generate the stated earnings: usable equipment, an ordinary inventory level, customer contracts, intellectual property, working capital and relevant permits. Identify owner-dependent relationships and agreements that might not transfer. Record separately whether property is included or leased. Otherwise an apparently higher offer may simply include more assets or require a different amount of cash at closing.

Use the accounting-equation guide to reconcile records, but do not equate book equity with sale value. A business can have modest recorded assets and valuable transferable customer relationships, or attractive reported assets that need replacement. Business expansion planning addresses whether a proposed additional operation works; valuation addresses the price and scope of an existing interest.

Normalize earnings with a reversible adjustment record

Consider a fictional service company reporting $52,000 annual profit before income tax. Its records include $6,000 interest, $8,000 depreciation and a verified $4,000 nonrecurring repair after an isolated event. The owner’s recorded wage is $10,000 below a supported replacement-manager cost. For this teaching case, an operating earnings bridge is $52,000 + $6,000 + $8,000 + $4,000 − $10,000 = $60,000 adjusted EBITDA. This is a proposed analytical basis, not an accepted appraisal result.

Retain the actual expense record and the reason for every adjustment. Do not add back ordinary repairs just because the next owner hopes to avoid them. Do not eliminate owner pay while also assuming the owner’s work continues without a replacement. Verify whether rent, related-party charges and customer concentration make the observed period unrepresentative. Compare three methods without averaging incompatible answers

An earnings-multiple approach applies a supported multiplier to a consistently defined earnings measure. If an analyst merely tests an invented 3×–4× range on the $60,000 adjusted EBITDA, the resulting enterprise-value sensitivity is $180,000–$240,000. Those multipliers are chosen assumptions, not published small-business transaction benchmarks. A real comparable requires evidence about transaction date, size, industry, growth, risk, earnings definition and which assets or liabilities changed hands.

An asset-based approach asks what the included assets and obligations are worth under the stated premise. A separate illustration with $180,000 supported asset value, including cash, and $50,000 included liabilities gives $130,000 net assets. This is not automatically the minimum price for a going concern, because liquidation costs, unrecorded obligations, goodwill and continued operating requirements can matter. A $130,000 net-asset figure cannot simply be averaged with enterprise values that exclude cash and debt.

A discounted cash-flow approach values explicitly timed future cash available to the selected capital providers. It depends on supported cash forecasts, a matching discount rate and a defensible continuation assumption. Cost of equity explains a required-return input; it does not determine the business sale price by itself.

Bridge enterprise value to equity on the same assumptions

Suppose the multiple illustration excludes surplus cash and interest-bearing debt. With $50,000 debt and $10,000 genuinely surplus cash, equity sensitivity becomes enterprise value − debt + surplus cash: $140,000–$200,000. Do not add the same cash a second time if the chosen asset perimeter already includes it. Ordinary operating cash needed to deliver the forecast may not be surplus, and an agreement can require a working-capital adjustment at closing.

Keep a debt-like-items schedule rather than assuming only the bank loan matters. Unpaid tax, litigation, leases, deferred customer obligations or a seller note can change an actual transaction analysis, depending on the agreement and applicable rules. They do not all receive the same automatic adjustment in every valuation. A qualified reviewer should decide which items belong in the chosen perimeter.

Explain whether an asking price is before or after adviser fees, transaction costs and tax. A business valued at $200,000 does not necessarily leave the owner $200,000 spendable cash. Link the proposed funding with a net-proceeds and repayment review; purchase affordability and seller proceeds are different calculations.

Check a cash-flow case and the source of uncertainty

For an independent enterprise cash-flow illustration, assume year-end unlevered cash flows of $40,000, $45,000 and $50,000, plus a $200,000 terminal value at the end of year three. At a deliberately assumed 20% annual discount rate, present value is $40,000/1.2 + $45,000/1.2² + ($50,000 + $200,000)/1.2³ = $209,259.26. On the same $50,000 debt and $10,000 surplus-cash bridge, illustrative equity value is $169,259.26.

The terminal amount contributes $115,740.74 of that enterprise value after discounting, so more than half the result comes from the continuation assumption. Do not hide this dependence behind a precise final number. Record separate changes to customer retention, manager replacement, maintenance investment and working capital, then rerun the model. A calculator output with cents is still an assumption-sensitive estimate.

Check whether customer-acquisition spending is necessary to sustain the forecast. Removing marketing from expenses while retaining all future customer growth inflates the cash story. Likewise, net revenue after returns and discounts provides a clearer starting point than a gross-billings number.

Prepare a decision record before using the range

Collect statements, tax records where appropriate, debt agreements, asset schedules, customer and supplier concentrations, employee obligations and the evidence behind adjustments. Reconcile earnings with cash movements and investigate unexplained differences. State the period, accounting basis and who prepared the records. The aim is a traceable conversation about the business, not a large unexplained spreadsheet.

Compare the asking price with a supported range only after matching the transaction perimeter. Record unresolved issues, the evidence owner, a deadline and how each issue could change value. Obtain qualified valuation, accounting and legal support when the purpose or transaction requires it. A method that is useful for an initial discussion may be inappropriate for a regulated or contested decision.

Frequently asked questions

Is book equity the value of a small business?

No. Book equity is an accounting residual. A valuation uses a stated date, interest and premise and may consider transferable earnings, assets, obligations and risk.

Can I use a universal revenue multiple?

No. A comparable needs consistent transaction scope, earnings definitions and dated evidence. The 3×–4× EBITDA figures here are invented sensitivities, not market benchmarks.

What is the difference between enterprise and equity value?

Under the stated convention, enterprise value concerns the operating business before the financing bridge. Equity value adjusts for relevant debt, surplus cash and agreement-specific items.

Should I add back all owner compensation?

No. Retain a supported cost for the work required to operate the business. Replacement management can reduce a proposed adjusted earnings figure.

Why does a terminal value matter so much?

It can represent a large share of a discounted cash-flow estimate. Show it separately and test the cash, growth, investment and discount assumptions supporting it.

Is this a professional appraisal?

No. It is an educational planning framework and original numerical illustration. The actual purpose, transaction and jurisdiction determine the appropriate professional work.
