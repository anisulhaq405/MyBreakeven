Bookkeeping for Small Business: A Monthly Record Workflow

Quick answer

Small-business bookkeeping turns supported transactions into consistent records and reconciled balances. A useful monthly routine collects evidence, posts transactions once, resolves differences and preserves a reviewed closing package.

Read the source-backed application and limits. Worked business amounts are fictional teaching examples.

All amounts and operating results below are original fictional teaching examples in USD. They are not customer results, industry averages or forecasts. Use your own dated records and applicable accounting policies.

Build the record around evidence rather than the bank feed

Keep an identifiable link between each posting and the underlying sale, supplier bill, receipt, payroll record or agreement. The IRS describes supporting business documents as the evidence behind entries. Its tax-record guidance is not a substitute for your financial-reporting policy, but it reinforces why a bank description alone may not explain what a transaction represents.

Assign a unique reference, reporting date, amount, currency, counterparty and account. Separate business and personal activity appropriately. A bank feed entry can match an invoice payment already recorded; importing it as another sale would duplicate income. Keep unidentified items in a visible investigation list rather than assigning convenient categories.

Frequency depends on transaction volume and commitments, not a universal deadline invented by this guide. A business with payroll this week may need cash review before the formal month-end close. The formal close should use a consistent cutoff across reports.

Reconcile a bank difference without inventing a plug

Original cash-reconciliation case, USD
Record / adjustmentAmountReason
Cash book before omitted bank fee$9,7508,000 opening + 6,200 receipts − 4,450 payments
Unrecorded bank fee−$50Post supported fee once
Adjusted cash book$9,700Agrees to adjusted bank evidence
Statement balance$9,850Statement at same month-end cutoff
Deposit in transit+$250Already in books; absent from bank
Outstanding payment−$400Already in books; absent from bank
Adjusted statement$9,7009,850 + 250 − 400

The $50 fee is missing from the books, so it needs a supported book entry. The $250 deposit and $400 outstanding payment are already recorded in the books; adding or subtracting them there again would duplicate their effects. Their role is to explain the statement timing. Both adjusted views reach $9,700.

An equal result does not validate every payment. Verify the recipient, amount and business purpose. Investigate old reconciling items and retain evidence of later clearing. Do not force unexplained differences into fees or owner equity.

Reconcile detail before accepting the report totals

In a separate customer subledger example, $3,000 opening receivables + $6,000 invoices − $5,200 allocated collections = $3,800 closing gross receivables. If the GL says $3,600, identify the $200 difference before treating the report as reconciled. Check credits, manual journals, duplicate invoices and unapplied receipts. This is independent of the bank example’s total receipts.

Use AR records and AP records for their respective workflows. Match asset cost and accumulated depreciation to an asset register. Check prepayment periods and accrued obligations. An account can reconcile mathematically while its valuation or classification still needs review.

Preserve the close and the unresolved-action list

Review unusual balances and changes against actual operations. Retain the trial balance, reconciliations, adjustment support and reports with their cutoff date. Assign an owner and deadline to each remaining issue. A lock date is useful only when corrections are still made through a controlled, documented process rather than silently changing prior reports.

The general ledger template supplies posting references and account-level balances; the accounting cycle explains adjustments and closing. The statement-analysis guide addresses interpretation after the records are prepared. Check the actual retention requirements for each document type; this article does not assert a single period for every business record.

Build a close package that another person can reproduce

The IRS recordkeeping guidance allows a system suited to the business, while requiring records that support the amounts reported. It does not require this guide’s particular spreadsheet, chart of accounts or monthly timetable. Keep the document that explains a transaction alongside its posting reference; a category selected in an app is not the underlying evidence.

For the $9,700 cash reconciliation, preserve the original statement, opening book balance, receipts and payments, the omitted $50 fee, and the $250/$400 timing items. The reviewer should be able to reach the same adjusted balance without asking the preparer to remember what happened. Retain subsequent clearing evidence for the timing items. If a payment remains outstanding unusually long, record the investigation rather than deleting it to make the schedule shorter.

A practical close package also contains customer and supplier detail, the bank reconciliation, asset and loan schedules, approved adjustments, the trial balance and the reports actually reviewed. Make the cutoff and version visible. Record who prepared and reviewed each reconciliation and which issues remain open. Retention is a separate decision: IRS retention guidance has different conditions for different records, so a blanket “discard everything after three years” instruction would be misleading. Consider other applicable contractual or legal requirements before disposal.

Frequently asked questions

Is the bank feed the whole bookkeeping system?

No. It does not by itself explain unpaid invoices, noncash adjustments or the purpose and period of every transaction.

Should deposits in transit be entered again?

Not if already recorded correctly. Use them to explain timing between books and statement.

Can I close with an unexplained difference?

Record it as unresolved and investigate; do not conceal it with an unsupported balancing entry.

Does this guide recommend accounting software?

No. It defines the evidence and reconciliation workflow independently of a product.

Must every business use the same chart of accounts?

No. The chart should support the business and reporting requirements, with consistent account identities and documented mappings.

Is a bank-feed category sufficient supporting evidence?

Not by itself. Retain the invoice, receipt, agreement or other evidence explaining the amount, purpose and reporting period.
