WACC: Weighted Average Cost of Capital

Published November 11, 2026Updated November 11, 2026

Calculate WACC from debt and equity weights, reconcile the tax assumption, and test sensitivity. See when a company-wide rate does not fit a project cash flow.

WACC is a weighted estimate of the required return across a firm’s financing sources. With only debt and common equity, WACC = [E / (D + E)] × Re + [D / (D + E)] × Rd × (1 − T). The weights, required returns and usable tax benefit must describe a consistent capital structure and valuation basis.

All numerical examples are original fictional teaching cases in USD, not market benchmarks or promised outcomes. Assumptions are stated beside the calculations; replace them with your own dated evidence.

Define the financing sources before using the formula

E is the relevant equity value; D is interest-bearing debt value. Re is the estimated required equity return, Rd is the relevant current debt cost and T represents the tax assumption applied to deductible interest. Market-value weights are normally used in the standard valuation framework, rather than mixing book equity with market debt without explanation.

Accounts payable is not automatically another interest-bearing capital component. If preferred shares, leases or other financing are material, their treatment needs explicit reconciliation. This guide uses only debt and common equity, so those two weights must sum to 100%. A spreadsheet that leaves 15% of capital unassigned has not estimated a complete weighted average.

Reconcile a two-source capital mix

The fictional case uses an as-of-date equity value of $600,000 and debt value of $400,000. The chosen Re is 12%, Rd is 7%, and T is 25%, with an assumed fully usable interest tax benefit. All rates and values are illustrative assumptions, not current market observations or a tax opinion. No preferred shares, leases or excess-cash adjustment is included.

After-tax debt cost is 7% × (1 − 25%) = 5.25%. WACC is 60% × 12% + 40% × 5.25% = 7.20% + 2.10% = 9.30%. Enter rates consistently: 12% is 0.12 in decimal arithmetic. The weighted debt contribution is 2.10 percentage points, not the full 5.25% debt rate.

Test whether the tax shield is actually supported

The 25% input is not a universal business tax rate. Entity form, losses, deduction limits, jurisdiction and timing can alter whether and when interest reduces tax. If the case has no usable tax benefit, set that benefit to zero for the modeled horizon: WACC becomes 60% × 12% + 40% × 7% = 10.00%, rather than 9.30%.

Do not both reduce the debt rate for the tax shield and subtract the same financing benefit again from an otherwise unlevered cash-flow forecast without a consistent framework. A business with uncertain taxable income needs an evidence-based treatment, not a convenient lower rate. Record the source and date of any professional tax assumption used in a real model.

The last row is a mechanical sensitivity, not a prediction that more borrowing makes financing cheaper. Leverage can change equity risk, debt pricing and distress costs. Holding Re and Rd constant while changing the weights does not capture those effects. Present it as a limited scenario, not an optimized capital structure.

Match the rate to the cash being valued

WACC can be a starting point for discounting unlevered cash available to the firm when the project has comparable operating risk and the assumed financing policy remains relevant. Equity-only cash flows need an equity required return instead. The cost-of-equity guide explains that component and its model assumptions.

Currency, nominal-versus-real treatment, tax basis and period length must also match. A 9.3% annual nominal USD rate should not be copied into a monthly real cash-flow model unchanged. For unusually risky expansion, a stable existing-business WACC can understate the project hurdle. Capital budgeting shows how a rate assumption affects project comparisons.

Handle private-company values as estimates, not quotes

A private firm may not have an observable traded equity value or beta. Document how values and target weights were estimated, why comparable businesses are relevant and how sensitive the result is to reasonable alternatives. Owner capital still has an opportunity cost even when the business pays no dividend this year.

Do not plug retained earnings from the balance sheet into E and describe the result as a market-value WACC. A range can be more honest than a point estimate when inputs are uncertain. Keep debt terms, valuation assumptions, review date and exclusions alongside the calculation so a later reviewer can reproduce it. WACC is a required-return estimate; it is not a promised borrowing rate, accounting expense or guaranteed realized return.

Frequently asked questions

What is WACC?

It is a weighted estimate of required returns across the firm’s financing sources, often debt, common equity and any preferred capital.

What is WACC in the fictional example?

9.30% using 60% equity at 12%, 40% debt at 7%, and an assumed fully usable 25% interest tax benefit.

Why multiply debt cost by one minus the tax rate?

The standard formula models an interest tax benefit. Its applicability and timing need support; it is not automatic for every entity or loss-making firm.

Is a lower WACC always achieved by adding debt?

No. Changing leverage can change required equity return, debt cost and financial risk. A constant-rate weight sensitivity is not a financing recommendation.

Can I discount equity cash flows with WACC?

Not without reconciling the framework. Equity cash flows normally use the cost of equity, while WACC is used with matching firm-level cash flows.

Is book equity an observable market value?

No. For a private business, values and target weights need a documented estimation method and sensitivity analysis.
