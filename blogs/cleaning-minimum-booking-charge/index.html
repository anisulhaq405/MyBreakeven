Cleaning Minimum Booking Charge: Cost, Time and Route

Published October 6, 2026Updated October 6, 2026

Published by MyBreakeven. Editorial standards and corrections. Report a calculation or content issue to support@mybreakeven.com.

Build a cleaning minimum charge from labor, travel, supplies and fees. Test short visits, repeat routes, owner pay and whole-visit delivery capacity.

CleaningCleaning Minimum Booking Charge

A cleaning minimum booking charge should cover the costs of turning up plus the contribution you need from that slot. In this example, a short visit costs $58 before card fees; a $125 minimum leaves $63.25 toward monthly overhead and owner pay. Charging by cleaning time alone misses travel, access and setup. All amounts below are fictional USD assumptions, not local market rates or a promise that customers will accept the price.

Quick answer

Add paid cleaning labor, paid travel and setup, supplies, vehicle expense and acquisition cost for the smallest visit you accept. Add the contribution required from that booking, then divide by one minus your payment fee rate. Check the resulting minimum against the number of short visits your route can actually deliver.

Build the minimum from a complete visit

A customer asks for a one-hour kitchen and bathroom clean. You pay one cleaner $24 per worker-hour, including the employment costs included in your own estimate. The cleaner spends one hour cleaning and another half-hour traveling, gaining access, unloading and packing away. Paid job labor is therefore 1.5 × $24 = $36.

Consumables cost $4, vehicle expense and parking cost $8, and acquisition costs average $10 per completed first visit. Total non-percentage variable cost is $36 + $4 + $8 + $10 = $58. Travel labor and vehicle expense are separate costs: one pays the worker's time, the other pays for operating the vehicle.

The owner plans $1,200 of monthly overhead, $2,400 of owner pay and $600 of additional profit. The funding requirement is $4,200. Suppose the route can support 70 completed visits with this scope and customers can be found for those visits. Required contribution is $4,200 ÷ 70 = $60 per visit.

With a 3% fee on the collected service price, the minimum that meets this plan is ($58 + $60) ÷ 0.97 = $121.6495 before rounding. Round upward to $121.65, or choose a simple quoted minimum such as $125. At $125, the fee is $3.75 and contribution is $125 − $58 − $3.75 = $63.25.

Seventy visits produce $4,427.50 of contribution. After $1,200 overhead and $2,400 owner pay, $827.50 remains before taxes and other unmodeled expenses. The $600 goal is met under these assumptions. It is still necessary to test demand: a mathematically sufficient price does not fill the route.

This is a booking minimum, not an hourly wage multiplied by the time inside the property. Describe what the minimum includes, such as one cleaner, defined rooms and a normal-condition visit. An unusually dirty property or extra work needs a revised scope before confirmation. The house-cleaning pricing guide covers a broader quote.

Four variables that change the floor

Travel and access time. A small job on an existing route differs from a standalone visit across town. Record departure, arrival, entry and departure from the property. If parking or key collection routinely adds time, include it in the standard scope rather than calling every occurrence an exception.

Visit frequency. A returning customer may not require another $10 acquisition cost at every appointment. Use a cost per completed visit derived from the chosen customer period. Do not remove acquisition because the booking came through a referral if you paid a referral reward or spent staff time following it up.

Paid labor commitments. This example treats labor as varying with completed visits. If you guarantee a weekly wage regardless of bookings, put the committed amount in fixed costs and only incremental labor in the visit costs. You still count the full worker-hours for capacity. Mixing both methods overstates cost.

What occupies the slot. One hour cleaning plus half an hour outside the property consumes 1.5 worker-hours. Two cleaners working together for one hour consume two cleaning worker-hours before travel. A customer-facing duration is not interchangeable with paid team time.

A minimum can also depend on condition and equipment needs. Carrying a carpet machine into an upstairs apartment differs from wiping a small kitchen. Keep scope categories few enough for customers to understand, but different enough that the cost estimate describes the promised work.

Three practical booking scenarios

A repeat visit on the same route

Assume travel and setup fall to 0.25 hours and acquisition falls to $2 per completed visit. Labor becomes 1.25 × $24 = $30. With $4 supplies and $4 vehicle expense, total variable cost before percentage fees is $40.

To leave the same $60 contribution, the price floor is ($40 + $60) ÷ 0.97 = $103.0928, rounded upward to $103.10. A $105 repeat-visit minimum leaves $105 × 0.97 − $40 = $61.85. The lower minimum comes from a defined cost saving. Confirm that the shorter travel assumption describes the route before offering it.

An isolated visit priced at $95

Retain the original $58 cost. At $95, contribution is $95 × 0.97 − $58 = $34.15. Covering $4,200 now needs 123 visits, because 122 × $34.15 = $4,166.30 and 123 × $34.15 = $4,200.45.

One worker with 35 available hours per week has 35 × 52 ÷ 12 = 151.67 monthly hours. At 70% utilization, usable job time is 106.17 hours. Dividing by 1.5 hours allows only 70 whole visits. Even a full route of these visits cannot fund the plan. Seventy contribute $2,390.50, leaving a $1,209.50 shortfall after overhead and owner pay.

A two-hour visit under the same minimum policy

The longer visit uses 2.5 paid hours including travel and setup, or $60 of labor. Assume $6 supplies, $8 vehicle expense and $10 acquisition. Cost is $84. Charging the same $125 leaves only $37.25 after the fee.

To retain $60 contribution, the floor is ($84 + $60) ÷ 0.97 = $148.4536, so round upward to $148.46. A $150 quote leaves $61.50. That price still earns less contribution per worker-hour than the short visit: $61.50 ÷ 2.5 = $24.60 compared with $63.25 ÷ 1.5 = $42.17. Compare the full monthly service mix before using the minimum as a universal price.

Check the route before publishing the policy

Use a recent week's completed visits to build a route sheet. Keep cleaning time, travel time, access delay and billable extras in separate columns. A single average can hide several isolated jobs that make the rest of the route look slow. Recalculate after removing those jobs or quoting them differently.

The recurring versus one-time cleaning guide helps distinguish appointment economics. The monthly cleaning expenses guide helps check the overhead total. Neither replaces actual visit records.

Write the minimum into the quote before the customer books. Explain included work and how added rooms or condition changes are approved. Do not promise a one-hour finish when the minimum describes a service scope rather than a stopwatch limit. Clear scope gives the crew a usable delivery instruction and reduces disputes over what was bought.

How to run your own numbers

In the cleaning business break-even calculator, enter $125 price, $4 materials, $36 labor, $8 other variable expense and $10 acquisition, with a 3% fee. Add $1,200 overhead, $2,400 owner pay and $600 target profit. For capacity use one worker, 35 weekly hours, 70% utilization and 1.5 worker-hours per visit. Other currencies are supported. The model requires 67 visits and permits 70; it does not predict bookings or optimize your route.

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying one hour inside the home by the wage while leaving paid travel out.
  • Entering guaranteed payroll in overhead and again in every visit's labor cost.
  • Offering the first-visit minimum on repeat jobs without checking acquisition and route differences.
  • Applying the short-visit minimum to longer work with more supplies and labor.
  • Treating the 70-visit capacity estimate as evidence of customer demand.

FAQs

Can I charge a minimum even for a tiny job?

You can use a minimum to describe the smallest service your business offers. Explain it in the quote before booking and state the included scope. Customer acceptance still needs to be tested in your own market.

Should the minimum include owner pay?

Include owner pay once in the funding requirement if the business must support that income. This example shows it separately from operating overhead and extra profit. Do not also charge the same owner labor through per-visit wages.

What if I work alone?

Use a consistent labor boundary for your own time. You may put your income goal in owner pay and set direct owner labor to zero for cash planning. Keep all delivery hours in capacity even when no employee wage is paid.

Is travel labor the same as mileage expense?

No, paid travel time and vehicle operating expense are different items. Include each actual cost once. Do not add a mileage allowance and the same fuel expense again without checking what the allowance covers.

Should every customer receive the same minimum?

Use clear scope or route categories when costs differ materially. A close repeat visit and an isolated first visit need not have identical economics. Avoid changing the price after acceptance without agreeing on changed work.

Does a higher minimum guarantee more profit?

No, completed bookings may fall when the price changes. Compare contribution at the actual booking volume, not only at the previous volume. Also measure time freed for other work.

Takeaways

  • Cost the whole visit, including paid travel and access.
  • Connect the minimum to a realistic completed-booking count.
  • Keep committed payroll and variable labor separate.
  • Quote longer work from its own scope and worker-hours.
