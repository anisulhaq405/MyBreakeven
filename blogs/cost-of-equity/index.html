Cost of Equity: Inputs, Formula and Assumptions

Cost of equity is the return equity investors require for taking the business’s equity risk. A common estimate is CAPM: Re = risk-free rate + beta × equity market risk premium. It is a model-dependent required return, not a guaranteed gain or a cash interest bill owed to shareholders.

All numerical examples are original fictional teaching cases in USD, not market benchmarks or promised outcomes. Assumptions are stated beside the calculations; replace them with your own dated evidence.

Distinguish required return from accounting profit

Equity holders receive the residual outcome after other claims. They may accept retaining earnings rather than receiving cash now, but that does not make the funding free. Cost of equity represents the return required for bearing that risk; actual returns can be above or below it, including a loss.

It is not the same as historical return on equity, a net-profit margin or the dividend yield alone. Those measures describe different denominators and cash patterns. Use an equity required return with matching cash flows to equity holders; combine it with other financing costs only within a consistent WACC calculation.

Define each CAPM input and its evidence

Beta, βEquity sensitivity to systematic market riskRecord comparable business, sample, leverage and market definition

If a source gives total expected market return Rm rather than a premium, calculate ERP = Rm − Rf before multiplying by beta. Adding Rm as though it were already a premium counts the risk-free component incorrectly. Beta is not a volatility percentage or a direct measure of every operational risk.

Calculate an original CAPM example

Assume a fictional annual nominal USD model with Rf = 4%, beta = 1.2 and ERP = 5%. These are deliberately selected teaching inputs, not today’s Treasury yield or an observed company estimate. Re = 4% + 1.2 × 5% = 10%. The beta-adjusted premium contributes six percentage points; the risk-free component contributes four.

If the total expected market return were presented as 9%, the same result follows: ERP = 9% − 4% = 5%. The incorrect expression 4% + 1.2 × 9% would produce 14.8%, a different number caused by a definition error. Retain the input labels in the worksheet, not just three unlabeled cells.

Use dividend growth as a separate cross-check when it fits

For a firm with a sustainable constant dividend-growth assumption, a different model is Re = next-period dividend / current share price + growth rate. A fictional current annual dividend of $2, next-year growth of 3%, and a $30 share price give next dividend $2.06. Re = $2.06 / $30 + 3% = 9.8667%, or about 9.87%. Using the current $2 dividend without growing it first would produce 9.67%.

This model is unsuitable when the business has no meaningful sustainable dividend policy, growth is unstable or the price and distribution assumptions are inconsistent. Do not average 10% CAPM and 9.87% dividend growth mechanically to manufacture precision. Investigate what assumptions differ and whether both methods actually describe the business.

Explain a private-company estimate without inventing a traded beta

A private business does not automatically have an observable market beta or share price. An analyst may use comparable firms, adjust for leverage and evaluate business differences; those choices need documentation and appropriate expertise. A single industry beta copied without its date, capital structure and selection rationale is weak evidence.

Small-firm, country, liquidity or company-specific adjustments are sometimes considered in practice, but adding arbitrary premiums can double count risks already reflected in cash-flow scenarios or other inputs. This introductory case does not prescribe such add-ons. Show a sensitivity range and make the unresolved assumptions visible rather than presenting a personal target return as a market-derived estimate.

Match the estimate to the decision and review it

A $100 one-year equity cash flow discounted at 10% has present value $100 / 1.10 = $90.91. Discounting the same flow at the case’s highest sensitivity rate of 13.6% gives $88.03. The difference illustrates why the required-return assumption matters; it does not value an entire company or establish an investment recommendation.

Use capital budgeting to connect project cash, funding and risk. Record rate currency, as-of date, horizon, source methodology and whether the cash flows are nominal, real, levered or unlevered. Revisit the estimate after material business-risk, financing or market-input changes. A documented model is more useful than an unexplained “standard” percentage.

Frequently asked questions

What is the CAPM cost-of-equity formula?

Re = Rf + beta × equity market risk premium. If given total expected market return, subtract Rf to obtain the premium first.

What is the example’s cost of equity?

10%, from fictional inputs of 4% risk-free rate, 1.2 beta and 5% market risk premium.

Is cost of equity the same as dividend yield?

No. A constant-growth dividend model combines next-period dividend yield with expected sustainable growth; CAPM uses systematic risk inputs.

Can retained earnings have zero equity cost?

Retained earnings still have an opportunity cost to owners. An absence of current dividends does not imply shareholders require no return.

Can a private business use a public-company beta unchanged?

It needs a documented comparability and leverage assessment. A public beta is not automatically an observed beta for a private company.

Is the estimated required return guaranteed?

No. It is an assumption-dependent estimate used in a valuation or decision framework. Actual returns may be much lower, including losses.
