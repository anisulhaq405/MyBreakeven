A selected withdrawal rate converts spending into a portfolio target; it does not guarantee that withdrawals will last. Retirement length, return order, fees, taxes, healthcare, accessible assets and willingness to change spending all need a separate drawdown plan.

Define the withdrawal rule before selecting a percentage

An initial withdrawal fraction divides the first year’s portfolio withdrawal by the starting retirement portfolio. A fixed-real rule then adjusts that dollar withdrawal for inflation. Withdrawing the same percentage of each year’s changing balance is a different policy: income falls when assets fall. Neither is described adequately by writing “4%” without a rule.

Same returns, different order, different remaining money

Start with a fictional $100,000 portfolio. Withdraw $10,000 at the beginning of each of two years. Ignore fees, taxes and inflation to isolate return order. Both paths use the same −20% and +25% annual returns.

Independent two-year sequence demonstration; USD
PathYear 1 after withdrawal and returnYear 2 after withdrawal and return
Loss first(100,000 − 10,000) × 0.80 = 72,000(72,000 − 10,000) × 1.25 = 77,500
Gain first(100,000 − 10,000) × 1.25 = 112,500(112,500 − 10,000) × 0.80 = 82,000

Both portfolios paid the same $20,000 in withdrawals, yet the second ends $4,500 higher. Without withdrawals, 0.80 × 1.25 = 1, and both would end at $100,000. Selling assets early reduces the capital available for recovery. The $10,000 withdrawal is deliberately a teaching amount, not a recommended retirement policy.

Plan for the full horizon and uncertain lifetime

Retiring at 40 can require funding far more years than retiring at 65. A withdrawal finding for a specified horizon, asset mix and modeled success definition cannot simply be relabelled a lifetime guarantee. Include the possibility of living longer, a survivor’s changed expenses and late-life care. A smooth average return suppresses the return-order effect shown above.

The Coast FIRE calculator is even narrower: it projects untouched retirement assets to a chosen future age. Its zero-contribution milestone does not test the years after withdrawals begin. Compare FIRE, Coast and part-time-income scenarios before choosing which result to use.

Build a spending boundary that includes the actual bills

Separate essential expenses from discretionary spending and dated one-off costs. If annual living expenses are $40,000 but another $6,000 of withdrawal-funded taxes and $4,000 of healthcare are omitted, the required withdrawal is $50,000. At the same selected 4% fraction, the arithmetic target rises from $1 million to $1.25 million. These costs are fictional, not tax or insurance estimates.

For taxable withdrawals, after-tax spending and gross withdrawals can differ. Do not apply one generic tax rate to every account or treat all benefits as tax-free. Include fees once: if a return assumption is already net of the same investment expense, do not subtract it again.

Separate total wealth from accessible retirement funding

Write a dated inventory of liquid funds, account types, expected benefits and access restrictions. Home equity does not pay a bill without a real sale or financing plan. An emergency reserve already committed to short-term needs should not also be counted as invested retirement capital. Early US retirement-plan distributions can carry additional tax unless an applicable exception is met; exceptions and account rules require individual verification, not a blanket assumption.

Specify what changes after a poor market year

A flexible policy might reduce a named discretionary expense, postpone a purchase or revisit withdrawals at an agreed review point. It helps only if the budget actually has room to change. Write the trigger and affordable floor instead of promising that all spending can be cut. Test loss-first paths, higher costs and delayed other income separately; do not multiply arbitrary stress tests into a supposed success percentage.

Common mistakes

Using a withdrawal rate as an expected investment return; comparing fixed-real with percentage-of-balance policies; ignoring a longer horizon; counting restricted assets as immediately spendable; and interpreting a target age as a retirement authorization can each mislead. Preserve the base scenario and document changed assumptions.

Frequently asked questions

Does the FIRE calculator prove a withdrawal rate is safe?

No. It is an accumulation projection with a selected target fraction. It does not model variable market returns or retirement drawdowns.

Why can the same average returns lead to different outcomes?

Withdrawals change how much remains invested between returns. In the worked loss-first path, less capital participates in the recovery.

Should taxes and healthcare be in my spending input?

Include the portion you expect the portfolio to fund. Record other income and its dates separately, and avoid counting a cost twice.

Is a withdrawal percentage the same as a return assumption?

No. A withdrawal rule describes cash taken from assets; a return assumption describes their growth or loss. They have different roles in the plan.
