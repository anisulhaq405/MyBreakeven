Accrued Expenses: Recognize, Reconcile and Settle Unpaid Costs

Accrued expenses are costs incurred but not yet paid. When the cost and obligation have not already been recorded, a supported cutoff adjustment recognizes them; the later invoice or payment must clear the balance without counting the same expense twice.

All amounts and operating results below are original fictional teaching examples in USD. They are not customer results, industry averages or forecasts. Use your own dated records and applicable accounting policies.

Start with the consumed service and reporting cutoff

Accrual recognition follows the transaction facts and applicable reporting policy, rather than waiting automatically for a supplier invoice. OpenStax’s adjusting-entry discussion illustrates an expense paired with a payable when an incurred cost remains unrecorded. This guide applies that basic mechanism to a service business; it does not determine whether a complex legal claim, uncertain bonus or tax obligation satisfies its own recognition requirements.

Ask what work was received, which dates it covers and what evidence supports the amount. A future service in an unsigned proposal is not an incurred expense merely because management expects to buy it. A bill already entered in accounts payable also needs no second expense accrual. Distinguish an omitted liability from a different label for an existing one.

Build an accrual record that another preparer can follow

Use a unique reference, counterparty, service period, expense account, liability account, estimate basis, supporting record and responsible reviewer. For payroll, retain approved work dates and the unpaid portion. For utilities, use relevant consumption evidence and the supported billing basis. For professional work, retain the accepted delivery or documented service performed, not only an annual purchase budget.

Search both the general ledger and supplier subledger before posting. An approved $700 supplier bill already recorded as expense and accounts payable stays outside the new-accrual total below. The payment can still be outstanding without creating another $700 expense. Review supplier statements and invoices received after cutoff as completeness evidence, with their actual service dates.

Recognize three original November accruals

Every amount in this example is an original fictional USD teaching input. At November 30, six wage days have been earned at $200 per day but remain unpaid. November utilities consumed are supported at $450. A consultant has completed accepted November work priced at $900, with the invoice to arrive in December. Assume all three are operating expenses, no opening accruals, no payroll withholdings, sales tax, foreign currency or other adjustments.

The total is $1,200 + $450 + $900 = $2,550. Relative to omitting these entries, November expense rises $2,550, November profit falls $2,550 and liabilities rise $2,550. The posting itself changes no cash balance. It is not a payment and does not establish that cash is available for the due dates.

Allocate the later payment to the correct periods

Suppose December payroll pays $2,000 for ten days: the six November days plus four December days. Without a reversing entry, debit Wages Payable $1,200, debit December Wage Expense $800 and credit Cash $2,000. November’s expense stays $1,200; December receives only its four-day $800 portion.

Assume the final utilities invoice is exactly $450 and consulting invoice exactly $900. If invoice processing transfers these accruals into supplier payables, debit their accrual liabilities and credit Accounts Payable, without another expense. Their payments subsequently debit Accounts Payable and credit Cash. A direct debit to the accrued liability on payment is another workflow; select the actual system path and reconcile it, rather than posting both alternatives.

Compare an optional reversal with direct settlement

OpenStax’s reversing-entry appendix describes reversal as an optional aid for specified accruals. For the wage example, a December 1 reversal debits Wages Payable $1,200 and credits Wage Expense $1,200. The payroll system then debits Wage Expense $2,000 and credits Cash $2,000. December’s net wage expense is still $800: $2,000 less the $1,200 reversal.

These are alternative methods for the same obligation. Do not reverse the accrual and also debit that cleared payable in the payment entry. That would remove the liability twice. Track which exact journal is scheduled to reverse, its date and whether the normal invoice or payroll feed will use expense. A temporary expense credit after reversal is not a cash receipt or an approved cost reduction.

Investigate a changed invoice before choosing the period

An estimate needs a documented update process. In an independent variation, the utility invoice arrives before November statements are finalized and provides better evidence that the November service cost was $480, rather than $450. Assuming that evidence belongs to the same recognition period, increase November utility expense and payable by $30. The revised three-item November accrual becomes $2,580.

Do not automatically call every difference a December expense, and do not rewrite issued statements without assessing the applicable correction and subsequent-event policy. Retain the original estimate, new document, timing and reason for the adjustment. An estimate revision, duplicate invoice and prior-period error are different diagnoses; the invoice date alone does not resolve them.

A liability-to-payables transfer changes the ledger location, not the overall obligation. Reconcile both accounts together so a transferred item is not reported as an unpaid accrual and a supplier payable simultaneously. New December accruals, if any, would require separate records; the zero closing figure assumes none within this deliberately bounded case.

Use the accrual schedule as a close and payment handoff

At each close, check service cutoff, source evidence, duplicate references, estimate approval, reversal settings and later settlement. Give unresolved items an owner and next evidence date. Preserve the final record so the next preparer can understand why the balance exists, not just copy last month’s amount.

The accrual-accounting overview explains revenue, cash and prepayments together. Use the payable workflow for supplier approval and the ledger record for journal references. The accounting cycle connects the reviewed adjustment to statements; this guide’s task is the supported unpaid expense and its later clearance.

Frequently asked questions

Does an accrued expense require an invoice first?

Not necessarily. The service or cost must meet the applicable recognition requirements, and the amount needs support; an invoice date alone does not determine the expense period.

Is an unpaid bill already in accounts payable another accrual?

It is already an obligation. Do not post the same expense again merely to rename an accounts-payable balance as an accrual.

Are reversing entries mandatory?

No. The case compares direct settlement with an optional specified reversal; both reach the same period expenses when implemented consistently.

Why is December wage expense only $800 on a $2,000 payment?

Six $200 days were already recognized in November. The payment covers those $1,200 plus four December days worth $800.

What if the actual invoice differs from the estimate?

Investigate the service period, evidence and statement timing. The guide’s separate $480 variation updates November by $30 before finalization; it does not prescribe every difference as a current-period expense.

Does the accrual entry reduce cash immediately?

No. It records expense and a liability. The later payment changes cash and clears the appropriate liability.
