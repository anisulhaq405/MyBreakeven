This restaurant budget template pairs an editable Excel workbook with a monthly planning example. Use it to budget sales, food costs, loaded payroll and recurring overhead, then compare actual results with your original plan.

A restaurant budget should tell you how many sales must arrive this month and what remains after food, labor, occupancy and owner pay. Start with orders or covers and an average check, then budget costs on the same monthly basis. Compare actual results with that plan as the month progresses. A lease budget written only as percentages cannot tell you whether Tuesday service will pay for a guaranteed shift.

Quick answer: Forecast monthly orders and average check, subtract variable food and transaction costs, then subtract the costs due even when orders are slow. For example, 2,000 orders at $25 bring $50,000 sales; at $9 variable cost each, contribution is $32,000. With $27,000 fixed costs, the example leaves $5,000 operating profit. Make your own budget using quoted costs.

How to budget a restaurant in five steps

  1. Choose the calendar month or accounting period, then forecast orders or covers by daypart and channel. Multiply each by the expected average check.
  2. Use inventory records for food and beverage cost, and record channel-dependent fees and packaging separately.
  3. Build paid hours by role, including employer costs. Separate guaranteed shifts from hours added for incremental orders.
  4. Add occupancy and other recurring commitments, counting owner compensation once. Keep equipment, deposits and financing payments in a separate cash schedule.
  5. Save the original budget. Compare actual results each week, explain the largest dollar variances, and update a separate forecast.

The workbook example below follows its stated simplified cost boundary. Add any missing channel costs before using it as your own break-even model. A target percentage cannot replace the minimum staffing and payments your operation actually needs.

The monthly restaurant budget, line by line

Start with food and beverage revenue separately. Estimate covers by daypart, open days and average spend, not a single hopeful annual growth rate. Then enter food and beverage cost of goods sold (COGS), guaranteed payroll including employer costs, rent, utilities, marketing, owner pay and other fixed charges. Put opening equipment and deposits in a cash schedule; they are not the same as this month's cost of serving a meal. The SBA break-even guide explains why fixed and variable costs need separate treatment for break-even.

A simple test uses 2,000 orders at $25 average spend: sales are $50,000. Ingredients, packaging and payment fees of $9 an order total $18,000, leaving $32,000 contribution. Assume rent, guaranteed labor, insurance, owner pay and other fixed costs total $27,000. Operating profit is $5,000 before taxes and financing principal. Required orders to cover those fixed costs are $27,000 ÷ ($25 − $9), rounded up to 1,688 orders. At 26 open days, that is about 65 orders daily. Compare the required volume with your actual service capacity.

When the workbook cannot report a break-even threshold

The contribution calculation requires positive sales. If sales are zero, the workbook shows No sales; if sales less COGS is zero or negative, it shows No positive contribution instead of a zero or negative break-even target. Adding volume at that contribution does not fund positive monthly commitments. Complete inputs means a required amount is blank or nonnumeric, while Check inputs identifies a negative entered amount. Enter a genuine zero explicitly. The break-even variance is Not comparable unless both columns have numeric thresholds. A valid zero threshold is possible when all committed costs are explicitly zero and contribution is positive.

These checks do not validate demand or add omitted costs. The workbook remains a simplified operating model; use a dated cash forecast for payment timing and check service capacity separately.

Use a budget that changes when the month changes

Your monthly budget is the original target. Update a rolling forecast when bookings, sales mix, staff hours or ingredient invoices change; keep the original target to measure the difference. A $3,000 favorable sales variance may still leave you behind if a delivery channel carries $2,000 extra commissions. Separate the variance in sales volume from the variance in average ticket.

Include payroll taxes and employer insurance in labor, and split minimum scheduled hours from extra hours needed for a busy service. Treat fixed rent and software differently from per-order processing fees. If your supplier prices rise, adjust the cost per plate rather than multiplying the entire restaurant's expenses by the same inflation number. The budget is only useful when its expense categories match invoices and the owner can update it each week.

Three budget outcomes from the same dining room

Base case: 2,000 orders × $25 = $50,000 sales. Variable cost is 2,000 × $9 = $18,000. Contribution is $32,000; less $27,000 fixed costs leaves $5,000.

Slow month: 1,600 orders at the same ticket give $40,000 sales. Variable cost is $14,400, leaving $25,600 contribution. With the same guaranteed shifts and rent, the restaurant loses $1,400. Changing the order count alone changes the answer; copying last month's percentage profit would hide that loss.

Discounted delivery mix: 2,200 orders at an average $23 ticket give $50,600 sales. If packaging, commissions and food raise blended variable cost to $11 per order, contribution is $26,400 and the business loses $600 after fixed costs. Higher order count did not repair the weaker contribution. Check channel mix before praising a sales increase.

Operating budget: original target, flexible comparison and cash plan

An operating budget estimates same-period revenue and operating expenses. An annual operating budget can be built from monthly sales drivers, expense behavior and seasonal schedules rather than spreading every line equally across twelve months. Keep the original approved target unchanged so the comparison remains auditable.

At month end, compare actual results with a budget flexed to actual activity using original rates. This distinguishes fewer orders from unfavorable ingredients or staffing costs. The operating-budget result is not the bank balance: equipment purchases, loan principal and collection/payment timing require a separate cash budget.

Connect the restaurant budget to its supporting schedules

A restaurant operating budget is part of a wider master budget. Keep the sales plan, purchasing and staffing needs, payment dates, equipment spending and financing connected. Common budgeting mistakes

Using purchases as food cost. Inventory bought in the last week may not be used until next month; reconcile opening inventory, purchases and closing inventory. Ignoring owner hours. Profit improves on paper when the owner works a full schedule without a wage, even if that arrangement is unsustainable.

Changing the target when results disappoint. Keep budget and revised forecast side by side. Budgeting labor as a flat percentage. A two-person minimum shift costs money even when no one walks in. Mixing sales tax with revenue. Remitted tax is not operating sales; keep your accounting basis consistent.

Diagnose a restaurant budget variance before cutting costs

Keep the original budget intact. Then ask three separate questions: did you serve the planned number of orders, did each order leave the planned contribution, and did monthly committed costs change? A lower food bill can simply mean fewer customers; it does not prove purchasing improved.

Match each budget line to a source record

  • Sales: reconcile POS net sales by channel and service period. Keep customer taxes, tips and marketplace settlements distinct from the restaurant's sales.
  • Ingredients used: reconcile opening stock plus purchases minus closing stock, with a consistent scope for transfers, credits and waste. Buying a month's stock is different from using it that month.
  • Labor: reconcile the roster, paid hours and employer costs. Keep committed staffing in monthly costs and genuinely additional order-driven labor in direct costs; do not count the same shift in both.
  • Delivery and payment fees: reconcile each channel's merchant statement. Do not add a second processing charge when the contracted fee already includes it.
  • Overhead and cash: compare bills with the reporting period, then separately schedule their payment dates. Equipment purchases, loan principal and owner drawings need a cash plan even when outside the operating result.

Investigate the largest dollar movement first, record its evidence and assign one action with a review date. For example, inspect portion and waste records before calling the $1 direct-cost increase supplier inflation. Keep the budget versus actual report for period review and the 13-week cash forecast for payment timing.

What to do after the first month

Compare budget with actual by food, beverages, labor and fixed categories. If food cost ran high, identify whether portions, purchase prices, waste or theft changed; do not assume the menu price is wrong. If sales fell, compare order counts by daypart and channel with what you expected. Use those observations to forecast the next month, then test whether the revised daily order target can actually be served.

  • Build sales from orders and average checks.
  • Keep guaranteed labor and per-sale costs distinct.
  • Compare actuals with the original budget before updating the forecast.
  • Maintain a separate cash schedule for opening and financing payments.

Build an annual restaurant budget from seasonal monthly plans

A restaurant annual budget should add twelve dated monthly plans rather than multiply its busiest month by twelve. Keep covers or completed orders, net average sale, food usage, loaded payroll and other commitments visible in each month. Use the same reporting scope when comparing a monthly restaurant budget example with actual results.

FAQs

What belongs in a restaurant monthly budget?

Sales by channel, cost of goods sold, loaded labor, occupancy, owner pay, utilities, marketing and other recurring expenses. Add notes for costs that are one-time or paid on a different schedule.

How do I calculate restaurant break-even from the budget?

Divide monthly fixed costs by contribution per order. In the worked example, $27,000 divided by $16 requires 1,688 whole orders, before a profit target.

Should restaurant payroll be budgeted as a percentage of sales?

Use the actual minimum schedule and employer burden first. A percentage can help compare periods, but it does not tell you what a slow Tuesday shift costs.

What is the difference between budget and forecast?

The budget is the approved target for the period. A forecast is your best current expectation after you learn more; preserving both lets you explain variances.

Can I use last month’s food invoices as COGS?

Not without checking inventory. Beginning inventory plus purchases minus ending inventory is the period cost of goods sold under a consistent inventory method.

Is the downloadable sheet a cash-flow forecast?

No. It compares operating revenue and costs in one month. Plan deposits, debt principal, equipment purchases and timing of collections separately.

Assumptions and cost boundary

Four average tickets ($20, $25, $30, $35) × five food costs ($4, $6, $8, $10, $12 per order). Every case adds $2 packaging and a 4% payment fee on its own ticket. Acquisition and variable labor are explicitly zero; guaranteed payroll is included in the $23,000 monthly overhead, with $4,000 additional owner pay and zero extra profit goal. Monthly funding is $27,000; open days are 26. Count payroll and owner compensation once. Sales exclude remitted sales tax.

Contribution = ticket × 0.96 − food − $2. Required orders = $27,000 ÷ contribution, rounded up. Daily planning target = whole monthly orders ÷ 26, rounded up; selling that same daily target every open day may exceed the monthly minimum. Revenue at the whole-order target = whole orders × ticket.

For reproducibility, capacity inputs are six workers × 40 hours/week × (52 ÷ 12) weeks × 75% productive utilization ÷ 0.15 total worker-hours/order = 5,200 whole orders/month. These time assumptions stay fixed; they do not prove kitchen throughput, customer demand or available bookings. Conversion is 25% solely to complete the calculator inputs; the table makes no inquiry forecast.

What the comparison shows—and what it cannot show

At a $25 ticket, $6 food cost plus $2 packaging and a $1 fee leave $16 contribution: 1,687.5 exact orders, 1,688 whole orders and a 65-order daily planning target. A higher ticket does not automatically lower the required volume: at a $30 ticket but $12 food cost, contribution is only $14.80 and the requirement rises to 1,825 whole orders (71/day). The ticket and food changes are distinct cases, not a causal claim about price changes. Recheck demand, sales mix and omitted channel costs.

Displayed amounts are rounded for reading; calculations use the unchanged decimal engine and unrounded intermediate values. CSV retains the numeric outputs and every required input. For zero or negative contribution, no finite positive-funding break-even target is valid; these grids use positive contribution only. Cash timing, taxes, financing principal, startup equipment cash and unentered costs are outside these scenarios. Any new staffing commitment changes both funding and capacity and requires a separate calculation.
