Product Costs vs Period Costs: Classification Examples

Product costs are assigned to inventory and generally become cost of goods sold when the related goods are sold. Period costs, such as selling and administrative costs, are recognized in the relevant period. Whether a cost is fixed or variable is a separate question.

All numerical examples are original fictional teaching cases in USD, not market benchmarks or promised outcomes. Assumptions are stated beside the calculations; replace them with your own dated evidence.

Classify the purpose before looking at the payment date

Begin with what the resource does. In a manufacturing setting, direct materials, direct production labor and manufacturing overhead belong to the production cost pool. Selling and general administration sit outside that pool. This classification is about the function and recognition of the cost, not whether a bank payment happened this month.

A factory lease can be a fixed product cost, while sales commission can be a variable period cost. Therefore, “fixed” does not mean “period” and “variable” does not mean “product.” Use the cost-behavior guide when the question is how a total changes with activity. Use this guide when the question is where the cost goes before and after a sale.

Do not classify the whole supplier invoice by its label. A single building can contain both production and sales space. Document a reasonable, consistent allocation driver and distinguish abnormal waste or other excluded items under the accounting policy that applies. A planning allocation is not proof that a cost is capitalizable.

Follow a 500-unit batch into inventory and expense

A workshop starts with no work in process or finished goods. It completes 500 identical shelves this month. Direct materials cost $6,000; direct labor costs $4,000; manufacturing overhead is $5,000. Assume all $15,000 qualifies for allocation to the completed units, with no abnormal idle capacity, waste, allocation variance or unfinished production. Each shelf therefore carries $30 of product cost.

The reconciliation is $15,000 production cost = $12,000 expense through sold units + $3,000 remaining in inventory. The $3,000 selling and administrative expense is separate. Charging the entire production pool to this month and also retaining $3,000 as inventory would count part of the cost twice.

A later sale changes expense timing, not the original cash payment

If the remaining 100 shelves sell next month for $60 each, the base case releases their $3,000 inventory cost to cost of goods sold and records $6,000 sales. This assumes the inventory remains recoverable at its carrying amount and no additional costs or adjustments arise. The inventory release does not require paying the original wood supplier again.

Keep a separate cash schedule for supplier terms, payroll and customer collections. Product cost can become an expense after its cash payment; a period expense can be accrued before payment. Accrual accounting explains that timing difference. The cost-of-goods-sold guide handles the inventory bridge in more detail.

Adjust the record for a retailer or service business

A retailer usually starts with purchased merchandise and the applicable costs of bringing it to the required condition and location. It does not manufacture the merchandise merely because it relabels its sales spreadsheet. A service business needs a cost model that reflects the actual work, contracts and accounting requirements; the shelf-factory labels should not be pasted onto every consultant invoice.

For internal job profitability, distinguish traceable job inputs from shared support and selling costs even when external reporting uses different presentation. Job-order costing helps trace a specific assignment. Absorption versus variable costing addresses the separate question of fixed production overhead under two costing approaches.

Review the classification without using it to hide costs

Retain the invoice, business purpose, allocation basis, production quantity and inventory movement. Reconcile opening inventory plus qualifying additions less released cost to closing inventory. Investigate negative quantities, duplicated costs and inconsistent allocations before using the margin.

Capitalizing a cost postpones expense recognition; it does not make the resource free. Producing unsold stock can tie up cash even while some cost remains on the balance sheet. Review actual demand and stock condition alongside the reported profit. Use the inventory management record to reconcile units and purchasing commitments. The decision record should identify who approves classification changes and why the policy remains consistent between periods.

Frequently asked questions

Is factory rent a product cost or period cost?

In this manufacturing case the production portion is manufacturing overhead, a product cost. A general office or selling portion is treated separately; being fixed does not settle the classification.

Are all variable costs product costs?

No. A variable sales commission is a selling period cost in this case. Function and cost behavior are different dimensions.

When do product costs become an expense?

Generally when the related inventory is sold through cost of goods sold, with other required adjustments such as impairment considered under the applicable accounting policy.

Why is ending inventory $3,000 in the example?

The 100 unsold shelves carry $30 each. The other 400 units release $12,000 of the $15,000 production pool to cost of goods sold.

Does paying a supplier determine expense recognition?

No. Cash timing and expense recognition can differ. Track both the inventory or payable movement and the payment.

Can this factory example be used unchanged for a service business?

No. Identify the actual business model and applicable policy. A service job-cost record can support decisions without assuming every service cost is merchandise inventory.
