Activity-based costing traces resource costs to activities and then assigns activity costs to the products, services or customers that use them. Its value depends on a defensible cost boundary, meaningful drivers and a complete reconciliation.

All amounts and operating results below are original fictional teaching examples in USD. They are not customer results, industry averages or forecasts. Use your own dated records and applicable accounting policies.

Define what the ABC model will explain

Start with the decision and cost object. A product-cost model, a customer cost-to-serve model and a quote for one job can need different pools. Write which ledger costs are included and which remain outside the model. Direct materials or directly traced labor must not be added to an overhead pool and then charged to the product a second time.

OpenStax’s ABC lesson describes assigning indirect costs through activity pools and drivers. This guide supplies a separate original fictional USD manufacturing case with two products and three support pools for one period. It does not reuse a source’s numerical case, claim that an allocation measures avoidable cash or prescribe an external-reporting inventory policy. Use the customer profitability guide when customer-specific support is the question.

Build pools whose activity records can be checked

The fictional factory has $18,000 setup support, $12,000 inspection and $30,000 machine support: $60,000 included overhead. The case assumes these pools are mutually exclusive and fully reconciled to the selected ledger accounts. Setup counts are appropriate only under its assumption that setups require sufficiently similar resources; inspection and machine support use hours rather than units sold.

Keep costs and activity in the same period. Record whether the quantities are budgeted or actual, what a setup includes, how hours are captured and who reviews incomplete logs. A count generated by software can still omit rework or combine different activity definitions. A driver should explain resource consumption for the chosen pool, not merely correlate with revenue.

Apply each rate and reconcile the complete allocation

For each pool, rate = included pool cost / selected driver quantity. Product allocation = pool rate × product driver consumption. These units matter: dollars per setup cannot be added directly to dollars per inspection hour. Apply the rates separately, then add the dollar allocations. A zero denominator with a nonzero pool is an unresolved model condition, not permission to return a zero rate.

Product A receives $4,500 + $7,200 + $22,500 = $34,200. Product B receives $13,500 + $4,800 + $7,500 = $25,800. The allocations sum to $60,000, and each pool also reconciles individually. Dividing by 1,000 and 500 completed units gives $34.20 and $51.60 overhead per unit. This case has no work in process, losses or unallocated base-case cost.

Explain the difference from one machine-hour rate

A single-rate comparison would allocate $60,000 / 2,000 machine hours = $30 per machine hour. That assigns $45,000 to A and $15,000 to B, or $45 and $30 per unit. Relative to that method, ABC shifts $10,800 from A to B without changing total included overhead. B has fewer machine hours but more setups, which is why the mix of activities changes the assignment.

Assume separately traced manufacturing costs of $26 per A unit and $34 per B unit. The base-case ABC assigned manufacturing costs become $60.20 and $85.60 per unit; the single-rate comparison gives $71 and $64. These are assigned manufacturing costs, not contribution margins, market prices or complete business profits. Nonmanufacturing costs, demand and capacity remain outside this comparison. A more complicated method is useful only if the resulting decision information justifies maintaining the evidence.

Report an unused-capacity scenario separately

The base case divides the machine-support pool by its specified 2,000 used hours. Now consider a separate management scenario in which the same $30,000 resource budget supplies 2,500 practical machine hours, but only 2,000 are used. A practical-capacity rate is $12 per hour. A receives $18,000 machine support and B $6,000; $6,000 is shown separately as unused machine capacity.

Keeping setup and inspection allocations unchanged gives A $29,700 and B $24,300, totaling $54,000 assigned plus $6,000 unused = $60,000 supplied. Harvard Business School’s capacity discussion explains why resource use and resource supply need separate visibility. This alternative management model is not a direction to capitalize or expense amounts under a particular reporting standard. Preserve its denominator and separate it from the full-allocation base case rather than silently switching the rate.

Do not turn an allocated saving into an automatic cash saving

Stopping Product B would remove its recorded activity demand from this model, but not necessarily its share of paid staff, machinery or facility commitments. Identify which resources can actually be reduced, when contracts can change and whether released capacity has a better use. A cost transfer between products changes the report even when the next bank payment is identical.

Test driver quality before changing price or mix. If B’s thirty setups include many short changeovers while A’s ten are unusually long, setup count may distort the assignment; measure duration or split genuinely different activities. If inspection hours reflect defects, retain the distinction between necessary inspection and avoidable rework. Reconcile the proposed action to CVP assumptions only after identifying which costs change with activity and which remain committed.

Maintain the model as an evidence-backed operating record

Preserve pool account mappings, driver definitions, quantity sources, rate version and reconciliation. Investigate any variance between budgeted resource cost, applied activity cost and actual ledger spending instead of hiding it in the unit price. Document treatment of facility-sustaining activity and excluded costs; forcing every cost through an arbitrary driver can make a complete-looking model less informative.

Update when process design, product mix, resource prices or capacity changes materially, and retain the previous version for comparison. A small pilot with a few consequential pools can reveal whether better records alter the decision before every minor expense is modeled. The cost-driver guide supports driver investigation; job order costing supports a job-level cost sheet. Frequently asked questions

What is the activity-based costing formula?

For each pool, divide the included cost by the selected driver quantity, multiply by each object’s usage and reconcile the resulting allocations.

Must every pool use the same driver?

No. A setup count, inspection hour and machine hour can represent different resource demands; their definitions and evidence must match their pools.

Does ABC change the total overhead incurred?

Allocation alone changes who receives the included cost, not the underlying total spending. Separate actual resource changes from reporting transfers.

Why is Product B more costly under this example’s ABC model?

It uses thirty of forty setups despite using fewer machine hours, so multiple drivers assign it more setup support than the single-rate comparison.

Is unused capacity a cash saving?

Not automatically. Cash changes only when the resource supply or other relevant cash consequence changes; unused availability can also support future work.

Is this an automatic external-reporting inventory calculation?

No. It is a management-costing illustration. Financial-statement inventory must follow the applicable recognition, allocation and measurement requirements.
