Flexible Budget: Formula, Variance Analysis and Worked Example

A flexible budget recalculates planned revenue and variable expenses for a different activity level while retaining the original budgeted rates and appropriate fixed commitments. For performance review, use actual completed activity. Comparing actual results with that same-activity budget separates the effect of doing more or less work from the effect of prices and spending.

This guide uses one fictional USD service-business month, shows the full profit reconciliation and provides the calculator above. Figures are invented planning inputs, not benchmarks. The tool is a simple equivalent-unit operating model, not a cash forecast, manufacturing inventory model or explanation of who caused a variance.

Flexible budget formula and assumptions

Flexible revenue = budgeted price × actual activity. Flexible variable expense = budgeted variable cost per unit × actual activity. Flexible total expense = flexible variable expense + budgeted fixed expense. Subtract total expense from flexible revenue to calculate flexible operating income.

Keep the original selling price and variable-cost rate when flexing the budget. Substituting actual rates would hide the differences you need to investigate. Do not scale every expense by the volume change: rent can stay fixed within a relevant range, while a per-visit supply cost changes with completed visits. A further supervisor, minimum staffing requirement or additional vehicle can create a new fixed-cost step.

Use the fixed, variable and mixed-cost guide to define cost behavior. If a pool mixes fixed and variable amounts, split it using defensible records. The high-low method gives only a preliminary two-observation estimate and still needs checks against other records.

A complete flexible budget example

The original monthly plan is 200 completed equivalent cleaning visits at $180 each, with $80 variable expense per visit and $14,000 fixed operating commitments. Actual activity is 180 visits. Actual net sales are $31,500, actual variable expenses $15,120 and actual fixed operating expenses $14,300.

At 180 actual visits, the original $180 price produces flexible revenue of $32,400, and the original $80 variable rate produces flexible variable expense of $14,400. The fixed budget remains $14,000. Actual revenue implies $175 per visit and actual variable spending $84 per visit in this simplified case. Those implied averages diagnose differences; they do not establish why prices or costs changed.

Flexible budget variance formula: label the sign

This guide defines flexible-budget variance = actual result − flexible budget. A positive revenue difference is favorable; a positive expense difference is unfavorable. For operating income, positive is favorable. A label must accompany the sign so a higher expense is not mistaken for good performance.

The $900 revenue shortfall, $720 variable-spending excess and $300 fixed-spending excess together reduce operating income by $1,920. Do not add the contribution-margin variance again: contribution already combines revenue and variable expenses. A variance directs investigation; it is not automatically an employee fault, waste finding or price recommendation.

Reconcile the full original-to-actual profit change

Activity effect = flexible operating income − original operating income. Here it is $4,000 − $6,000 = −$2,000. At the unchanged $100 planned contribution per visit, completing 20 fewer visits loses $2,000 contribution toward fixed commitments.

Performance effect = actual operating income − flexible operating income. Here it is $2,080 − $4,000 = −$1,920. The full change is −$2,000 + −$1,920 = −$3,920, exactly matching $2,080 actual minus $6,000 original. That reconciliation prevents an unexplained residual from disappearing into a general budget label.

For a multiple-product business, the equivalent-unit assumption can fail when mix changes. A more complex analysis must separate volume, mix, price and cost effects using a consistent base. Start with the sales-mix and weighted-margin guide rather than treating every completed order as identical.

How to prepare a useful flexible budget

  1. Retain the original approved plan, rates and reporting period. A revised forecast is useful, but should not overwrite the comparison baseline.
  2. Reconcile actual activity and recognized net sales. Returns, discounts and incomplete jobs need a stated convention.
  3. Flex each variable line with its real driver. Job count can suit consumables, while miles or machine-hours may better explain another pool.
  4. Keep fixed commitments at their budgeted amount only within the valid range. Separately identify actual changes, cost steps and scope changes.
  5. Compare actual and flexible lines, then reconcile the activity and performance effects to the total operating-income difference.
  6. Investigate the largest controllable drivers with invoices, work records and schedules. Record an owner, action and next review date.

A favorable cost variance can arise from deferred maintenance or reduced service quality. An unfavorable labor variance can arise from training, changed customer requirements or incorrect standards. The budget identifies a question to investigate, not a complete operational diagnosis.

Flexible budget in Excel or Google Sheets

For a compact worksheet, place planned units in B2, actual units B3, budgeted price B4, variable rate B5 and fixed budget B6. Original operating income is =B2*(B4-B5)-B6; flexible operating income is =B3*(B4-B5)-B6. Enter actual revenue B7, actual total variable expense B8 and actual fixed expense B9. Actual operating income is =B7-B8-B9.

Require complete numeric inputs and a consistent period before calculating. The activity effect is flexible minus original; the performance effect is actual minus flexible. All three operating-income calculations should use the same included cost boundary. Use the contribution statement template to reconcile the revenue, variable-expense, contribution and fixed-expense lines.

Where the simple model stops

The calculator does not validate productive capacity, forecast demand, model tax or loan payments, or reproduce an external financial statement. Inventory changes and production-versus-sales differences require additional schedules. At zero actual units, unit-price averages are undefined; any positive actual revenue needs a timing or unit reconciliation before interpretation.

Use a separate cash budget and receipt/payment schedule for liquidity. A favorable operating variance cannot pay a bill before customer cash arrives. Keep operating results, future demand and payment timing distinct.

Turn the variance table into a performance report

A useful flexible budget performance report answers three separate questions: how much work happened, what that work should have earned and cost at the approved rates, and what actually happened. Give each line an activity driver, evidence reference and explanation. Keep the signed difference separate from its favorable or unfavorable label. The label describes the operating-income effect, not whether someone made a good decision.

In the existing cleaning example, revenue is $900 below the same-activity budget. The implied actual price is $31,500 ÷ 180 = $175, versus the original $180. The arithmetic explanation is 180 × ($175 − $180) = −$900. That does not establish a price-cutting problem: inspect discounts, refunds, job scope and the mix of visits before calling this a pure selling-price variance. A different service mix can change the average even if no individual price changes.

The $720 unfavorable variable-expense difference equals 180 × ($84 − $80). Calling this an efficiency variance would go beyond the data. The $84 average can reflect higher input prices, more input quantities, different work or incorrect classifications. A visit-level total cannot identify labor hours and wage-rate effects without a supporting hours schedule.

Separate labor rate from hours with a supporting schedule

Consider a separate fictional labor schedule, not a breakdown of the $720 above. For 180 comparable visits, the standard allows 1.5 delivery hours per visit at $24 per hour. Standard hours allowed are 270 and the flexible labor budget is $6,480. Actual approved hours are 288 at $25, costing $7,200. The actual-minus-standard cost difference is $720 unfavorable.

When one activity driver is not enough

Suppose a separate monthly support pool includes $1,200 fixed dispatch, $2 per completed visit and $0.40 per delivery mile. At 180 visits and 2,100 miles, its flexible amount is $1,200 + $360 + $840 = $2,400. Multiplying the entire original pool by a visits percentage would incorrectly scale the fixed commitment and assume miles always move with visits. Keep the three components in a supporting schedule and reconcile the total back to the operating report.

Where a contract creates a capacity step, such as a second vehicle above a documented mileage threshold, use the applicable budgeted step rather than pretending the original fixed amount covers unlimited activity. Preserve the approved baseline and show scope changes separately. The simple calculator uses one equivalent activity unit and one fixed pool; use it only when that simplification fits, or prepare a driver-level schedule outside the widget.

Example of a variance action record

For the original $900 revenue shortfall, assign the sales owner to reconcile the 180 completed visits to net invoice lines, approved discounts and credits. Record a due date and the resulting explanation. For the $720 variable-spending difference, the operations owner should reconcile purchase usage and approved delivery time. For the $300 fixed-expense excess, inspect the actual invoice and whether it belongs to this period. These are evidence requests, not conclusions that the business should raise prices or reduce staffing.

Review absolute dollars and the operational context together. A small percentage of a large pool may deserve attention, while a large percentage on a tiny line may not. Do not invent a universal investigation threshold. Agree a review policy before the period closes and track repeated small differences that accumulate. Close the review by recording the evidence, action, owner and next check; retain the original standards so a revised forecast does not erase accountability.

FAQs

What is a flexible budget?

It recalculates planned revenue and variable costs at a selected activity level while keeping original rates and appropriate fixed commitments. For performance review, use actual activity.

What is flexible budget variance?

Using actual minus flexible, positive revenue variance is favorable while positive expense variance is unfavorable. For operating income, a positive difference is favorable.

Should fixed costs change when the budget is flexed?

Not simply because activity changes within the valid range. Separate a genuine cost step or scope change instead of multiplying every fixed expense by volume.

Does a favorable variance prove good management?

No. It can reflect deferred spending, changed scope or a poor standard. Investigate invoices, quality and operating records before attributing responsibility.

Make-or-Buy Decision: Relevant Costs, Calculator and Example

A make-or-buy decision compares the future costs and benefits that differ between doing work internally and purchasing the same acceptable deliverable from a supplier. It is a relevant-cost decision: a cost that continues unchanged under both alternatives cannot make one option cheaper than the other.

Use the calculator above to compare a single period and a consistent quantity. Then assess quality, lead time, supply continuity, cash timing and realistic internal capacity. A mathematically cheaper quote is not automatically the better operating decision, and the tool does not certify that either option is feasible.

Make-or-buy decision formula

Relevant make cost = required quantity × avoidable make cost per unit + avoidable fixed make costs + contribution forgone by making. Relevant buy cost = required quantity × supplier unit price + additional buying-only period costs. The modeled cost advantage is the difference between those two amounts.

Include material purchases that change with the decision, labor that is genuinely avoidable, variable processing and the fixed commitments that actually disappear if production is outsourced. For buying, include freight, inspection, onboarding, handling, expected rework and other incremental costs where supported by evidence. Keep a cost either in the unit quote or the additional period line; do not count it twice.

An allocated share of rent is not avoidable merely because an accounting report charges it to the component. If the lease and payment remain unchanged, the amount is common to both alternatives. Likewise, the historical purchase price of existing equipment is a sunk cost. Future sale proceeds or actual cancellation costs can be relevant, but require their own evidenced cash flows and may need a more detailed model.

A worked make-or-buy example with capacity

A fictional small business needs 1,000 acceptable product components this month. Making costs $14 per component in truly avoidable materials, paid labor and running costs. It can avoid $3,000 of period setup/support expense if it buys. A supplier quotes $18 per acceptable component, with an additional $500 for delivery and inspection. A separate $4,000 commitment continues under either alternative.

If the internal team makes the components, it cannot perform another feasible job that would earn $2,000 net contribution after that alternative job's own variable costs. That is an opportunity cost of making, not another supplier invoice. These USD figures are invented examples, not market quotations or verified supplier terms.

Buying has a $500 lower modeled economic cost. Including the unchanged $4,000 in both columns leaves exactly the same difference. Removing that common cost from only one alternative would manufacture a false saving.

Economic comparison and cash comparison are different here. Relevant make cash outflows are $17,000, whereas buy cash outflows are $18,500. Buying uses $1,500 more direct cash under this boundary, but releases an opportunity worth $2,000 net contribution if it is genuinely feasible. Its modeled economic advantage is therefore $500. Actual collections, deposits and payment dates still require a cash forecast.

Idle capacity can reverse the result

If there is no feasible alternative use of the released resources, enter zero opportunity contribution. Relevant make cost becomes $14,000 + $3,000 = $17,000; buy remains $18,500. Making now has a $1,500 advantage. Do not claim a released employee hour earns revenue just because the hour becomes available.

Conversely, if making requires overtime, additional equipment or delayed higher-contribution work, those consequences may change the comparison. A salaried employee who stays employed can be an unavoidable cash commitment even when their scarce time has an economic opportunity cost. Model the relevant distinction without charging the same salary twice.

For service work, the equivalent decision can be internal delivery versus subcontracting. Use comparable scope, acceptance standards, customer obligations and rework responsibilities. See the employee-versus-freelancer guide and subcontractor job-cost guide for related operating contexts.

Calculate the crossover quantity carefully

With constant unit rates and period amounts, equate the relevant cost lines. Crossover quantity = (avoidable make fixed costs + contribution forgone − buying-only period costs) ÷ (buy unit cost − make variable cost per unit).

Using the main example: ($3,000 + $2,000 − $500) ÷ ($18 − $14) = 1,125 components. Both alternatives have a relevant economic cost of $20,750 at that quantity. At 1,000, buying is cheaper; at 1,200, making costs $21,800 versus $22,100 buying, so making is cheaper by $300 under the same assumptions.

The opportunity amount is held constant in that mathematical illustration. In reality it may depend on released hours, demand or the quantity made. Supplier price tiers, minimum orders, overtime and capacity steps can also invalidate the crossover. A negative threshold is not a useful positive production target; equal unit rates may produce no unique threshold or a tie at every quantity.

Records to collect before deciding

  1. A comparable specification: quantity, usable yield, quality tolerance, packaging, warranty and required date.
  2. A verified internal cost list: mark each line as avoidable, unavoidable, sunk or opportunity-related. Confirm cancellation and reassignment possibilities.
  3. A complete supplier quote: price basis, freight, inspection, minimum order, payment terms, rework responsibility and validity period.
  4. A capacity record: usable hours, bottlenecks, required setup time and the best feasible alternative work.
  5. An opportunity calculation: expected retained revenue less the alternative job's incremental costs, supported by demand and delivery capacity.
  6. A cash schedule: deposit dates and outflows, expected collections, available funding and a realistic buffer.

Keep the original comparison and record what happens after implementation: accepted quantities, delivery time, defects, rework, actual supplier charges and retained internal commitments. A decision that looked cheaper can deteriorate when the quoted scope differs from the work actually supplied.

Check allocation before treating it as a saving

A cost-driver allocation can assign a support pool to a job without making that amount avoidable. Keep the documented driver and cost pool for job reporting, then independently identify which commitments disappear under each make-or-buy alternative.

Common mistakes and practical controls

Comparing a full allocated make cost with a partial buy quote. Reconcile both columns to the same cost boundary. Treating all direct labor as avoidable. Review actual employment and scheduling commitments. Using alternative revenue as opportunity benefit. Use net contribution from a feasible alternative after its relevant costs.

Ignoring the cost of failures. A low quote is not comparable if it shifts inspection, waste or warranty obligations back to you. Confusing lower economic cost with cash affordability. An early supplier deposit can create a funding gap despite an attractive comparison. Use the cash forecast separately.

Confusing a make-or-buy crossover with business break-even. The crossover compares two cost alternatives. Business break-even compares revenue with all included operating costs. Review the break-even formula guide for that separate question.

What is the maximum acceptable supplier price?

The crossover quantity answers how volume changes the comparison. A different question is the supplier unit-price ceiling at a fixed quantity. With the original 1,000 components, $14 avoidable internal unit cost, $3,000 avoidable fixed cost, $2,000 feasible opportunity contribution and $500 buying-only cost, the economic ceiling is ($19,000 − $500) ÷ 1,000 = $18.50 per acceptable component. At that quote both options have the same modeled economic cost. Below it buying is lower; above it making is lower, within the stated assumptions.

Without an alternative job, relevant make cost is $17,000 and the ceiling falls to ($17,000 − $500) ÷ 1,000 = $16.50. The $2 difference is the $2,000 opportunity contribution spread over 1,000 components. A buyer should therefore not negotiate against an $18.50 ceiling unless that alternative contribution is evidenced. This is an economic ceiling, not a statement that a deposit or invoice is affordable.

How much alternative contribution makes outsourcing worthwhile?

At the original $18 supplier quote, buying costs $18,500 and avoidable make cash costs are $17,000. Buying needs at least $1,500 of feasible released-capacity contribution to reach an economic tie. At $1,000 contribution, making remains lower by $500. At $2,000, buying becomes lower by $500. Use net contribution after the alternative job’s incremental costs, not its invoice value or gross revenue.

Convert a quote into cost per acceptable component

A quoted purchased unit and an acceptable delivered unit are not necessarily the same denominator. In a separate fictional case, the supplier charges $10 per purchased unit, expected usable yield is 95%, and the business needs 1,000 acceptable units. Before considering minimum order sizes, it must purchase at least ceiling(1,000 ÷ 0.95) = 1,053 units. Purchase cost is $10,530; with $300 incremental inspection and delivery, the modeled cost is $10,830, or $10.83 per required acceptable unit.

The extra 53 purchased units are a planning allowance, not a guarantee that exactly 1,000 will pass. A yield estimate needs evidence and a contingency for variability. If the contract bills only accepted units or provides replacements, use those terms instead. Do not apply both a yield adjustment and the same defect allowance again as rework cost. Minimum orders, freight tiers and surplus inventory can invalidate a smooth unit-cost crossover, so compare actual order-sized alternatives.

Check when an internal cost can actually disappear

Attach a date and evidence to each claimed saving. Materials not yet ordered may be avoidable immediately. A cancellable machine rental may disappear only after its notice period. A supervisor salary that continues for the next three months is not an immediate saving merely because the production line stops. A reassigned employee can remain a cash commitment while released hours have a separate opportunity value.

For a one-month decision, include only the costs and benefits relevant to that month. For a longer decision, show the transition months, cancellation payments, severance where applicable, continuing commitments, working capital and eventual equipment proceeds separately. Do not divide a first-year saving by monthly volume and present it as a permanent unit advantage. The embedded single-period calculator cannot discount a multi-year project or recognize staged cash flows; build a dated schedule where the decision requires one.

Write a decision note someone else can verify

A defensible note states the required quantity and acceptance specification; the quote validity date; make and buy cost boundaries; avoidable commitments and supporting cancellation terms; evidence for alternative contribution; and the owner responsible for delivery and quality. Include the base comparison plus a scenario with no opportunity contribution. In the original case, the note must state both the $500 economic buying advantage and the $1,500 higher direct cash outflow before the alternative work is collected.

Set operational conditions before committing: agreed sample approval, inspection responsibility, acceptable delivery timing, escalation contacts and treatment of rejected units. Record which costs remain after outsourcing and compare actual accepted output with the assumptions after implementation. If the $2,000 alternative job is cancelled, revisit the decision rather than preserving a saving that no longer exists.

FAQs

Which costs matter in a make-or-buy decision?

Future costs and benefits that differ between alternatives matter. Common unavoidable commitments and sunk historical costs do not create an incremental saving.

How does idle capacity change the choice?

If there is no feasible alternative use, opportunity contribution can be zero. If internal work displaces a feasible profitable alternative, the contribution forgone belongs in the comparison.

Should allocated fixed overhead be included?

Include only the portion that changes with the decision. An allocated commitment that remains unchanged is common to both alternatives and must not be presented as a saving.

Is the lower modeled cost always the right choice?

No. Quality, delivery reliability, capacity, cash timing and contractual responsibilities also need evaluation. The calculator compares the entered economic cost assumptions.
