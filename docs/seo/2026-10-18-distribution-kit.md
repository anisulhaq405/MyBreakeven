## Week 1 — three post drafts

### 1. A monthly restaurant budget needs an order target

A fictional $25 order with $6 food, $2 packaging and a 4% payment fee leaves $16 contribution. A $27,000 monthly funding requirement needs 1,688 whole orders. At 26 open days, a uniform daily planning target is 65 orders.

That target is not a forecast of diners. Check actual service capacity and use the same cost boundary for payroll and owner pay. We have published 20 transparent comparisons with downloadable inputs.

MyBreakeven is our tool. Review the free example: https://mybreakeven.com/blogs/restaurant-budgeting/
Content value: restaurant_budget_preview_w1

### 2. Cleaning budgets: paid hours still matter when a visit cancels

A job-based cost average can hide payroll that stays payable during cancellations. Our cleaning monthly budget treats entered monthly payroll as committed, then compares completed jobs, overhead, owner pay and available productive hours.

The workbook includes a blank tab and a clearly labelled fictional example. Reconcile payroll once; keep short-term cash timing separate from the operating result.

MyBreakeven is our tool. Download and review: https://mybreakeven.com/resources/cleaning-business-budget-template/
Content value: cleaning_monthly_budget_w1

### 3. 103 landscaping jobs needed. Only 94 complete jobs fit.

In our fictional landscaping case, $110.72 contribution per job must fund $11,300 monthly commitments and income goals. That requires 103 whole jobs. The entered crew-hours fit 94 complete jobs, leaving a nine-job gap.

More inquiries do not create crew-hours. Check job duration, route assumptions and monthly commitments before treating a financial target as a workable schedule.

MyBreakeven is our tool. Compare the 20 model cases: https://mybreakeven.com/blogs/landscaping-break-even/#landscaping-20-scenarios
Content value: landscape_job_target_w1

## Week 2 — three post drafts

### 4. Active-season work still funds year-round commitments

Landscaping revenue can be seasonal while insurance, storage or equipment commitments continue. A monthly busy-season average does not automatically cover the full year.

List annual commitments, then test the workload you can deliver during active months. Keep monthly and annual periods explicit; weather and bookings remain assumptions.

MyBreakeven is our tool. Read the existing seasonal method: https://mybreakeven.com/blogs/landscaping-seasonal-break-even/
Content value: landscape_seasonality_w2

### 5. The denominator changes the sales cushion

In a fictional example, $18,000 forecast sales minus $12,000 break-even sales gives a $6,000 cushion. Margin of safety is $6,000 divided by $18,000: 33.33%.

Dividing by the break-even amount answers a different question. The cushion is not $6,000 profit or cash, and a forecast is not an actual result.

MyBreakeven is our tool. Review the formula and cost boundary: https://mybreakeven.com/blogs/margin-of-safety-business/
Content value: margin_safety_denominator_w2

### 6. Restaurant prime cost: include every payroll dollar once

Food cost and loaded labor need the same reporting period before they can form a useful prime-cost figure. Employer costs, guaranteed shifts and genuinely variable hours need a consistent boundary.

A payroll allocation is not an additional cost if the same wages are already in monthly overhead. Reconcile totals to records before comparing percentages.

MyBreakeven is our tool. Review the food-plus-labor method: https://mybreakeven.com/blogs/restaurant-prime-cost/
Content value: restaurant_cost_boundary_w2

In our fictional scenarios, a $25 ticket with $6 food cost leaves $16 contribution after $2 packaging and a 4% fee. A $30 ticket with $12 food cost leaves only $14.80.

Against the same $27,000 monthly funding need, that is 1,688 versus 1,825 whole orders. These compare different assumptions; they do not predict how customers react to a price change.

### 9. Available hours are not automatically billable hours

Agency pricing needs a realistic billable-hours denominator. Time off, admin, sales and revisions affect the hours left to sell, while a higher target rate does not prove clients will buy them.

Define billable and available hours for the same period, then connect that capacity to your costs and income goal. Avoid counting the same delivery salary twice.
