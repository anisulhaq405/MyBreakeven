## Five-factor decisions by article

The articles already contained meaningful worked examples, cost boundaries, internal calculator links and FAQs. The update preserves that work and adds specific missing decisions, rather than replacing it with generic longer copy. Existing sources remain in place. New financial examples are explicitly hypothetical USD management models, not market benchmarks, tax rates or legal rules.

### 1. Cleaning contract payroll cash gap

1. Competition: [Janitorial Manager's cash-flow guide](https://www.janitorialmanager.com/blog/7-cash-flow-mistakes-to-avoid-in-your-cleaning-business/) makes collection processes, payment terms and time recording actionable. These are observable strengths, not proven ranking causes.
2. Content/link gap: the existing dated ledger was strong but did not compare a priced early-payment alternative or explain a shared multi-contract reserve. Link actual-job costs into the forecast.
3. Diagnosis: no returned GSC row; insufficient evidence for an indexing or keyword diagnosis. Narrow owner-focused payroll language is a relevance hypothesis.
4. Improvement: funding-action table, a 2% early-payment timing test, combined-company cash boundary and payroll-run reconciliation.
5. Mistakes addressed: treating invoice due dates as cleared receipts; giving every contract the entire company reserve; treating a discount as free financing or loan proceeds as sales.

### 2. Cleaning estimate versus actual

1. Competition: Google surfaces cleaning estimating/job-costing content and rate questions. [PHG's restoration variance discussion](https://phgadvisory.com/xactimate-estimate-vs-actual-job-cost-where-restoration-margin-goes/) usefully separates a selling estimate from actual costs; its industry prices are not cleaning benchmarks.
2. Content/link gap: the article already calculated the $54 variance but did not attribute the labor difference between approved scope and original-scope time. Link reconciled contribution to acquisition and payroll planning.
3. Diagnosis: the sparse sample is weak visibility evidence, not proof of a particular cause. Rate-oriented results show potentially broader competing intent; owner closeout intent is made explicit.
4. Improvement: cost-to-contribution variance table, scope-adjusted hours, full worker-hour comparison and closeout evidence fields.
5. Mistakes addressed: blaming all overrun on efficiency; rewriting the accepted estimate; comparing actual contribution with estimated rather than actual hours.

### 3. Cleaning acquisition payback

1. Competition: [Estimatty](https://www.estimatty.com/blog/how-to-reduce-customer-acquisition-cost) connects acquisition spending with sales follow-up and repeat clients. Generic SaaS payback results require industry translation, not copied benchmarks.
2. Gap: detailed CAC definitions existed, but not a side-by-side lead-cost versus actual cohort-recovery decision. Add cash and actual-cost links.
3. Diagnosis: the sparse sample cannot prove a snippet failure. Clear paid-visit intent and title are testable changes.
4. Improvement: equal-age two-channel cohort table, client distribution, review cutoff, zero-client treatment and cash/capacity gate.
5. Mistakes addressed: preferring cheap leads without conversion or retention; equating aggregate payback with every client's payback; reporting zero CAC when there are zero clients.

### 4. Cleaning client churn

1. Competition: [BigChange](https://www.bigchange.com/blog/why-your-customer-churn-rate-is-high-whats-driving-customers-away) connects service reasons with retention actions. General claims about replacement-cost multiples are not adopted.
2. Gap: existing churn arithmetic and a retention budget did not test the incremental effect of an intervention. Link acquisition and renewal alternatives.
3. Diagnosis: the sparse sample does not establish stable ranking or customer demand.
4. Improvement: explicit three-month retention scenario, probability uplift, break-even intervention effect and contribution concentration.
5. Mistakes addressed: crediting an offer for all retained clients; confusing a per-visit discount with a one-time cost; hiding churn behind new-client growth.

### 5. Commercial cleaning price increase

1. Competition: [The Janitorial Store](https://www.thejanitorialstore.com/public/Why-How-Raise-Prices-Cleaning-Customers-458.cfm) links account profitability, cost review and customer communication.
2. Gap: calculations and scope choices existed, but a customer-facing proposal worksheet and current-cost retained-portfolio test were absent. Connect actual cost and churn evidence.
3. Diagnosis: the sparse sample is insufficient evidence to identify acceptance, CTR or rank causality.
4. Improvement: editable communication example, scope/date/confirmation fields, eight-versus-seven retained-account sensitivity and post-acceptance review.
5. Mistakes addressed: comparing new prices with obsolete lower costs; counting account acceptance alone; claiming payroll is avoidable or implying an automatic right to raise prices.

### 6. Landscaping subcontractor cost

1. Competition: [DoorstepHQ](https://doorstephq.com/blog/landscaping-subcontractor-vs-turn-down-work) frames outsourcing against lost work and specialist capacity. These decision alternatives are useful beyond a quote-only comparison.
2. Gap: existing contribution and freed-hours analysis mentioned payment timing but lacked a dated cash bridge. Link crew and measured material scope.
3. Diagnosis: the sparse sample cannot establish stable query placement or traffic potential.
4. Improvement: staged supplier/customer payment table, fee-settlement sensitivity, $39.44-per-freed-hour hurdle and variable/committed/overtime labor boundaries.
5. Mistakes addressed: adding customer advances to total revenue; treating accounting contribution as cash available; assuming committed payroll is saved or an enquiry fills freed hours.

### 7. Mulch installation

1. Competition: Homewyse calculators and forums dominate the observed broad intent. [HomeAdvisor](https://www.homeadvisor.com/cost/landscape/mulch-delivery-install/) provides consumer price context, not an owner's contribution model.
2. Gap: original volume/purchase rounding was correct, but bag-versus-bulk comparison lacked a complete numerical purchase and handling case. Connect crew and subcontract scope.
3. Diagnosis: no returned row; broad consumer intent suggests an owner-cost title is worth testing, not that a technical problem is proven.
4. Improvement: area × inches / 324 shortcut, whole-bag comparison, landed invoices, handling sensitivity and site/stock closeout sheet.
5. Mistakes addressed: comparing bag count with yard price; ignoring handling labor; counting alternative material purchases together or charging stock twice across jobs.

### 8. Landscaping crew utilization

1. Competition: [SynkedUp](https://synkedup.com/landscaping-crew-tracking-software/) connects job-tagged time to estimated/actual labor. Tracking categories are useful observable features; software promises are not independent performance evidence.
2. Gap: the article already defined hours consistently, but its comparable-visit examples lacked a mixed-service schedule and a quantified scarce-role/rework check. Link mulch and subcontracting.
3. Diagnosis: no returned row, so no indexing or keyword diagnosis is asserted.
4. Improvement: 72-of-78-hour mixed workload, six-hour remaining capacity, specialist bottleneck and separately labeled first-pass fieldwork measure.
5. Mistakes addressed: treating unequal services as equal jobs; assuming spare general labor replaces a specialist; calling rework-backed activity new sales capacity.

### 9. Photography mini-session profit

1. Competition: [Katie Lee](https://katieleephotos.com/how-to-run-smooth-and-profitable-family-mini-sessions/) explains repeatable dates, setup, booking and buffers. Practitioner experience gives operational detail but does not guarantee demand.
2. Gap: existing full event costing mentioned editing capacity but did not turn the delivery deadline into a numerical booking cap. Link full-session alternatives and print contribution.
3. Diagnosis: the sparse sample supplies too little evidence for a CTR conclusion or top-three forecast.
4. Improvement: four-booking minimum versus five-gallery cap, owner-hours reconciliation, launch review date and sunk-versus-avoidable decision boundary.
5. Mistakes addressed: lead wording suggesting booked slots alone fund the event; equating eight camera slots with eight deliverable galleries; using full-cost break-even as an automatic late-cancellation rule.

### 10. Mobile detailing travel fee

1. Competition: forum questions and [Housecall Pro's pricing guide](https://www.housecallpro.com/resources/how-much-to-charge-for-car-detailing/) cover travel/setup and complete delivery costs. National advertised prices are not adopted as local cost inputs.
2. Gap: original fee/capacity examples were useful, but did not solve the exact hourly-preservation price or distinguish nearest-cent display from a minimum rounded-up fee. Link route cancellation and refill costs.
3. Diagnosis: the sparse sample indicates limited visibility but the reason is unproven. More exact owner pricing language is a testable relevance improvement.
4. Improvement: four-option fee table, $253.43 hourly-target total, explicit $22.69 minimum rounded-up cost-recovery surcharge and route quote fields.
5. Mistakes addressed: intro's $22.68 rounded display presented as exact preservation; cost recovery confused with hourly recovery; multiplying vehicle mileage by passenger count; surprise repricing after cancellation.
