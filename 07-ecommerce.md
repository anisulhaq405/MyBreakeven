# How to Price Products for Ecommerce
![Ecommerce owner reviewing product costs, shipping and pricing](/assets/blog/ecommerce-pricing.png)

If you sell products online, start with your cost per order, not a competitor's sticker price. The right price covers the product, fulfilment, payment fees, returns, marketing, and the share of monthly overhead assigned to each order, then leaves a contribution you can use to pay yourself and make a profit.

A low price can produce plenty of sales and still lose money. A high price can produce a healthy margin but too few orders. The practical goal is a price and order volume that work together.

> **Quick answer:** Add every cost that rises with an order, then choose the contribution you need from each sale. If variable costs are $31.05 before a 2.9% payment fee and you want a 40% contribution margin, the price is about $54.90: ($31.05 + $0.30) ÷ (1 − 0.029 − 0.40). Test that price against demand, conversion, and capacity.

## The direct answer, expanded

Use this sequence: add landed cost, packaging, pick-and-pack labour, shipping subsidy, returns, order-level marketing, and payment fees. Set a contribution margin target, then check the price against customer demand and the orders your store can fulfil.

The payment fee makes the calculation different from simply multiplying cost by a markup. Suppose one product has these costs on every order:

- Product landed cost: $18.00
- Packaging: $1.25
- Pick-and-pack labour: $2.00
- Shipping subsidy: $3.50
- Returns allowance: $1.00
- Advertising cost per order: $5.00
- Payment fee: 2.9% of the selling price plus $0.30

The costs other than payment processing total $31.05. If the target contribution margin is 40%, let the selling price be **P**. The contribution is `P − ($31.05 + 0.029P + $0.30)`, or `0.971P − $31.35`. Set that equal to 40% of the price:

`0.971P − $31.35 = 0.40P`

`0.571P = $31.35`

`P = $54.90`

At $54.90, the payment fee is $1.89, total variable cost is $32.94, and contribution is $21.96. Divide $21.96 by $54.90 and the contribution margin is 40.0% after rounding. The earlier quick-answer shortcut should therefore use the full fixed payment fee in the numerator: `(31.05 + 0.30) ÷ (1 − 0.029 − 0.40)`, which gives $54.90.

That is a **cost-based floor and target**, not permission to ignore the customer. If comparable products sell for $39, check whether their shipping, product cost, advertising cost, or margin target differs. Cutting your price to match without checking those differences turns competitor research into guesswork.

Next, calculate the orders needed. If monthly fixed costs are $6,000 and you want $2,000 for owner pay plus $1,000 in profit, the store needs $9,000 of contribution. At $21.96 per order, that is `9,000 ÷ 21.96 = 409.84`, so you need **410 orders**. At $54.90 each, those orders produce `$54.90 × 410 = $22,509` in revenue. Contribution, not revenue, covers fixed bills and target pay.

## What changes the answer

**Landed product cost** is the first major lever. Include supplier price, inbound freight, duties, and preparation before sale. A product invoiced at $12 may cost more once it reaches storage.

**Fulfilment and shipping terms** change the economics of an order. If you advertise free shipping, the amount you pay the carrier belongs in the variable-cost line. If customers pay shipping separately, model the shipping revenue and shipping expense rather than pretending either one does not exist.

**Fees and acquisition cost** are easy to underestimate. Payment processing may have both a percentage and a flat fee. Marketplace commissions, transaction app charges, affiliate payouts, and advertising allocated to a first order can take more from the sale than packaging.

**Returns, refunds, and discounts** need an allowance. A 15% sale is not only a smaller price; it may also leave less room for a payment fee that is not fully recovered, return shipping, and replacement handling. Run a normal-price version and a promotion version before you advertise the discount.

**Order mix and capacity** matter because one store rarely sells one identical order. A $20 accessory may have a better percentage margin but use nearly the same packing time as a $100 bundle. Seasonal demand can change ad cost and expedited shipping. Read [link: ecommerce profit margins after fees] and [link: ecommerce order volume to break even] when comparing products.

## 2–3 realistic worked scenarios

### Scenario 1: A low-priced accessory

A phone accessory has a landed cost of $8.00. Packaging is $0.75, fulfilment is $1.50, the average shipping subsidy is $2.75, returns allowance is $0.50, and advertising costs $3.50 per order. The non-payment variable costs total `$8 + 0.75 + 1.50 + 2.75 + 0.50 + 3.50 = $17.00`.

At a selling price of $29.00, payment processing is `($29 × 0.029) + $0.30 = $1.14` after rounding. Total variable cost is `$17.00 + $1.14 = $18.14`, so contribution is `$29.00 − $18.14 = $10.86`. The contribution margin is `$10.86 ÷ $29.00 = 37.4%`.

If monthly fixed costs are $3,000, owner pay is $2,000, and the profit target is $1,000, required contribution is $6,000. The store needs `6,000 ÷ 10.86 = 552.49`, or **553 orders**. That means about `553 ÷ 26 = 21.3 orders per selling day` on a 26-day month. Being above product cost is not enough; required daily volume is the test.

### Scenario 2: A higher-priced bundle

A skincare bundle costs $42.00 landed. Packaging is $1.50, fulfilment is $3.00, shipping subsidy is $6.00, returns allowance is $2.00, and advertising is $8.00 per order. Those costs total `$42 + $1.50 + $3 + $6 + $2 + $8 = $62.50` before payment processing.

At $119.00, the payment fee is `($119 × 0.029) + $0.30 = $3.75` after rounding. Total variable cost is `$62.50 + $3.75 = $66.25`; contribution is `$119 − $66.25 = $52.75`, and contribution margin is `$52.75 ÷ $119 = 44.3%`.

With $9,000 in monthly fixed costs, $3,500 of owner pay, and a $2,500 profit target, required contribution is $15,000. The order requirement is `15,000 ÷ 52.75 = 284.36`, so it needs **285 orders**. Revenue at that volume is `$119 × 285 = $33,915`. If the bundle were priced at $99, the payment fee would be $3.17, contribution would be `$99 − ($62.50 + $3.17) = $33.33`, and the same target would require `15,000 ÷ 33.33 = 450.05`, or **451 orders**. The $20 cut adds 166 monthly orders.

### Scenario 3: A subscription-style replenishment product

A replenishment box costs $16.00 landed. Packaging is $1.00, fulfilment is $2.25, shipping is $4.25, expected refunds and replacements are $1.25, and retention marketing allocated to each renewal is $2.00. The non-payment total is `$16 + $1 + $2.25 + $4.25 + $1.25 + $2 = $26.75`.

At $59.00, the payment fee is `($59 × 0.029) + $0.30 = $2.01` after rounding. Total variable cost is `$28.76`; contribution is `$30.24`, and contribution margin is `$30.24 ÷ $59 = 51.3%`. If fixed costs and target owner pay together require $8,000 of monthly contribution, required renewals are `8,000 ÷ 30.24 = 264.55`, or **265 renewals**.

If 10% of billed subscriptions fail or cancel before renewal, 265 successful renewals require roughly `265 ÷ 0.90 = 294.45`, so plan for **295 billed subscribers or renewal attempts**. Retention affects how much new acquisition you need.

## Common mistakes

**Using supplier cost as the whole variable cost.** The missing lines are usually fulfilment, shipping, fees, returns, and advertising. Add them per order before you decide that a product has a healthy margin.

**Applying a markup to a price that includes percentage fees.** A 50% markup on cost does not mean a 50% contribution margin. Percentage fees rise with the price, so solve for the price after including them.

**Treating free shipping as free.** The customer may see $0 at checkout, but the carrier still invoices you. Put the average shipping subsidy in the order cost.

**Ignoring the discount version of the offer.** A product can work at $60 and fail at $45 during a promotion. Recalculate contribution at the actual sale price, then check whether the extra volume would really cover the lost dollars per order.

**Blending first orders and repeat orders.** Acquisition cost is often concentrated on the first purchase. If repeat buyers cost less to acquire, calculate first-order contribution separately and avoid using a blended average that hides an unprofitable entry offer.

**Pricing every product with one percentage markup.** Bulky, fragile, low-priced, and high-return products consume different resources. Price by order economics instead.

## FAQs

### Should I price from cost or from competitors?
Start from cost so you know the minimum economics of the order. Then use competitor prices to test positioning, features, shipping terms, and customer expectations; a competitor's price is context, not proof that your costs are covered.

### What contribution margin should an ecommerce store target?
There is no universal target because fulfilment, advertising, repeat purchase rate, and fixed costs differ. Choose a margin that covers your monthly contribution requirement at a plausible order volume, then test the result against demand and capacity.

### Do I include my own salary in product pricing?
Yes, if the store is expected to pay you. Add the monthly owner salary to fixed costs when calculating required contribution; otherwise the model can show a profit while your labour remains unpaid.

### How should I price when customers pay shipping?
Separate the shipping charge from the product price in your model. Include the shipping revenue and the carrier cost, plus any difference you absorb, so the order's contribution reflects what actually reaches your bank account.

### Should discounts come out of margin or marketing budget?
Either treatment can be useful, but the arithmetic must be visible. Model the discounted selling price as the price the customer pays, then include the campaign's advertising and fulfilment costs; do not label the discount as marketing while leaving the lower revenue out of the calculation.

### How often should I recalculate prices?
Recalculate when supplier costs, carrier rates, fees, ad costs, or return rates change. A monthly review is sensible for a growing store, while a promotion or supplier change deserves a fresh calculation before it goes live.

## Closing

- Price from **landed cost plus the full cost of serving an order**, not from product cost alone.
- Solve for contribution after payment and marketplace fees; markup and margin are different measures.
- Check the price against the orders, renewals, traffic, and fulfilment capacity it requires.
- Run normal, discounted, and repeat-purchase cases because one average order can hide a loss.
