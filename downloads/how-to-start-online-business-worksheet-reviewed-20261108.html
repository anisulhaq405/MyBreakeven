Set separate launch tests for merchandise and service promises

For US merchandise covered by the FTC’s order rule guidance, a seller needs a reasonable basis for its stated shipping time; when no shipping time is stated, the usual rule is shipment within thirty days after a properly completed order. If timely shipment becomes impossible, follow the applicable delay-consent or cancellation/refund procedure. This is not a universal thirty-day delivery rule for every online transaction, and the FTC guidance identifies exemptions and special cases. Services are outside this merchandise rule.

For the original service pilot, make the delivery promise testable: what file or work product is due, how the buyer confirms receipt, which revisions are included and who handles a failed handoff. A payment receipt establishes a payment event; it does not by itself prove completed service performance. Keep the five fictional orders labeled as teaching data when presenting the 25% inquiry conversion. Replace them with genuine dated business records before describing a real customer result. The $1,050 includes a one-off $250 test budget, so it is a defined pilot surplus rather than standard sales-minus-variable-cost contribution margin.

The launch decision should have separate evidence for offer accuracy, payment, fulfillment and customer communications. The FTC review-rule Q&A explains why businesses must avoid fake or false testimonials and incentives conditioned on a particular review sentiment. Ask genuine buyers for their actual experience; a test order, generated persona or fictional worked case must not become a customer endorsement. Record unresolved launch defects with an owner and retest the repaired step before increasing demand.
