Quick answer

A price increase calculator compares contribution before and after raising prices, then finds the minimum sales needed to preserve your current monthly contribution. How to use the price increase calculator for customer loss

Choose one consistent product, service or customer group that will receive the increase. Use completed sales for the same monthly period in both scenarios. If customers buy different amounts, estimate lost sales rather than the percentage of customer names.

  1. Enter your current selling price, direct variable cost per sale and percentage selling fee.
  2. Enter revised variable cost only if delivery costs will change. Leave it blank to keep current cost; entering zero deliberately sets the revised cost to zero.
  3. Choose Percentage increase or New selling price. Both modes describe the same price change; the latter calculates the percentage for you.
  4. Enter current monthly completed sales and expected sales loss. Model only affected sales, not customers who remain on the old price.
  5. Enter any additional fixed monthly overhead caused by the change, and optional available capacity.
  6. Compare both thresholds, the expected profit change and the sales-loss scenarios. Download the CSV to retain your assumptions.

Price increase and allowable customer-loss formula

Current contribution per sale = current price × (1 − fee rate) − current variable cost. New price = current price × (1 + increase rate). New contribution per sale = new price × (1 − fee rate) − revised variable cost.

Minimum required whole sales = round upward (current contribution per sale × current monthly sales ÷ new contribution per sale). Allowable whole-sale loss = current monthly sales − minimum required whole sales, when that difference is positive.

The tool calculates expected sales by rounding downward (current sales × (1 − expected loss rate)). Required sales are rounded upward because a partial completed sale cannot fund the baseline. If higher costs make required sales exceed the starting volume, the result shows additional sales needed instead of an allowable loss.

Percentage increase = (new price − current price) ÷ current price × 100. Amount of increase = new price − current price. A rise from $80 to $100 is 25%, not 20%; the denominator is the original price.

With additional monthly overhead H, profit-preserving sales = ceiling[(current monthly contribution + H) ÷ new contribution per sale]. Profit change = expected new contribution − current contribution − H. If new contribution is zero or negative, no positive sales volume can preserve this positive baseline.

Revenue-preserving sales = ceiling(current price × current sales ÷ new price). With no whole-sale rounding, revenue-neutral loss = 1 − current price ÷ new price. Contribution-neutral loss = 1 − old contribution per sale ÷ new contribution per sale when overhead is unchanged. These are different limits.

How many customers can you lose after a 10% price increase?

All figures below are illustrative planning assumptions. The starting service sale contributes $100 × 0.97 − $60 = $37. At 100 sales, monthly contribution is $3,700. Raising price to $110 leaves $110 × 0.97 − $60 = $46.70 per sale.

The exact target is $3,700 ÷ $46.70 = 79.2291… sales, so you need 80 completed sales. Losing 20 of the original 100 sales leaves $3,736 contribution, still $36 above the baseline. Losing 21 leaves 79 × $46.70 = $3,689.30, below the baseline.

Understand revenue loss versus contribution after raising prices

In the default service example, 90 sales at $110 produce $9,900 revenue, down from $10,000. Contribution rises from $3,700 to $4,203 because each sale leaves more money after direct cost and fees. This is why revenue retention and contribution retention answer different questions.

The allowable loss applies to completed sales with comparable economics. It is a customer-loss threshold only when each customer represents the same number and type of sales. A high-value customer leaving can change the answer more than several small customers leaving.

The capacity check compares required and expected sales with the entered limit. It does not cap projections or prove demand. Enter new fixed commitments as additional overhead; use the full break-even calculator for absolute monthly profit targets.

The default scenario needs 91 whole sales to preserve $10,000 revenue ($10,010 at $110 each), but only 80 to preserve $3,700 contribution. Exact continuous loss thresholds are about 9.09% and 20.77%; whole-sale loss limits are 9% and 20%. Neither number predicts actual customer response.

If the same price change also adds $600 monthly overhead, the unchanged-profit target rises to ceiling($4,300 ÷ $46.70) = 93 sales. At the expected 90 sales, contribution improves $503 but profit falls $97. This explains why contribution growth alone can be misleading.

Common mistakes when testing a business price increase

  1. Using lost customer count when customers have different order values. Model affected sales or comparable groups.
  2. Leaving revised cost blank even though fulfillment costs are rising.
  3. Treating higher revenue as proof of higher profit without checking contribution.
  4. Applying a price rise to new customers but modeling all existing sales at the new rate.
  5. Ignoring flat transaction charges or counting percentage fees twice.
  6. Treating allowable loss as predicted churn. The calculator gives a threshold based on your inputs.

Assumptions and limits

Model only the product, service or customer group receiving the increase, with the same sales mix and percentage selling fee. Costs entered are variable per completed sale; added monthly overhead is a separate fixed commitment. Existing fixed overhead is unchanged and cancels out of the profit comparison. Profit change is contribution change minus additional overhead; it is not absolute net profit. Taxes, working capital, staged rollout, customer lifetime value and recurring churn over several months are not modeled. Customer loss equals sales-volume loss only for customers with comparable purchasing patterns. Required sales round up; expected sales round down. Currency selection changes labels, not exchange rates. Capacity warnings do not cap projections or prove demand.

Frequently asked questions

How much customer loss can a 10% price increase absorb?

It depends on contribution before and after the increase, including fees and direct costs. In the worked service example, you can lose 20 of 100 completed sales. Use your own inputs; 20% is not a universal threshold.

Why can revenue fall while contribution rises?

Fewer higher-priced sales can leave more contribution per sale after delivery costs and fees. In the default example, revenue falls $100 while contribution rises $503. Monthly overhead still needs to be deducted.

Can I use this when supplier or labor costs rise?

Yes. Enter the changed cost in revised variable cost per sale. If revised contribution is lower than before, the tool may show that you need more sales despite the higher selling price.

Is customer loss the same as sales loss?

Only if customers represent comparable completed sales. For different order sizes or recurring frequencies, enter the percentage of sales lost or run separate customer-group scenarios.

Does the calculator recommend a safe price increase?

It shows the contribution threshold for the percentage you enter. It does not forecast churn, measure willingness to pay or recommend a guaranteed increase. Compare several scenarios with your actual retention evidence.

Does it include taxes, overhead and owner pay?

Enter any additional monthly overhead caused by the change. Existing overhead is assumed unchanged; owner pay, taxes and absolute net profit are not calculated. Enter selling prices excluding sales tax collected for others. Use the full business break-even calculator for a complete monthly target.

Can I raise prices only for new customers?

Yes, but model that affected group separately. The tool applies the new price to every proposed sale you enter; it does not automatically preserve legacy customer rates.

How do I calculate the percentage increase from an old and new price?

Subtract the old price from the new price, divide by the old price and multiply by 100. Moving from $80 to $100 is ($20 ÷ $80) × 100 = 25%. Choose New selling price to calculate this automatically.

How much customer loss preserves revenue after a 10% increase?

The continuous revenue threshold is 1 − 1 ÷ 1.10 = 9.0909…%. With 100 original completed sales, 91 whole sales are required, so at most 9 sales can be lost. The contribution or profit threshold depends on costs and overhead.

Does this calculate net profit?

It calculates the change in profit under unchanged existing fixed overhead, after deducting any additional overhead entered. It does not calculate absolute net profit, owner tax or cash flow. Contribution remains separately labeled.

Can I use this for subscriptions or an increase that applies to only some customers?

Use monthly paying accounts only when each account has comparable economics. Model the affected cohort separately. The tool is a one-period comparison; it does not compound churn over time, model downgrades or forecast customer lifetime value.
