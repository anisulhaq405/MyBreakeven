INDUSTRY-SPECIFIC UNIT ECONOMICS

What this restaurant break-even analysis includes

This model calculates contribution per order after food and ingredients, direct labor, packaging, delivery commissions, card fees and promotion. It then determines the exact volume and revenue required to cover monthly operating overhead, owner compensation and an optional target profit.

Because a financial target is useful only when the business can deliver it, MyBreakeven also compares required volume with productive team hours and estimates the customer inquiries needed at your conversion rate.

Formula, assumptions and rounding

Contribution per order = average price minus direct materials, labor, other variable costs, acquisition cost and percentage payment fees. Required monthly orders = (operating overhead + owner pay + target profit) divided by contribution per order.

For a traditional operating break-even calculation, set owner pay and target profit to zero. Keep owner pay if you want the business to cover your compensation, and add profit only when calculating a profit target. The example values are illustrative USD inputs, not industry averages.

The example contribution is $10.41 per order. The exact target is 3218.06 orders; rounding up gives 3,219 whole orders and $109,446.00 in monthly sales. Costs entered per order must not also be included in monthly overhead.

Capacity uses team members × weekly hours × 52 / 12 × productive utilization ÷ delivery hours per order. Delivery hours mean total team labor hours per order, including preparation and travel where relevant. Whole-unit capacity rounds down, while required sales round up.

Inquiry estimates use your assumed conversion rate, not a prediction of demand. For recurring work, count existing customers separately from new customer acquisition. The full calculator supports other currency labels; it does not convert exchange rates.

Restaurant break-even questions

How many restaurant orders cover monthly fixed costs?

At the example price of $34.00, each order contributes $10.41 after direct costs and fees. Covering $33,500.00 in overhead, owner pay and target profit requires 3219 whole orders, or $109,446.00 in practical monthly sales. Replace these sample inputs with your figures.

What sales revenue reaches break-even?

Can kitchen and service capacity handle required demand?

The example requires 3219 whole orders and estimates capacity for 2852 whole orders per month, using 10 team members, 32 hours each per week and 72% productive time. Adjust those inputs to check your own delivery limit.

Should all restaurant labor be variable?

No. Include labor that changes with the modeled sales unit in per-order cost. Keep guaranteed staffing in monthly fixed costs for the chosen period, and split mixed payroll consistently so the same wages are not counted twice.

What if required sales exceed capacity?

The target does not fit the productive hours you entered. Test price, costs, delivery time or a revised capacity plan. Recalculate any new monthly staffing or equipment commitment; more available hours do not prove customer demand.

Is this a cash-flow forecast or net-profit statement?

No. It models contribution, a monthly funding target and productive-hour capacity. Schedule collections, supplier payments, taxes, equipment cash and loan principal separately; reconcile any unentered items before interpreting accounting net profit.

Review the target before changing your plan

  1. Record the entered unit, period and cost boundary alongside the result.
  2. Compare required whole sales with both capacity and evidence of demand.
  3. Run a downside case using a lower realized price or higher delivery cost. Add new commitments if the operating range changes.
  4. Check a dated cash forecast before treating a profitable target as available cash.
