Contribution = retainer × (1 − percentage fee ÷ 100) − contractor − client software − other variable cost − flat fee. Fixed commitment = overhead + committed payroll. Conservative retained clients = floor(existing clients × (1 − churn ÷ 100)).

For each candidate total Q: new clients = max(0, Q − retained); acquisition = new clients × CAC. The smallest whole Q must satisfy Q × contribution − acquisition ≥ the selected funding requirement. Operating uses fixed commitments; owner coverage adds owner allocation; the full goal also adds extra profit.

Staff capacity = floor(staff hours × utilization ÷ 100 ÷ staff hours per client); owner capacity uses its own inputs. The smaller applicable capacity controls. How to calculate agency break-even without counting payroll twice

Define one unit as one active retainer client for a full month. Use the agency’s own service fee after discounts. Keep pure client ad budgets and reimbursed pass-through spending outside both sides of this planning model; a management fee belongs in agency revenue. One-off projects, different service tiers and onboarding months need separate scenarios unless their cost and hour mix is genuinely comparable.

1. Separate committed payroll from incremental delivery cost

Guaranteed staff salaries remain a monthly commitment when a client leaves. Enter loaded delivery payroll once in the payroll field and shared software, administration and premises in overhead. Contractor hours purchased only for the client, incremental licences and sale-dependent charges belong in per-client costs. Staff hours still constrain delivery even though the salary is fixed. Owner time is another capacity constraint, with compensation funded once by the owner allocation.

In the original retainer example, a $3,000 agency fee incurs $600 variable contractor work, $100 client software, $50 other variable cost and a 3% payment fee ($90). Contribution is $3,000 − $600 − $100 − $50 − $90 = $2,160 per client-month. Monthly commitments are $2,500 overhead plus $8,000 loaded payroll, totaling $10,500. Owner allocation is $4,000 and the extra profit goal is $2,000. These are illustrative USD inputs, not market rates or recommended salary levels.

2. Fund retained clients and new-client acquisition separately

Seven clients enter the example month. With a 10% churn assumption, the projection is 6.3, rounded down to six retained clients as a conservative whole-client planning assumption. This does not mean a fraction of an actual client cancels; use actual renewals when available. Each needed new client costs $400 to acquire in this month. Do not also deduct that $400 from every retained client's contribution or repeat the same spending in overhead.

When retained contribution cannot cover a requirement, an added client must contribute more than its one-time CAC to make a larger total solve the gap in this one-month view. Lifetime contribution from a new client can still be attractive, but this calculator does not project subsequent retention, acquisition timing, onboarding delays or customer lifetime value. It assumes new clients contribute a full month; use a separate prorated ramp-up forecast if they start mid-month.

3. Test staff and owner delivery capacity independently

The default staff pool has 320 monthly hours at 75% productive utilization: 240 delivery hours. At 20 staff worker-hours per client, that supports 12 whole clients. The owner has 80 hours at 75%, or 60 delivery hours; five owner hours per client also supports 12. The lower applicable limit controls. Do not count owner hours in staff capacity too, or remove the same nonbillable time twice.

The scope-creep preset raises staff hours to 32 per client, owner hours to eight and contractor cost to $900. Contribution falls to $1,860 and the full financial target becomes ten clients, but both resource limits fall to seven. A ten-client invoice target cannot fix that delivery gap. Additional hires, contractor substitution or less scope must change the corresponding costs and hours together. Aggregate capacity also does not prove that a specialist designer or account director is available when needed.

4. Compare a freelancer with a staffed agency

The owner-led example has a $1,800 retainer, $150 contractor cost, $40 software, $20 other cost and $54 percentage fee, leaving $1,536 contribution. Fixed overhead is $800 with zero staff payroll; owner allocation is $4,000 and extra goal $1,000. Three retained clients plus one new $300-CAC client require four total clients. They leave $1,044 after owner allocation, beating the extra goal by $44. The owner has 84 productive hours and needs 72 for four clients at 18 hours each. Five clients would require 90 hours and exceed that capacity.

5. Use the minimum retainer as a cost boundary

At ten planned clients in the default case, four are new and acquisition expense is $1,600. Required net contribution per client is ($10,500 + $4,000 + $2,000 + $1,600) ÷ 10 = $1,810. Add $750 other variable costs and divide by 0.97 to allow for the 3% revenue fee: $2,639.18 minimum retainer, rounded upward to a cent. This price supports the stated goal only at the entered volume, scope, hours and acquisition pattern. It does not establish buyer demand or a value-based market price.

Formula, assumptions and rounding — quick estimate

Contribution per client = average price minus direct materials, labor, other variable costs, acquisition cost and percentage payment fees. Required monthly clients = (operating overhead + owner pay + target profit) divided by contribution per client.

For a traditional operating break-even calculation, set owner pay and target profit to zero. Keep owner pay if you want the business to cover your compensation, and add profit only when calculating a profit target. The example values are illustrative USD inputs, not industry averages.

The example contribution is $916.20 per client. The exact target is 16.59 clients; rounding up gives 17 whole clients and $37,400.00 in monthly sales. Costs entered per client must not also be included in monthly overhead.

Capacity uses team members × weekly hours × 52 / 12 × productive utilization ÷ delivery hours per client. Delivery hours mean total team labor hours per client, including preparation and travel where relevant. Whole-unit capacity rounds down, while required sales round up.

Inquiry estimates use your assumed conversion rate, not a prediction of demand. For recurring work, count existing customers separately from new customer acquisition. The full calculator supports other currency labels; it does not convert exchange rates.

QUESTIONS THIS MODEL ANSWERS

Agency break-even questions

What does one client mean in the detailed calculator?

One active retainer client for one month, with a consistent service scope. Exclude client ad budgets and other pure pass-through amounts from both revenue and delivery costs. One-off projects need a separate scenario.

Where should salaried agency staff go?

Enter committed loaded monthly delivery payroll in Payroll, once. It remains payable when client numbers fall. Only genuinely client-dependent contractors and software belong in variable cost. Owner compensation is separate.

How are retained clients and acquisition costs calculated?

The model conservatively rounds down existing clients × (1 − monthly churn rate). New clients are the positive gap between a modeled total and that retained count. CAC applies to those new clients once in this modeled month, not to every active retainer.

Why are there three client targets?

Operating break-even covers fixed overhead, committed payroll and modeled new-client acquisition. Owner coverage adds the owner allocation. The full target also funds the extra profit goal. Each uses whole clients and its own required new-client count.

What if new-client acquisition costs more than monthly contribution?

Retained-client contribution may still cover the target. If it does not, and contribution per added client is no greater than CAC, no finite total can cover the selected target in this one-month acquisition model. Longer-term lifetime value and cash timing need a separate cohort forecast.

Does a capacity result prove the agency can serve that many clients?

No. Staff and owner productive hours are separate aggregate limits. The lower whole-client capacity controls, but specialist roles, review bottlenecks, skills and demand still require a delivery schedule.

What happens when conversion is zero?

No finite inquiry estimate is shown when new clients are needed and conversion is zero. If no new clients are needed, required new inquiries are zero. A conversion assumption never forecasts demand.

Can I export or print my agency scenario?

Yes. Download a CSV of the entered assumptions and results, or print/save the page as a PDF. The detailed mode stays on this page; switching to the quick estimate does not transfer detailed inputs.

Review the target before changing your plan

  1. Record the entered unit, period and cost boundary alongside the result.
  2. Compare required whole sales with both capacity and evidence of demand.
  3. Run a downside case using a lower realized price or higher delivery cost. Add new commitments if the operating range changes.
  4. Check a dated cash forecast before treating a profitable target as available cash.
