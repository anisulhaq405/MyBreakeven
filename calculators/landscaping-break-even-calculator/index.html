Landscaping break-even: job costs, routes and seasonal recovery

Define one completed, comparable job. A maintenance visit, a mulch installation and a complete design/build project do not share the same material, labor or equipment consumption. Use separate scenarios unless you have a measured stable mix. Enter realized service revenue after discounts, excluding sales tax collected for others. Canceled appointments and unpaid quotations are not completed revenue-producing jobs.

Separate people, machines and route costs

Employee hours = employee count × (elapsed on-site hours + paid travel/setup hours per employee)
Loaded variable wage = wage × (1 + employer-burden fraction)
Employee labor = employee hours × loaded variable wage
Equipment cost = total machine-hours × use-related cost / machine-hour
Job contribution = price × (1 − fee fraction) − employee labor − materials − route vehicle/disposal − equipment − acquisition − other direct costs − flat fee

Only visit-dependent employee wages belong in variable labor. Put committed payroll in fixed operating costs once; capacity still uses those employees’ delivery hours. A blended wage and employer-burden percentage are inputs from your own records, not a statutory payroll calculation. Owner time is a separate capacity constraint and owner compensation is funded once through the monthly owner allocation. Do not add that same owner wage again in Other.

Equipment hours are total running machine-hours. Two mowers each running one hour use two machine-hours even if four employees attend the job. Choose a consistent allowance for fuel, repairs and wear, and keep any cost already included there out of other lines. Fixed depreciation, rental or insurance may belong in fixed costs when not already allocated; a replacement allowance and depreciation for the same economic cost should not both be charged. Debt principal and buying a mower are cash movements that need their own schedule.

Vehicle/disposal expense differs from paid travel time. The Department of Labor’s hours-worked guidance distinguishes workday travel between job sites from ordinary commuting. Use the attributable paid time appropriate to your employment arrangement; this tool does not calculate legal wages or overtime. No national wage, equipment rate, margin or acreage productivity benchmark is assumed.

Calendar-month targets versus a shorter earning season

The standard fixed-cost/contribution relationship gives operating jobs = fixed cost / contribution. Owner-covered jobs add the owner allocation; the full target also adds the extra monthly remainder goal. These first three results cover one calendar month only.

Annual fixed requirement = monthly fixed cost × 12
Annual full requirement = (monthly fixed cost + monthly owner allocation + monthly extra goal) × 12
Seasonal full jobs / active month = annual full requirement ÷ (active months × job contribution)
Annual planned contribution = planned jobs / active month × active months × contribution

The seasonal comparison deliberately assumes zero off-season contribution and all twelve months of the entered costs and goals. It is a recovery budget, not a prediction of rainfall or demand. If you have snow work or other winter services, reconcile their contribution and hours in a separate combined annual plan. Do not use their gross sales as a contribution credit. If all twelve months earn the same job mix, seasonal monthly targets equal the calendar-month targets.

Financial targets round upward to whole jobs; employee and owner capacity each round downward. The smaller applicable role limit controls. A role with zero job time does not constrain capacity. Available delivery hours must include the travel/setup time used in the denominator, after other downtime: subtracting the same travel twice understates capacity. Hours alone do not prove that routes, staff availability, equipment and appointment windows align.

Use price floors and recurring-client counts carefully

At positive planned volume, the calendar operating price is (non-percentage variable cost + fixed cost / planned jobs) / (1 − fee fraction). Add owner allocation and extra goal to solve the calendar full price; use the annual full requirement divided by active months and planned jobs for the seasonal price. The worked seasonal full price is $310.14 rounded upward to a cent at 90 jobs per active month. That price does not resolve its staffing/owner-capacity gap or establish what customers will pay.

Margin of safety uses the exact calendar operating threshold and is unavailable at zero planned volume or nonpositive contribution. Recurring-client counts on the main results relate to the calendar full target and your entered jobs per client; they are not the seasonal goal. Validate actual service dates. The all-new inquiry estimate assumes every target job needs acquisition; existing repeat work should instead reduce your new-job gap.

The minimum annual full-goal job count and a constant rounded monthly pace can differ. In the worked case, 778 whole annual jobs are the financial minimum; 98 jobs in each of eight months gives 784. Plan uneven demand with a dated budget rather than calling those two numbers identical. How to do break-even analysis for a landscaping business

  1. Find contribution per completed job after materials, paid crew labor, fuel, equipment use, acquisition and percentage fees.
  2. Divide monthly overhead by positive job contribution for operating break-even and round jobs upward.
  3. Add owner pay and a profit goal once for the higher monthly funding target.
  4. Compare required whole jobs with productive crew worker-hours divided by complete worker-hours per job.
  5. Check job mix, seasonal demand and actual collection/payment dates before committing to expansion.

Use a consistent mix of mowing visits and installation projects

Do not average a weekly mowing visit with an installation project unless the planned mix is explicit. For a fictional ten-job mix of eight visits leaving $50 each and two projects leaving $300 each, weighted contribution is $100 per job. If visits require two worker-hours and projects 12, weighted time is four worker-hours. Both averages must use that same 8:2 mix. When the mix changes, revise contribution and time together, or plan separate service schedules.

Plan seasonal months separately

The 52 ÷ 12 conversion is an average month, not a promise of working days. Reduce available hours or productive share for weather, equipment downtime and seasonal work patterns. Count paid idle payroll once in the relevant cost schedule. Model each month separately when winter revenue or staffing differs; do not multiply a strong summer month by twelve and call it an annual forecast. Equipment replacement, debt principal and delayed customer payments also need a separate cash forecast.

For route travel and repeated mowing stops, use the lawn route profit calculator. For a property quote, use the lawn care pricing per acre guide. Keep the monthly model focused on how many complete jobs the business must fund and can deliver.

Formula, assumptions and rounding — quick estimate

Contribution per job = average price minus direct materials, labor, other variable costs, acquisition cost and percentage payment fees. Required monthly jobs = (operating overhead + owner pay + target profit) divided by contribution per job.

For a traditional operating break-even calculation, set owner pay and target profit to zero. Keep owner pay if you want the business to cover your compensation, and add profit only when calculating a profit target. The example values are illustrative USD inputs, not industry averages.

The example contribution is $110.72 per job. The exact target is 102.06 jobs; rounding up gives 103 whole jobs and $32,960.00 in monthly sales. Costs entered per job must not also be included in monthly overhead.

Capacity uses team members × weekly hours × 52 / 12 × productive utilization ÷ delivery hours per job. Delivery hours mean total team labor hours per job, including preparation and travel where relevant. Whole-unit capacity rounds down, while required sales round up.

Inquiry estimates use your assumed conversion rate, not a prediction of demand. For recurring work, count existing customers separately from new customer acquisition. The full calculator supports other currency labels; it does not convert exchange rates.

What does the seasonal landscaping target recover?

It assumes all entered monthly fixed costs, owner allocation and extra goal continue for twelve calendar months, but job contribution is earned only during your entered active months. Off-season contribution is zero in this comparison. The calendar-month targets remain separate.

Are equipment machine-hours the same as crew-hours?

No. Two employees working one hour use two worker-hours. One mower running for one hour uses one machine-hour. Enter the combined running time of all included equipment and a representative use-related cost per machine-hour. Do not multiply it by crew count again.

How do I avoid counting equipment costs twice?

Use the hourly equipment allowance for the usage-related costs you choose to include, and fixed overhead for separate fixed commitments. If depreciation, a replacement reserve or rental is already included in that hourly allowance, do not add the same amount again to fixed costs. Loan principal and capital purchases need a separate cash forecast.

Does owner labor get counted twice?

Employee labor is calculated only for employees. Owner on-site and travel time limit owner capacity, while owner compensation is funded once through monthly owner allocation. Operating break-even excludes that allocation, so inspect the owner-covered and full-goal targets too.

What if I earn income from snow work or winter cleanups?

The seasonal comparison assumes zero off-season contribution. If winter services contribute, build a dated combined plan with their own prices, costs and hours rather than subtracting their gross revenue from landscaping overhead. Twelve active months models the same representative job mix all year.

Can I combine mowing visits and installation projects?

Only with a stable, representative job mix and compatible price, material cost and hours. Otherwise run separate scenarios. Use the lawn route tool for route-specific economics and keep maintenance visit frequency separate from a complete installation project.

Does available time guarantee enough jobs can be delivered?

No. This is a pooled employee/owner hours screen. It does not optimize appointment windows, simultaneous crew availability, equipment sharing, routes or weather. Compare both planned volume and seasonal target with capacity, then check the actual schedule.

Can I download the detailed landscaping plan?

Yes. The free local CSV includes input assumptions and unrounded calendar and seasonal results. Detailed inputs remain separate from the original quick estimate, which retains full-calculator transfer and its existing saved-plan options.

Does acreage belong in the job count?

Only if one modeled unit consistently means one acre with matching price, costs and crew-hours. For complete visits, use visits as the unit and test property sizes separately.

What if required sales exceed capacity?

The target does not fit the productive hours you entered. Test price, costs, delivery time or a revised capacity plan. Recalculate any new monthly staffing or equipment commitment; more available hours do not prove customer demand.

Is this a cash-flow forecast or net-profit statement?

No. It models contribution, a monthly funding target and productive-hour capacity. Schedule collections, supplier payments, taxes, equipment cash and loan principal separately; reconcile any unentered items before interpreting accounting net profit.

Review the target before changing your plan

  1. Record the entered unit, period and cost boundary alongside the result.
  2. Compare required whole sales with both capacity and evidence of demand.
  3. Run a downside case using a lower realized price or higher delivery cost. Add new commitments if the operating range changes.
  4. Check a dated cash forecast before treating a profitable target as available cash.
