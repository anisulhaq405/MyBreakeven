How to test a menu price after fees

  1. Use ingredient cost per serving or whole item from the recipe calculator.
  2. Add sale-dependent labor, packaging and other non-percentage costs once.
  3. Enter fees, your chosen ingredient percentage and contribution percentage separately.
  4. Enter the selling price you actually want to test. The alternative price outputs do not automatically replace it.
  5. Define the monthly funding requirement and the comparable expected sales and capacity. Review fixed scope and owner compensation carefully.
  6. Compare the required whole units, capacity and contribution at expected volume before treating a price as feasible.

Food-cost, contribution and capacity price formulas

Ingredient-target price = ingredient cost ÷ target ingredient rate. It excludes the other variable costs when setting price.

Contribution-target price = all non-percentage variable cost ÷ (1 − fee rate − target contribution rate). The denominator must be positive.

At the tested price, contribution = price × (1 − fee rate) − non-percentage variable cost. Required units = ceiling(monthly funding requirement ÷ positive contribution).

Whole monthly capacity = floor(weekly capacity × 52/12). Capacity funding price = (variable cost + monthly requirement ÷ whole monthly capacity) ÷ (1 − fee rate), rounded upward. Zero capacity has no capacity-based price.

Check price, scope and deliverable demand together

  1. Keep a serving, a whole cake and a customer check as separate possible units; match every cost and capacity entry.
  2. For multiple items, use a stable representative mix or run separate scenarios. Do not assign the full overhead to every item.
  3. If expanding capacity adds payroll or equipment commitments, revise the monthly requirement before comparing the new result.
  4. After a price change, review paid volume, portion practice, contribution and kitchen time using actual records.

Assumptions and limits

One consistently defined selling unit and a stable cost/channel mix. Percentage fees stay constant. Required monthly units round up; capacity uses weekly units × 52/12 and rounds down. Price floors round up to cents. The funding requirement includes only entered fixed commitments, additional owner compensation and profit goal. Existing labor compensation must not be duplicated. No seating, kitchen bottleneck, demand prediction or accounting net-profit calculation is provided.

Frequently asked questions

Can I carry these numbers into a full restaurant plan?

Yes. Review the result-panel handoff, confirm one selling unit represents a complete order with matching monthly scope, and enter promotion cost, team hours, utilization and conversion. The tested price and itemized costs carry in the same tab with your currency. Weekly item capacity is replaced by a workforce-based capacity calculation; expected sales remain an assumption.

Is a 30% food-cost target a 70% profit margin?

No. It leaves 70% of price before labor, packaging, selling fees and fixed costs. The contribution calculation deducts all entered variable costs and fees separately.

Is contribution margin the same as markup?

No. Contribution percentage divides contribution by selling price; markup divides a price uplift by cost. This target uses margin on revenue.

Why is the capacity price different from the margin price?

One funds a stated monthly requirement at the available whole-unit capacity; the other meets a chosen per-unit contribution ratio. Both depend on inputs, not customer demand predictions.

How should I enter owner labor?

If the same owner work is already costed as variable labor, do not also enter it as additional monthly owner pay. A separate profit goal is beyond that compensation.

Can I price delivery orders separately?

Yes. Run a separate consistent scenario with that channel’s fees, packaging and other costs. Do not apply a delivery commission to unrelated dine-in sales.

Does a feasible capacity target guarantee sales?

No. The tool checks your entered volume and capacity. Demand, willingness to pay and operational bottlenecks still need evidence.
