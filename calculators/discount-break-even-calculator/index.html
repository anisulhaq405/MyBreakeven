How to use the discount break-even calculator

Start with one product, service or comparable order type. Use a representative completed month rather than mixing several periods. This makes the extra sales target comparable with your actual workload.

  1. Enter the current net selling price and variable delivery cost for one consistent product, service or customer cohort. Exclude sales tax collected for others.
  2. Enter revised promotion cost if extra packaging, shipping or variable labor changes the per-sale cost. Blank keeps the old cost; zero sets revised cost to zero.
  3. Add the percentage selling fee separately. Put flat transaction charges in variable cost and avoid counting any cost twice.
  4. Choose percentage off, amount off per sale or the proposed sale price. These are alternative ways to enter one discount, not stacked discounts.
  5. Enter completed monthly sales before the offer and your expected sales increase. All modeled sales receive the promotion; unaffected customers belong in a separate group.
  6. Add fixed campaign spending for this same month, then enter deliverable monthly capacity if known. Compare required sales, expected profit change and the discount limits.
  7. Compare several discounts, download the scenario CSV or print the result. A mathematical threshold does not predict demand.

Discount break-even formula after costs and payment fees

Let P be the current price, f the percentage fee as a decimal, V0 the original variable cost, V1 the promotion variable cost, Q0 the baseline monthly sales and K the additional fixed campaign expense for that month. Current contribution per sale C0 = P × (1 − f) − V0.

Discounted price P1 = P × (1 − d), or P − amount off. From a proposed sale price, d = 1 − P1 ÷ P. Discounted contribution per sale C1 = P1 × (1 − f) − V1.

Whole sales needed for the same profit = ceiling[(Q0 × C0 + K) ÷ C1], when C1 is positive. Required extra sales = target − Q0. Whole-sale lift = (target ÷ Q0 − 1) × 100. Negative extra sales can occur when revised costs fall enough.

Expected sales Q1 = floor[Q0 × (1 + assumed lift)]. Profit change = Q1 × C1 − Q0 × C0 − K. Existing fixed overhead is assumed unchanged and cancels from this comparison; this does not calculate absolute net profit.

Revenue-only recovery = ceiling(P × Q0 ÷ P1), when the sale price is positive. It ignores variable costs and campaign spending, so it can materially understate the sales needed to preserve profit.

At a chosen completed volume Q, the minimum price preserving baseline profit is [(Q0 × C0 + K) ÷ Q + V1] ÷ (1 − f). Maximum discount = 1 − minimum price ÷ P. If the minimum price exceeds P, no discount fits that volume. Apply this separately to expected volume and capacity.

With unchanged cost, no percentage fee and no campaign expense, the simplified continuous formula is extra sales ratio = d ÷ (m − d), where m is the original contribution margin ratio. A 40% margin and 20% discount need 100% more sales, not 20%. If d is at least m, no positive contribution remains.

How many extra sales does a 10% discount need?

These are illustrative planning examples, not customer results or market averages. The starting service example contributes $100 × 0.97 − $60 = $37 per sale. At 100 sales, the baseline is $3,700. A 10% discount makes the selling price $90 and contribution $27.30.

The exact recovery ratio is $3,700 ÷ $27.30 = 135.5311… sales. Round upward to 136 completed sales: 136 × $27.30 = $3,712.80. The whole-sale recovery target is therefore a 36% sales increase. At your assumed 20% lift, 120 sales contribute $3,276, which is $424 below the baseline.

Read the sales increase, margin impact and capacity results

The default 10% discount needs 112 whole sales just to preserve $10,000 revenue, but 136 to preserve $3,700 contribution with unchanged overhead. At the entered 120 sales, revenue rises to $10,800 while contribution falls to $3,276, a $424 reduction.

If the same offer adds $300 of fixed campaign spending, the unchanged-profit target becomes ceiling[($3,700 + $300) ÷ $27.30] = 147 sales. Expected profit falls $724 at 120 sales. Campaign spending is deducted once, not once per order.

At 120 expected sales and no campaign expense, the exact price floor is approximately $93.64261168, allowing about 6.35738832% off. A final price of $93.65 or a discount no larger than the displayed conservative 6.35% limit preserves the baseline under these assumptions. The proposed 10% discount does not.

At 150-sale capacity, the same default cost structure supports an exact discount of about 12.71477663%; the displayed conservative limit is 12.71%. This is not a recommended discount, and capacity is not evidence of demand.

Zero or negative discounted contribution means more units cannot recover a positive baseline plus campaign expense. The zero-contribution boundary ignores existing overhead and campaign recovery. Promotion-specific cost can move this boundary below the full price.

This model applies the promotion to every modeled completed sale. It does not estimate cannibalization, repeat purchase value, inventory liquidation value, refunds, tax, staggered redemptions or a changing product mix. Evaluate those separately instead of treating future benefits as guaranteed.

Common mistakes when calculating discount profitability

  1. Recovering revenue instead of contribution. Revenue ignores the cost of fulfilling each extra sale.
  2. Entering profit margin as variable cost. Use actual per-sale delivery costs.
  3. Counting the payment fee inside cost and again in the fee field.
  4. Assuming additional sales need no staff time or capacity.
  5. Applying the discount to a few customers while entering the volume of the whole business. Model the affected group separately.
  6. Calling the recovery target a forecast. Validate demand with your own promotion records.

Assumptions and limits

This is a one-month comparison of the same product, service or affected customer cohort. Every modeled sale receives the discount; price and per-sale cost are constant within each scenario. Existing fixed overhead is unchanged. Additional campaign spending is incremental fixed expense for the same month, counted once. Profit change is contribution change less campaign expense, not absolute net profit or cash flow. Sales mix and the percentage fee remain unchanged. New per-sale costs can be entered separately. Whole expected sales round down; recovery targets round up. Capacity warnings do not cap projections or prove demand. Currency changes labels only. Discount limits round down to two percentage decimals and quoted price floors round up to two amount decimals; actual currency or platform pricing increments may differ. No automatic churn, demand, tax, lifetime value or redemption prediction is made.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 10% discount recovered by 10% more sales?

Usually not. In the default example, 100 sales become a 136-sale recovery target after variable costs and a 3% fee, or 36% more completed sales. The discount percentage and the required sales lift use different bases.

How do I enter a fixed amount off or a sale price?

Select Amount off per sale or Sale price. A $10 reduction from $100 and a $90 sale price both describe a 10% discount. These modes are alternatives, not cumulative discounts.

What is the discount break-even formula?

Required whole sales = ceiling[(baseline monthly contribution + additional campaign expense) ÷ discounted contribution per sale]. Discounted contribution is sale price less percentage selling fees and promotion variable cost. It must be positive to recover the baseline.

Does this calculate net profit after a discount?

It calculates profit change when original fixed overhead stays unchanged, after deducting incremental campaign expense. Absolute net profit, owner tax and cash flow require a full business model. Contribution remains separately labeled.

Why does revenue rise while profit falls?

Extra sales still incur fulfillment costs. In the default example, 120 sales at $90 increase revenue to $10,800, but leave only $3,276 contribution versus $3,700 before the promotion. That is a $424 reduction before any campaign expense.

How should I enter advertising and promotion costs?

Enter a fixed campaign budget or setup expense for the modeled month as Additional campaign cost. Put a genuine variable cost per completed sale in promotion variable cost instead. Do not enter the same cost in both fields.

What if the offer changes shipping, packaging or labor cost?

Enter the promotion variable cost per sale. Blank keeps the current cost; zero deliberately removes that cost. Percentage selling fees stay separate and apply to the sale price.

What happens when required sales exceed capacity?

The recovery target is not deliverable within the entered limit. The calculator also reports profit change at full entered capacity and the discount supported by that volume. It does not reduce your expected-sales projection automatically.

How is the maximum discount at expected sales calculated?

It solves for the price that funds original monthly contribution plus campaign spending at your expected completed volume, after revised costs and fees. The displayed discount rounds down and the two-decimal price floor rounds up. This is a mathematical limit, not a recommendation or forecast.

What if discounted contribution is zero or negative?

No positive sales volume can recover the original positive baseline and campaign cost. The tool shows Not recoverable. A zero sale price also has no finite revenue-recovery target.

Can I use this for multiple products or selective coupons?

Use one consistent product or stable average order with comparable economics. Model only the cohort that receives the offer. Different product mixes, redemption rates and unaffected full-price buyers need separate scenarios.

Are my figures saved or converted between currencies?

Inputs and downloaded results are handled in your browser tab without an account. Currency selection changes labels, not exchange rates. The calculator does not save a promotion to your account.
