What this e-commerce break-even analysis includes

This model calculates contribution per order after COGS, fulfillment labor, shipping subsidy, return allowance, payment or platform fees and customer acquisition cost. It then determines the exact volume and revenue required to cover monthly operating overhead, owner compensation and an optional target profit.

Because a financial target is useful only when the business can deliver it, MyBreakeven also compares required volume with productive team hours and estimates the customer inquiries needed at your conversion rate.

Formula, assumptions and rounding

Contribution per order = average price minus direct materials, labor, other variable costs, acquisition cost and percentage payment fees. Required monthly orders = (operating overhead + owner pay + target profit) divided by contribution per order.

For a traditional operating break-even calculation, set owner pay and target profit to zero. Keep owner pay if you want the business to cover your compensation, and add profit only when calculating a profit target. The example values are illustrative USD inputs, not industry averages.

The example contribution is $25.25 per order. The exact target is 586.19 orders; rounding up gives 587 whole orders and $50,482.00 in monthly sales. Costs entered per order must not also be included in monthly overhead.

Capacity uses team members × weekly hours × 52 / 12 × productive utilization ÷ delivery hours per order. Delivery hours mean total team labor hours per order, including preparation and travel where relevant. Whole-unit capacity rounds down, while required sales round up.

Inquiry estimates use your assumed conversion rate, not a prediction of demand. For recurring work, count existing customers separately from new customer acquisition. The full calculator supports other currency labels; it does not convert exchange rates.

E-commerce break-even questions

How many e-commerce orders are needed to break even?

At the example price of $86.00, each order contributes $25.25 after direct costs and fees. Covering $14,800.00 in overhead, owner pay and target profit requires 587 whole orders, or $50,482.00 in practical monthly sales. Replace these sample inputs with your figures.

How do returns and shipping affect contribution margin?

In this example, each order contributes $25.25 after direct costs and fees. Changing the price or a per-order cost changes that contribution and therefore the number of orders needed. Enter your own assumptions to see the effect.

Can I use this for handmade products and small batches?

Yes. Preview and load the jewellery, ceramics, clothing or art-print example, then replace every assumption. Include making and packing worker-hours, allocate batch costs consistently, and keep owner compensation either in monthly owner pay or the relevant labor cost, once. The examples are illustrative USD inputs, not industry benchmarks.

How should I enter shipping, returns and platform fees?

Include seller-funded shipping, packing, flat fees and unrecovered return costs in the appropriate per-order costs. Exclude collected taxes from net price. Reconcile refunds and fee reversals so the same amount is not deducted twice; enter percentage fees separately from flat charges.

Do I need Pro to calculate, and where can I save my plan?

No. This page calculates free without an account. Continue with these numbers carries valid inputs to the full calculator in the same browser tab. A free account supports up to 3 saved scenarios; Pro supports up to 100 saved scenarios, three-plan comparisons and CSV/PDF reports. Opening the pricing page alone does not save inputs.

Does positive order contribution prove the store is profitable?

No. Order contribution must cover monthly overhead and the stated owner-pay/profit requirement. Returns, acquisition mix and unentered costs can change the full result; the tool does not verify your order records.

What if required sales exceed capacity?

The target does not fit the productive hours you entered. Test price, costs, delivery time or a revised capacity plan. Recalculate any new monthly staffing or equipment commitment; more available hours do not prove customer demand.

Is this a cash-flow forecast or net-profit statement?

No. It models contribution, a monthly funding target and productive-hour capacity. Schedule collections, supplier payments, taxes, equipment cash and loan principal separately; reconcile any unentered items before interpreting accounting net profit.

Review the target before changing your plan

  1. Record the entered unit, period and cost boundary alongside the result.
  2. Compare required whole sales with both capacity and evidence of demand.
  3. Run a downside case using a lower realized price or higher delivery cost. Add new commitments if the operating range changes.
  4. Check a dated cash forecast before treating a profitable target as available cash.
