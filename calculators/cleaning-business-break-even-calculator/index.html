What this cleaning break-even analysis includes

This model calculates contribution per job after cleaning supplies, direct labor, travel, equipment use, card fees and lead-generation cost. It then determines the exact volume and revenue required to cover monthly operating overhead, owner compensation and an optional target profit.

Because a financial target is useful only when the business can deliver it, MyBreakeven also compares required volume with productive team hours and estimates the customer inquiries needed at your conversion rate.

Formula, assumptions and rounding

Contribution per job = average price minus direct materials, labor, other variable costs, acquisition cost and percentage payment fees. Required monthly jobs = (operating overhead + owner pay + target profit) divided by contribution per job.

For a traditional operating break-even calculation, set owner pay and target profit to zero. Keep owner pay if you want the business to cover your compensation, and add profit only when calculating a profit target. The example values are illustrative USD inputs, not industry averages.

The example contribution is $76.78 per job. The exact target is 113.31 jobs; rounding up gives 114 whole jobs and $20,520.00 in monthly sales. Costs entered per job must not also be included in monthly overhead.

Capacity uses team members × weekly hours × 52 / 12 × productive utilization ÷ delivery hours per job. Delivery hours mean total team labor hours per job, including preparation and travel where relevant. Whole-unit capacity rounds down, while required sales round up.

Inquiry estimates use your assumed conversion rate, not a prediction of demand. For recurring work, count existing customers separately from new customer acquisition. The full calculator supports other currency labels; it does not convert exchange rates.

QUESTIONS THIS MODEL ANSWERS

Cleaning break-even questions

How many cleaning jobs do I need to break even?

At the example price of $180.00, each job contributes $76.78 after direct costs and fees. Covering $8,700.00 in overhead, owner pay and target profit requires 114 whole jobs, or $20,520.00 in practical monthly sales. Replace these sample inputs with your figures.

Can my cleaners deliver the required monthly jobs?

The example requires 114 whole jobs and estimates capacity for 97 whole jobs per month, using 3 team members, 35 hours each per week and 75% productive time. Adjust those inputs to check your own delivery limit.

How many cleaning leads does my conversion rate require?

At the example 30% inquiry-to-job conversion rate, the model estimates 377.70 inquiries for the exact fractional financial target (378 rounded up). Use your measured conversion rate for a more useful demand estimate.

Can I use a monthly cleaning contract as one job?

Yes, only if price, variable costs and total worker-hours all describe that entire monthly contract. Otherwise use a completed visit as the unit and keep the monthly contract quote separate.

What if required sales exceed capacity?

The target does not fit the productive hours you entered. Test price, costs, delivery time or a revised capacity plan. Recalculate any new monthly staffing or equipment commitment; more available hours do not prove customer demand.

Is this a cash-flow forecast or net-profit statement?

No. It models contribution, a monthly funding target and productive-hour capacity. Schedule collections, supplier payments, taxes, equipment cash and loan principal separately; reconcile any unentered items before interpreting accounting net profit.

Review the target before changing your plan

  1. Record the entered unit, period and cost boundary alongside the result.
  2. Compare required whole sales with both capacity and evidence of demand.
  3. Run a downside case using a lower realized price or higher delivery cost. Add new commitments if the operating range changes.
  4. Check a dated cash forecast before treating a profitable target as available cash.
